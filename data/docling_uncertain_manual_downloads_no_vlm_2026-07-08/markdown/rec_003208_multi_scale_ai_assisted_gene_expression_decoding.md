## ABSTRACT

Genes can be treated as a graph that can be mapped. Tremendous information is coded in genes to form a complex organism. Decoding this information is critical to understanding our biology and developing treatments for various diseases including cancer. Deep learning, a new branch of computer science, has gained traction over the past decade. Our study has shown that deep-learning models can be an effective tool in decoding genetic data such as gene tissue-deconvolution, gene graph mapping and genomic imputation. In tasks such as tissue deconvolution, our research has demonstrated the superior capability of deep learning-based approaches in capturing sample variations compared to traditional numerical analytical methods. While our approach requires large relevant datasets for effective deep learning training, this challenge can be addressed with increasing data availability. In gene graphing and mapping, our Graph Neural Network based approach consistently outperforms traditional regression techniques by a significant margin. The primary challenge here lies in the demand for substantial computing resources; however, the ongoing growth in average computing power and the enhanced accessibility of computational resources are expected to alleviate this constraint over time. Moreover, in the realm of generating and imputing missing biological data, cutting-edge generative AI models have proven to be invaluable. We are actively exploring the potential of generative AI to aid in imputing common missing biological data such as gene expression or methylation states. Overall, the evolution of advanced deep learning models has introduced fresh perspectives and possibilities to the field of biology and medicine, albeit accompanied by certain challenges. By addressing these challenges, deep learning models exhibit remarkable efficacy in resolving complex biomedical issues. In the foreseeable future, these advancements hold the promise of unveiling novel biological insights and facilitating the development of innovative treatments, thereby propelling biomedical research forward and ultimately benefiting humanity.

Keywords: Deep-learning, Gene-expression, Methylation, imputation, GCN, Diffusion

## SCOPE OF PROPOSED RESEARCH

The proposed research aims to investigate and advance the application of deep learning techniques in the fields of tissue deconvolution, gene mapping, and methylation imputation within the context of biomedical research. The tissue deconvolution refers to the process of identifying the composition of origin tissue types given the gene expression of a specific sample. The current widely accepted method is None-Negative Least Squares algorithm (NNLS). From which other similar regression analytical methods are also derived to predict the tissue origins from given gene expressions[1, 2]. The challenge lies in the limited capacity of analytical models to adequately capture the inherent variability of gene expressions across samples. Additionally, the reliance on a reference panel to calculate sample means in these algorithms inevitably results in data loss. To address these issues, we propose a novel approach utilizing a modified deep neural network for gene expression deconvolution. By leveraging this method, tissue origins can be inferred directly, circumventing the need for a reference panel, and thereby preventing data loss while better capturing sample variabilities. Moreover, the configurability of a deep neural network's input enables us to tailor input genes, harnessing the predictive power of tissue-specific genes. This customization enhances the accuracy and relevance of our predictions. To test our proposed method to address the challenges, the following steps are in consider.

- Exploration of deep neural network architectures optimized for accurate tissue deconvolution, with a focus on heterogeneous samples such as circulating tumor cell RNA (ctcRNA).
- Investigation into the identification of tissue origins of tumors and the uncovering of cellular dynamics through computational analysis.
- Comparative analysis between our approach and traditional methods such as NNLS, addressing their respective advantages and disadvantages.
- Examine the applicability of cross-tissue and cross-platform datasets, assessing their utility in enhancing the robustness of our model through comprehensive evaluation.

Gene mapping refers to the intricate process of correlating gene expressions onto a unified gene vector. This approach allows for the accurate inference of all gene expressions, facilitating comprehensive understanding and analysis of genetic data. Due to the large size of the human genome, the number of genes of interest could be in the magnitude of 10 4 . Mapping such large number of genes could mean the consumption of immense amounts of computing resources. Thankfully, the recent advancement in computing power has made this quest easier. Previously, genes were mapped using a unified gene expression vector via a linear regression model[3]. However, this method's limitation lies in its assumption that all genes can form a linear relationship with the gene vector. Such an oversimplified approach disregards the intricate biological processes and the considerable variance in gene expression levels. Various attempts have been made to improve gene mapping techniques, including the utilization of more sophisticated models. For instance, customized neural network models with neighboring connections stored in a list (NCNN)[4] or graph convolutional neural networks incorporating 3D genomic distances as edges (GC-MERGE)[5] have been explored. Despite these advancements, none of the models or methods have consistently achieved universally satisfactory results across different data platforms. The primary challenges stem from the vast number of edges or connections that must be simulated between tens of thousands of genes. Theoretically, the number of connections between all relevant genes could reach magnitudes ranging from 10^6 to 10^8, presenting a daunting computational task. We introduce two innovative models, the Graph Convolution Model (GCN) and the Principal Node Aggregation (PNA) model, which integrate a comprehensive array of connections (edges) across various biological spectra. These spectra include biological pathways, protein-protein interactions, and multi-dimensional genomic distances. Our research represents a pioneering effort, systematically examining the impact of diverse biological connections on gene mapping performance. Moreover, our study stands as the first to fully integrate the complete network of connections between mapped genes within the models, providing a holistic approach to gene mapping that accounts for the intricacies of biological interactions. The following steps are in consideration for this study.

- Development of the established graph GCN models and other types of graph neural network models tailored for gene mapping by integrating multi-omics data

- sources including gene expression, different scopes of gene distances and regulatory networks and pathways.
- Examination of the effectiveness of GCN models in deciphering gene regulatory networks and understanding the influence of different edge types on mapping accuracy.
- Benchmark the GCN models against more traditional models such as MLPs and autoencoders.
- Validation of model performance through comprehensive benchmarking against other state-of-the-art models such as the NCNN and GC-MERGE.
- Investigate the model performance impacts in tissue specific or cancer specific tasks.

DNA methylation is a biological phenomenon wherein methyl groups are added to the DNA molecule, typically at cytosine nucleotides. This epigenetic modification plays a crucial role in regulating gene expression and various cellular processes by influencing chromatin structure and transcriptional activity. Despite the crucial significance of methylation data, its completeness is often compromised during sequencing processes. Various technologies exist for sequencing methylation data, yet the extent of completeness varies significantly, spanning from as low as 1% to as high as 70% across all currently available platforms. This data is typically represented as a one-dimensional array, with each element denoting a decimal number ranging between 0 and 1 for each sample. These values signify the probability of methylation occurring at a specific DNA location, providing valuable insights into epigenetic regulation. A prior investigation revealed that employing a custom deep learning model integrating a Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) component to capture neighboring methylation information, alongside a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) component to capture base pair information, yielded promising results in predicting center methylation states[6]. However, a notable limitation of this approach is its restricted ability to accurately predict center methylation states, particularly at low coverages. Additionally, this method introduces -1 as a placeholder for missing methylation states and will also produce -1 as a model output, exacerbating the issue of reduced coverage and diminishing the practicality of the model. We propose a novel Diffusion Denoise Probabilistic Model (DDPM) that utilizes the generative power of the diffusion models to accurately generate all missing states for a given window. Also, by using high density and high coverage datasets, we eliminated the need to introduce placeholders for missing states, and the model will not produce 'missing' as an output. Our preliminary results show that our method is highly effective and accurate and suitable for mass deployments in completing first-hand sequenced methylation matrices. The following steps are in consideration for this study.

- Implementation of deep generative models, such as the Diffusion Denoise Probabilistic Model (DDPM), for accurate imputation of missing methylation data.
- Validation of imputation results using collected datasets and comparison with existing traditional imputation techniques to assess accuracy and reliability.
- Comparison of our model and other state-of-the-art models such as the DeepCpG model.

- Examination of the potential capabilities of the DDPM model in completing and imputing published public datasets with significant missing states.

## SIGNIFICANCE OF PROPOSED RESEARCH

Cancer stands as a formidable global health challenge, exacting a significant toll on human lives worldwide. The World Health Organization's alarming statistics reveal the magnitude of this issue, with approximately 10 million deaths attributed to cancer in 2020 alone. Projections suggest a further exacerbation of this crisis, with an estimated 29.5 million new cancer diagnoses and 16.4 million cancer-related deaths expected by 2040. Of particular concern is the role of metastasis, which accounts for approximately 90% of cancer-related deaths. Metastasis entails the dissemination of cancer cells from the primary tumor to distant organs, posing immense challenges in treatment and management. The burgeoning field of machine learning has increasingly permeated cancer research, offering novel avenues for early detection, prognosis, and personalized treatment. Notably, the analysis of circulating genomic information obtained from blood samples has emerged as a promising approach for detecting tissue origins of tumors. Pioneering studies have demonstrated the feasibility of predicting tissue origin based on methylation patterns of circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) and cell-free RNAs (cfRNA). However, traditional regression methods employed in these studies rely on reference panels, potentially limiting their accuracy and applicability. In contrast, we propose a neural network-based approach that leverages individual-level feature information to enhance accuracy and comprehensiveness in tissue origin prediction. Moreover, we extend the scope of tissue deconvolution to include circulating tumor cell RNA (ctcRNA), which offers insights into disease progression and metastatic potential. By analyzing ctcRNA alongside cfDNA and cfRNA, we aim to facilitate early detection of metastasis and improve clinical outcomes.

## RESEARCH STATUS IN AI ASSISTED TISSUE DECONVOLUTION

In cancer biology, gene expression analysis serves as a cornerstone in understanding biological processes and disease mechanisms. Co-expression analysis has provided invaluable insights into gene networks and functional interactions. Despite advancements in this field, conventional approaches often overlook non-linear gene interactions and fail to capture comprehensive associations among genes. To address these limitations, we introduce an innovative method for gene expression prediction using graph neural networks (GNNs). By integrating multiple graph features representing regulatory and structural elements, our approach enhances predictive modeling accuracy and provides a more holistic view of gene expression connectivity. We hypothesize that this new approach will yield superior results compared to traditional methods, thereby advancing our understanding of gene networks and disease mechanisms such as cancer. By doing so, we aim to pave the way for the development of more effective gene therapies tailored to the specific needs of cancer patients. Through our research, we aim to provide valuable insights that can directly inform the design and implementation of new treatment strategies, ultimately leading to improved outcomes and quality of life for individuals battling cancer.

Cancer-related deaths can be attributed to metastasis, which denotes the dissemination of cancer cells from the primary tumor site to distant locations within the body. Steps of metastasis consist of detachment of the cells from the primary tumor into blood, their transport through the blood stream, and dissemination to organ sites distant from the primary lesions and establishment of tumors in these sites. Once metastasis has occurred, the treatment of the disease becomes increasingly challenging. The early detection of metastasis bears significant clinical implications, as it allows for timely adjustments in treatment strategies[7]. The utilization of machine-learning techniques in cancer research has experienced a remarkable surge in recent years[8]. One notable application of such techniques involves the detection of tissue origin through the analysis of circulating genomic information gleaned from blood samples. For instance, Moss et al. established a model capable of predicting tissue origin based on methylation patterns of circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA)[1]. They employed a methodology known as nonnegative least squares linear regression (NNLS), which is commonly employed to predict continuous values by constraining all model parameters to be greater than or equal to zero. Subsequently, Larson et al. developed a distinct model using quadratic linear regression to predict the tissue origin of circulating cell-free RNAs (cfRNA)[9]. Although the regression methods utilized in these studies differed, they shared a common requirement: the utilization of a reference panel containing the average or median of the feature levels. The reference-based approach has been extensively employed in the realm of biological research, notably exemplified by the widely recognized PAM50 method that employs a 50-gene panel to effectively classify breast cancer intrinsic subtypes[10]. Although the reference panel approach yields sufficiently accurate results, there exists a notable opportunity for substantial improvement. One of the key limitations associated with the reference panel approach pertains to the loss of information incurred during panel construction, necessitating the computation of average or median values across all samples and for all features. Consequently, this procedure results in the exclusion of feature-specific information pertaining to individual samples. In contrast, we propose the adoption of a neural network-based approach, wherein the model training process encompasses the consideration of feature information from all individuals within the training dataset. This alternative approach effectively captures the inherent variation present within the data, thus enhancing accuracy and providing a more comprehensive analysis framework.
