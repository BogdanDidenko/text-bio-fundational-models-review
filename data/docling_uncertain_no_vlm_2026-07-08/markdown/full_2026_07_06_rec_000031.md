## CLAMP: predicting specific protein-mediated chromatin loops in diverse species with a chromatin accessibility language model

### Abstract

Emerging DNA language models provide powerful tools to address the challenge of accurately predicting chromatin loops, fundamental structures governing 3D genome organization and gene regulation. Here we present CLAMP, which utilizes a deep language model pre-trained on broad cross-species chromatin accessibility data. CLAMP achieves superior performance compared to existing methods in predicting specific protein-mediated loops across 10 species, 18 proteins, and 24 cell types. CLAMP incorporates a novel CoVE explainer that reveals context-dependent genomic feature contributions, providing insights into the features driving predictions. CLAMP predictions effectively identify functionally significant chromatin loops and associated biological pathways.

Keywords: Chromatin loops, 3D Genome, Language models, Epigenomics

### Background

The three-dimensional (3D) architecture of the eukaryotic genome orchestrates cellular function through precise spatial organization of regulatory elements [1]. At the heart of this organization are chromatin loops-dynamic structures that physically bridge distant genomic regions to coordinate gene expression across vast molecular distances [2-4]. These loops enable enhancers and promoters separated by millions of base pairs to interact with exquisite specificity, forming the structural foundation for cellular identity and function [5]. Critically, when these loops are disrupted, the consequences can be catastrophic, driving developmental disorders and cancer through dysregulated gene expression programs [6, 7]. Despite their fundamental importance, our ability to predict where and when these loops form remains severely limited, constraining our understanding of genome function and disease mechanisms.

Current  experimental  approaches  for  mapping  chromatin  interactions,  while groundbreaking,  face  inherent  constraints  that  limit  their  widespread  application. Techniques based on Chromosome Conformation Capture (3C) and its derivativesincluding Hi-C [8], ChIA-PET [9], and HiChIP [10]-require substantial resources, complex protocols, and large cell populations, while providing limited resolution [11, 12]. These experimental bottlenecks have created an urgent need for computational methods that can accurately predict chromatin loops from readily available genomic data, democratizing access to 3D genome analysis across diverse biological systems.

Early computational efforts made significant strides but were limited by their reliance on specific sequence motifs or a narrow range of epigenetic features [13, 14]. Deep learning approaches advanced the field by learning complex patterns from DNA sequence and epigenetic data [15-18]. However, these methods suffer from critical limitations: most are restricted to specific species or proteins [19, 20], their 'black-box' nature obscures biological  interpretation  [21,  22],  and  they  fail  to  optimally  integrate  the  multi-modal information: sequence patterns, epigenetic context, and genomic distance constraints, which govern loop formation.

Can we overcome these limitations by developing a unified framework that integrates diverse genomic information through an interpretable, biologically-grounded approach? The key insight lies in recognizing that chromatin loops predominantly connect regions of  accessible  chromatin, where regulatory elements reside in open, functionally active states [23, 24]. These accessible regions, readily profiled by ATAC-seq [25] and DNaseseq [26] across species [27], contain the genomic and epigenomic signatures essential for loop formation. Inspired by advances in genomic language modeling [28-36] and loop extrusion mechanisms [37, 38], we reasoned that DNA foundation models pre-trained on extensive chromatin accessibility data could capture the complex sequence dependencies and long-range interactions that determine loop formation.

Here, we present CLAMP (Chromatin Loop prediction via Accessibility language Model for Protein-mediated loops), a framework built upon a language model pre-trained on extensive, cross-species chromatin accessibility data. CLAMP primarily addresses the challenge of predicting chromatin loops by integrating multi-modal information. Moreover, its foundation model architecture provides biological sequence representations potentially applicable to a broader range of genomic tasks. To overcome the 'black-box' challenge often associated with deep learning, we also introduce CoVE (Coefficient of Variation Explainability), a novel interpretability  method  designed  to  systematically  dissect  the  factors  driving  predictions of CLAMP and their context dependency. By leveraging these innovations, CLAMP aims to  overcome  the  limitations  of  previous  methods,  offering  a  powerful,  interpretable,  and broadly applicable tool for investigating 3D genome organization across diverse biological contexts and opening new avenues for understanding genome function and disease. A userfriendly web platform   CLAMP + is available at https://  clamp.  sysom  ics.  com.

### Results

#### Overview of CLAMP

CLAMP is a computational framework for accurately predicting chromatin loops across diverse  species,  mediating  proteins,  and  cell  types  (Fig.  1).  CLAMP  integrates  three key genomic features: (1) deep semantic DNA sequence representations derived from pre-training  on  large-scale,  cross-species  chromatin  accessibility  data;  (2)  local  epigenetic signals at loop anchors; and (3) the genomic distance between anchors. The framework employs a pre-training and fine-tuning strategy.

DNA sequences from peak regions are extracted and processed via a tokenizer to form the input corpus. The resulting pre-trained CLAMP representations are applicable to various genomic tasks. c The CLAMP fine-tuning phase. Three data modalities are integrated for chromatin loop prediction: DNA sequence, local epigenetic signals, and genomic distance. Post fine-tuning, the CoVE Explainer enables model interpretation

During pre-training (Fig. 1b), we used 59 chromatin accessibility datasets from 7 species to train a 12-layer Transformer encoder. This deep language model learns to predict masked sequence fragments (k-mers) [39], capturing DNA sequence syntax and longrange dependencies relevant to higher-order chromatin structure. This process generates informative sequence representations and establishes a foundation for subsequent task-specific fine-tuning.

In the fine-tuning phase (Fig. 1c), the pre-trained model is adapted for specific loop prediction tasks. This stage fuses multi-modal information: it not only utilizes the pretrained encoder to extract deep features from anchor DNA sequences but also concurrently processes local epigenetic signals at the anchor regions and the genomic distance between anchor pairs. To model the symmetric nature of loop interactions [40], CLAMP incorporates a symmetric multi-head attention mechanism that integrates sequence and signal  features  from  both  anchors.  These  fused  features,  along  with  the  inter-anchor genomic  distance,  are  input  into  a  classifier  that  predicts  the  probability  of  loop  formation. To enhance adaptability, reduce computational cost, and preserve pre-trained knowledge,  we  utilized  the  (IA) 3 [41]  parameter-efficient  fine-tuning  technique.  This allows  for  the  fine-tuning  of  nearly  0.066%  of  the  total  parameters,  enabling  efficient adaptation across numerous chromatin loop datasets representing 10 species, 18 protein factors, and 24 cell types.

Through  this  strategy,  which  combines  deep  sequence  pre-training,  multi-modal information fusion, and parameter-efficient fine-tuning, the CLAMP framework aims to provide a powerful and flexible tool to tackle the challenge of accurately predicting chromatin loops in diverse biological contexts.

#### CLAMP accurately predicts chromatin loops across species, cell types and proteins

To evaluate the broad applicability of CLAMP, we analyzed 56 diverse chromatin loop datasets from five major eukaryotic groups: mammals, other vertebrates, invertebrates, plants and fungi. Chromatin loop structures varied substantially between species, with anchor region lengths ranging from 1 kb in fungi to 10 kb in mammals (Additional file 1: Fig.  S1a),  and  loop  distances  from  approximately  30  kb  in  fungi  to  megabase-scale  in mammals (Additional file 1: Fig. S1b). This diversity validates the comprehensiveness of our training dataset and underscores the challenge of developing models that capture both conserved and species-specific patterns.

CLAMP demonstrated high accuracy across these diverse datasets. In human cells, it  predicted  loops  mediated  by  different  proteins  with  Matthews  correlation  coefficients (MCC) exceeding 0.9 (Fig. 2a) and maintained this performance across cell types (Fig.  2b),  suggesting  the  model  captures  universal  chromatin  organization  principles. Multi-species prediction capabilities of CLAMP (Fig. 2c, d) were strongest in mammals and other vertebrates (AUC &gt; 0.990), robust in plants (AUC = 0.960) and invertebrates (AUC = 0.874),  and  better  than  random  even  in  fungi  (AUC = 0.742).  These  results demonstrate the capacity of CLAMP to predict chromatin loops across evolutionary distances, diverse cell types and different regulatory mechanisms.

Generalization  experiments  further  established  the  generalization  capabilities  of CLAMP across biological dimensions (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). These particular experiments  were  designed  to  specifically  isolate  the  model's  ability  to  generalize  learned sequence and distance patterns to a new context while holding the epigenetic landscape constant. In cross-protein prediction tasks (Additional file 1: Fig. S2a), CLAMP showed strong adaptability. For example, in human GM12878, a model trained on CTCF data accurately predicted RAD21-mediated loops. Similar performance was observed in H9 cells and mESC cells between different proteins, indicating CLAMP captures structural features beyond specific transcription factor binding preferences.

When tested  across  cell  types  within  the  same  species  (Additional  file  1:  Fig.  S2b), CLAMP  maintained  consistent  performance.  MCC  exceeded  0.96  when  comparing  CTCF or SMC1-mediated loops across human cell types (GM12878 vs. HeLa-S3/ HCT116), and surpassed 0.98 when comparing KLF4-mediated loops between mouse cell types. This consistency suggests that sequence and epigenetic determinants of chromatin loops are conserved across cell lineages within species.

In cross-species prediction tests (Additional file 1: Fig. S2c), models trained on conserved  proteins  (CTCF,  SMC1)  showed  excellent  performance  between  human  and mouse, confirming organizational similarities between these mammals.  Notably, CLAMP also showed significant generalization potential between evolutionarily distant human and maize for RNAPII-mediated loops, indicating it captures deeply conserved organizational principles.

#### CLAMP adapts to diverse epigenetic contexts for specific loop predictions

Having established strong generalization capabilities of CLAMP, we investigated its ability to incorporate new context-specific epigenetic information for predicting chromatin loops. Our approach used the fine-tuned CLAMP with fixed parameters, substituting the epigenetic signal inputs with those from target conditions before evaluating performance on independent test sets (Fig. 3a).

We first demonstrated that CLAMP effectively integrates cell-specific signals to predict chromatin loops across different cell types. Models trained on A673-CTCF data achieved high prediction accuracy (AUC &gt; 0.9, Fig. 3b) when tested on other human cell types with their respective epigenetic signals. Intersection analysis revealed minimal overlap between chromatin loops from different cell types (Additional file 1: Fig. S3), confirming the cell type-specificity of these predictions. The model also performed well across multiple species (Additional file 1: Fig. S4), demonstrating broad taxonomic applicability.

We next evaluated the performance of CLAMP across varying Hi-C resolutions (1-25 kb) [42]. Models finetuned on A673-CTCF HiChIP datasets maintained high prediction accuracy (MCC &gt; 0.9, Fig. 3c) when predicting loops from both intact Hi-C and in situ Hi-C  experiments  across  multiple  cell  types,  with  only  minor  performance  decreases at lower resolutions as expected. CLAMP also showed compatibility with signals from diverse epigenetic sequencing technologies. CLAMP effectively utilized data from bulk ATAC-seq, DNase-seq, ChIP-seq and single-cell ATAC-seq to predict chromatin loops with high precision (MCC &gt; 0.85, Fig. 3d).

In  a  more  practical  but  challenging  application,  we  assessed  whether  CLAMP  could predict chromatin loops using only peak information from epigenetic data. We used peak regions identified by different epigenetic sequencing technologies as sequence and distance feature inputs to predict loops formed by these regions. This approach has significant practical application, potentially enabling chromatin loop inference using only low-cost accessible technologies like ATAC-seq. Results were promising: even with such reduced information, CLAMP effectively predicted subsets of target loops in most cases (AUPRC &gt; 0.8, Fig. 3e), demonstrating its ability to leverage discrete genomic regions for loop prediction.

#### CLAMP outperforms existing methods across diverse benchmarks

In order to evaluate the performance of CLAMP with existing methods, a comparison was conducted with six open-source models for loop prediction that have been published since 2021 (Fig. 4a).

We first tested the models on the Bacon [43] dataset, the current gold-standard chromatin loop dataset. In the Bacon dataset, negative samples are randomly extracted from genomic regions after excluding known functional areas and strong interaction signals. These negative samples often contain regions with features that are significantly different from those in positive samples, potentially allowing models to learn 'shortcut features' rather than biologically relevant interaction patterns [44]. In the majority of test conditions,  CLAMP demonstrated the highest AUC (Fig. 4b). Despite all models exhibiting  diminished  performance  in  predicting  human  methylation  modification-mediated loops, CLAMP demonstrated a relatively high AUC (Additional file 1: Fig. S5).

We then assessed on our self-constructed CLAMP dataset. The negative samples in this  dataset  are  sampled  from  the  anchors  of  positive  samples,  selecting  regions  with similar features to positive samples but without detected loops. These adversarial negative samples more closely resemble positive samples, increasing the difficulty of discrimination while forcing models to learn more complex interaction patterns [45]. On this challenging dataset, CLAMP significantly outperformed other models on both overall average (Fig. 4c) and multi-species performance (Additional file 1: Fig. S6).

To  test  the  architectural  robustness  of  each  model,  we  evaluated  its  sensitivity  to swapped  loop  anchors  using  the  Bacon-swap  dataset.  The  performance  of  CLAMP remained virtually unchanged after anchor swapping, demonstrating effective adaptation to the symmetry of chromatin loop anchor interactions. In contrast, several other models showed notable performance declines (Fig. 4d).

Beyond  performance  metrics,  we  systematically  evaluated  all  models  across  four dimensions:  supported  input,  compatibility,  performance  benchmark  and  usability features (Fig. 4a). CLAMP supports multiple 3 C datasets and integrates epigenetic signals  for  context-specific  predictions.  Its  functional  compatibilities  include  direct  loop inference  from  epigenetic  signals,  built-in  symmetry  adaptation,  demonstrated  crossprotein/species generalization capability and a comprehensive explainability framework. Regarding usability, CLAMP and CLNN-loop provide convenient webserver.

#### CLAMP demonstrates broad applicability beyond loop prediction through biological representations

CLAMP demonstrated a proven aptitude for  predicting  chromatin  loops  across  various species and contexts, indicating that its pre-training phase captured fundamental genomic features that extend beyond specific looping mechanisms. This is analogous to the 'genomic grammar' learned by state-of-the-art DNA language models [29, 32, 36].

To assess the generalizability of learned representations, we evaluated the pre-trained CLAMP on a suite  of  seven  genomics-related  downstream  tasks  (Fig.  5a,  b).  Benchmarking against leading DNA language models and a conventional CNN revealed that CLAMP significantly outperformed the CNN across all tasks and achieved performance comparable to established foundation models (Fig. 5c). CLAMP performed best on tasks involving species included in the pre-training data (human, mouse), while for species absent from pre-training (yeast, virus), it ranked second only to DNABERT-2, indicating cross-species generalization potential (Additional file 1: Fig. S7). These findings corroborate that CLAMP learns versatile sequence representations with a 'genomic grammar' that is broadly applicable to diverse genomic prediction challenges.

Having established the general utility of CLAMP's representations, we next sought to elucidate  their  biological  relevance.  We  analyzed  the  internal  attention  mechanism  of CLAMP, which revealed an ability to identify key functional genomic regions. Attention scores compared with ChIP-seq data and motif information from human, zebrafish and chicken showed that high attention scores accurately identified important regulatory elements. In human GM12878, the highest attention scores coincided with CTCF

binding sites and their core motifs (Fig. 5d). In zebrafish embryos, the highest attention scores aligned with H3K4me3 modification regions (associated with promoter activity [46]; Additional file 1: Fig. S8a), while in chicken embryos, they corresponded to RNAPII binding  sites  (Additional  file  1:  Fig.  S8b).  This  ability  to  recognize  diverse  regulatory elements  across  species  demonstrates  that  CLAMP  learned  biologically  significant sequence patterns during pre-training.

#### CoVE analysis reveals the critical yet variable role of genomic distance

We previously demonstrated that CLAMP accurately predicts chromatin loops across diverse biological contexts. Furthermore, using attention-based methods, we discovered that pre-trained CLAMP captures features of important proteins, including transcription  factors.  However,  these  attention-based  approaches  cannot  compare  the  relative importance of features across multiple modalities, leaving unanswered which featuresdistance or signal-contribute more significantly to predictions. To address this limitation and understand the mechanistic basis of predictions, we developed Coefficient of Variation Explainability (CoVE), a novel interpretability method tailored for large-scale, multi-modal fusion models (Fig. 6a).

To  investigate  context-dependent  feature  contributions,  we  applied  CoVE  analysis to five datasets spanning different cell types, proteins and species (Fig. 6b). The results revealed distinct biological context dependencies. Distance features contributed differently  between  HeLa-S3-CTCF  and  K562-CTCF  samples,  while  signal  features  varied between K562-CTCF and K562-YY1. The YY1 protein showed different feature contribution patterns between human and mouse. These findings demonstrate that CLAMP dynamically adjusts its weighting of sequence, signal and distance information based on specific  biological  contexts,  reflecting  the  diverse  mechanisms  underlying  chromatin loop formation.

When analyzing YY1-mediated loops in mouse ESC dataset, CoVE analysis revealed that Signal features correlated most strongly with prediction outcomes (Additional file 1: Fig.  S9a).  This  suggests  that  the  local  chromatin  environment-including  chromatin accessibility  and  histone  modifications-dominates  YY1-mediated  loop  formation  in these cells, potentially reflecting the regulatory role of epigenetic states at YY1 binding sites during pluripotency maintenance and early differentiation, consistent with previous findings [47-49].

To assess the robustness of feature contributions, we evaluated their stability by introducing increasing levels of Gaussian noise to the top 10 most important features. We focused  on  the  feature  dimensions  derived  from  the  sequence  encoder  (input  DNA Sequence1 of loop anchor1) for the mouse ESC YY1 dataset (Fig. 6c). At a low noise level ( σ = 0.1),  the  relative  rankings  of  all  top  10  feature  dimensions  remained  unchanged compared to the no-noise condition ( σ = 0.0),  indicating  high  stability  against  minor perturbations. Increasing the noise to a moderate level ( σ = 0.5) resulted in minimal disruption, with only four feature dimensions swapping positions, while the other six maintained their ranks. However, at a high noise level ( σ = 1.0), more significant rank shifts were observed, particularly among features ranked 3rd/4th and 8th-10th. These findings suggest that while many sequence features are robust, some exhibit greater sensitivity to strong perturbations. This pattern highlights the differential robustness of the learned sequence features, with numerous high-ranking dimensions maintaining their relative importance even under substantial noise, while others exhibit fluctuations or a decline in rank. Signal features displayed moderate sensitivity (Additional file 1: Fig. S9b), while distance features exhibited substantial variability (Additional file 1: Fig. S9c).

Quantitative stability scores confirmed this hierarchy: sequence and signal features showed  high  stability  scores  (&gt;0.85),  while  distance  features  were  significantly  less stable (Fig. 6d), consistent with the inherent variability of long-range chromatin interactions [50, 51]. Nevertheless, feature ablation experiments demonstrated the paramount importance of genomic distance: its removal caused the largest performance decline (Fig. 6e). In this analysis, the 'Sequence' component represents the complete pre-trained DNA Sequence Encoder module. These results underscore that, despite its variability, distance is a critical determinant for loop prediction by CLAMP.

In  summary, CoVE analysis confirms the reliability of  sequence  and  signal  features, reveals the crucial yet variable role of distance in loop predictions, and identifies significant context dependency in model behavior. These findings offer computational insights into  the  mechanisms  that  govern  chromatin  loop  formation  across  various  biological contexts.

#### CLAMP reveals functionally significant chromatin loops and biological pathways

We examined the relationship between CLAMP prediction scores and chromatin loop interaction  intensity  (measured  by  PET  count)  on  CLAMP  dataset.  High-frequency interacting loops consistently received high prediction confidence scores (&gt;0.99), while low-frequency loops scored lower (&lt;0.65) (Additional file 1: Fig. S10a). Loops ranking in the top 60% by prediction score showed prediction probabilities concentrated above 0.99 (Additional  file  1:  Fig.  S10b).  This  indicates  that  high-scoring  predictions  of  CLAMP effectively enrich for stronger interacting chromatin loops with potentially greater functional significance, providing a valuable screening strategy for functional analyses.

Pathway  analysis  on  3D  genomic  data  is  commonly  used  to  reveal  gene  regulatory  mechanisms  and  explore  disease  pathways  [52-54].  To  assess  the  value  of  highconfidence  predictions  of  CLAMP,  we  examined  human  triple-negative  breast  cancer (TNBC) cell type and 20-HE treated drosophila embryonic cell type. Comparison was made between the pathway enrichment results of genes associated with loops identified by traditional methods and those identified by CLAMP high-score predictions (Fig. 7).

In  TNBC  samples,  the  two  methods  showed  partial  overlap  in  identified  pathways (Fig.  7a,  orange).  Both captured core cancer-related pathways such as 'MicroRNAs in cancer' , validating their basic effectiveness (Additional file 2: Table S14-S15). However, predictions by CLAMP not only encompassed pathways found by traditional methods but also revealed unique pathways (Fig. 7a, red). Notably, CLAMP uniquely identified the MAPK signaling pathway, which regulates cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival,  and  is  implicated  in  tumor  growth,  metastasis,  and  treatment  resistance-characteristics  highly  relevant  to  TNBC,  consistent  with  previous  research  [55].  Pathway clustering analysis (Additional file 1: Fig. S11a) revealed functional connections between CLAMP-identified  pathways,  while  gene-pathway  association  networks  (Fig.  7c)  pinpointed key gene sets driving these enrichments.

Similar patterns emerged in analyses of 20-HE treated drosophila embryonic cell type (Fig. 7b). Both methods identified pathways related to cellular stress and damage repair, such as 'Apoptosis' and 'Toll and Imd Signaling Pathway' (Additional file 2: Table S14S15), reflecting potential responses to hormone treatment. CLAMP uniquely enriched the 'TGF-beta signaling pathway' (Fig. 7b, red), which regulates cell growth, differentiation, and response to external stimuli-processes central to drosophila metamorphosis [56]. Pathway clustering (Additional file 1: Fig. S11b) and gene-pathway association networks (Fig. 7d) further supported the functional relevance of these pathways.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that CLAMP identifies high-confidence, functionally  relevant  chromatin  loops.  Pathway  analyses  revealed  CLAMP  corroborates traditional  methods but uniquely uncovers critical pathways. These distinct biological insights  highlight  CLAMP  as  a  powerful  complementary  tool,  augmenting  traditional pathway enrichment for a more comprehensive understanding of 3D genome regulation.

#### CLAMP + : an integrated platform for chromatin loop prediction and analysis

We developed   CLAMP + , a user-friendly web platform that integrates core capabilities of CLAMP through an intuitive graphical interface (Fig. 8a). This comprehensive platform makes advanced chromatin loop prediction accessible to researchers with limited computational resources or programming expertise.

CLAMP + comprises two core modules. First, the DNA Embedding Analysis module allows users to submit genomic sequences for processing by the validated pre-trained CLAMP.  This  process  extracts  deep  features  as  embedding  that  capture  intrinsic sequence information. The module generates downloadable embedding files for downstream tasks, alongside principal component analysis visualizations and attention weight maps that reveal nucleotide contributions (Fig. 8b). Second, the Chromatin Loop Prediction  module enables users to set biological parameters and input query anchor pairs. This module leverages the capability of CLAMP to integrate epigenetic signals, enabling users to upload custom files from both bulk and single-cell epigenomic data for cell-specific predictions.   CLAMP + generates comprehensive reports with statistical summaries, prediction lists, and interactive visualizations (Fig. 8c).

The   CLAMP + platform transforms complex computational models into an accessible web service, lowering technical barriers for 3D genomics exploration. We anticipate that CLAMP + will advance chromatin structure and function research by enabling a broader scientific community to investigate the 3D regulatory landscape of the genome.

### Discussion

We introduce CLAMP, a computational framework that predicts chromatin loops using a  language  model  pre-trained  on  broad  chromatin  accessibility  data.  This  approach addresses critical limitations in current methods, offering a scalable, accurate, and interpretable tool to decipher the complex 3D regulatory landscape across diverse biological contexts. By integrating deep sequence features, local epigenetic signals, and genomic distance, CLAMP provides a robust foundation for exploring genome organization.

A key strength of CLAMP lies in its remarkable generalizability, demonstrated by its high  accuracy  across  10  species,  18  protein  factors,  and  24  cell  types.  This  contrasts sharply with previous computational methods, often restricted to specific species (typically  human or mouse) or mediating proteins (like CTCF) [16-20]. Coupled with the parameter-efficient (IA) 3 fine-tuning strategy [41], CLAMP efficiently adapts its learned representations  to  specific  loop  prediction  tasks,  enabling  broad  applicability  without prohibitive  computational  costs.  Crucially,  our  ablation  study  on  evolutionarily  distant lineages not represented in the pre-training data (invertebrates, plants, and fungi) confirmed  that  the  pre-trained  model  outperforms  random  initialization  (Additional file 3: Text S2). This demonstrates that the foundational 'genomic grammar' learned by CLAMP is transferable and provides benefits beyond vertebrate species.

Furthermore,  CLAMP  demonstrates  significant  flexibility  in  adapting  to  various experimental  contexts  without  complete  training.  It  successfully  integrates  cell-typespecific epigenetic signals and maintains high performance across different Hi-C resolutions  and  diverse  epigenetic  sequencing  technologies,  including  bulk/sc  ATAC-seq, DNase-seq, and ChIP-seq. Notably, its ability to predict loops using only peak information  from  accessibility  data  (Fig.  3e)  opens  avenues  for  inferring  chromatin  structure from relatively low-cost and widely available datasets, potentially lowering the barrier for 3D genomics studies.

Benchmarking against loop prediction methods [17-20, 57, 58] revealed the superior performance of CLAMP, particularly on our challenging dataset, which features adversarially selected negative samples designed to prevent learning 'shortcut' features [44, 45].  This  suggests  CLAMP learns more biologically relevant interaction patterns. Our comparative analysis of negative sampling strategies (Additional file 3: Text S4) further revealed that while the choice of negative samples can influence outcomes, the model's performance remains highly  robust,  likely  due  to  the  strong  foundational  representations  established  during  pre-training.  Its  inherent  handling  of  loop  anchor  symmetry, confirmed by robustness tests (Fig. 4d), reflects a deeper understanding of the bidirectional nature of chromatin interactions, likely related to mechanisms like loop extrusion [37, 38].

Beyond loop prediction, the pre-trained CLAMP model generates versatile sequence representations  applicable  to  a  range  of  genomic  tasks  (Fig.  5a-c),  performing  comparably  to  state-of-the-art  DNA  foundation  models  [29,  32].  Analysis  of  the  internal attention  mechanism  revealed  its  ability  to  identify  biologically  significant  regulatory elements, such as CTCF binding sites and promoter-associated histone marks (Fig. 5d, Additional file 1: Fig. S10), directly from sequence context. This indicates that pre-training on chromatin accessibility data effectively embeds a functional 'genomic grammar' of regulation.

A central design choice of CLAMP is its pre-training strategy, which focuses exclusively on sequences from chromatin accessibility peaks. This approach is rooted in the hypothesis  that  the  functional  'genomic  grammar'  governing  3D  interactions  is  predominantly located within these open, functionally active regions. By concentrating the training corpus on regulatory DNA, we focus the model's learning capacity on relevant syntax, in contrast to whole-genome models that must expend significant capacity representing  large,  often  non-functional  or  repetitive,  genomic  territories.  The  primary trade-off is that our model is not optimized to interpret sequence context within heterochromatin.  However,  since  our  primary  task  is  to  predict  loops  connecting  active regulatory elements, which are by definition in accessible chromatin, we consider this an advantageous specialization. This focus allows CLAMP to develop a more nuanced understanding of the specific sequence features relevant to gene regulation and looping.

To provide insights into how CLAMP integrates its multi-modal inputs, we developed the  CoVE explainer.  Rather  than  solely  addressing  model  opacity,  CoVE  is  tailored  to dissect the contributions of sequence, epigenetic signals, and genomic distance in biological models. It provides a complementary, aggregate-level view to local interpretability methods like SHAP. While local methods can explain individual predictions, CoVE is designed as a lightweight approach to assess the global importance of different data modalities, which is crucial for drawing broad biological conclusions about the mechanisms of loop formation in a specific context. This analytical approach revealed the critical  yet  highly  context-dependent role of genomic distance in loop prediction (Fig. 6). While  sequence  and  epigenetic  signal  features  exhibit  high  stability,  the  contribution of  distance  varies  significantly  across  different  cell  types,  proteins,  and  species.  This dynamic weighting likely reflects the diverse underlying biological mechanisms, where factors such as loop extrusion, phase separation, or specific architectural protein interactions may dominate in different contexts [7, 37, 47-49]. The high stability of sequence representations further validates the robustness learned during pre-training.

Crucially,  high-confidence predictions of CLAMP enrich for functionally significant interactions. The correlation between prediction scores and experimental loop strength (PET  count)  provides  a  valuable  hint  for  prioritizing  loops  for  functional  validation. Pathway analysis demonstrated that CLAMP not only recovers known disease-relevant pathways (e.g., 'MicroRNAs in cancer' in TNBC) but also uniquely identifies pathways like MAPK signaling in TNBC and TGF-beta signaling in 20-HE treated Drosophila cells (Fig.  7),  suggesting  it  captures  distinct  regulatory  information  missed  by  conventional analyses based solely on experimental interaction frequencies [55, 56]. The availability of the   CLAMP + web platform further democratizes access to these advanced prediction and analysis capabilities.

Despite  these  advances,  several  limitations  remain.  First,  CLAMP  focuses  primarily  on  pairwise  anchor  interactions  without  fully  incorporating  broader  genomic  context,  such  as  regulatory  influences  from  nearby  elements  or  TAD  boundary  stability. Second, CLAMP currently predicts static chromatin structures despite the inherently dynamic nature of chromatin interactions. Future work could integrate time-series data or  model dynamic loop formation and dissociation processes to enhance understanding. Additionally, while CoVE enhances interpretability, elucidating precise biophysical mechanisms requires integration with complementary experimental approaches. Third, as revealed by our distance-stratified performance analysis (Additional file 3: Text S3), CLAMP's predictive accuracy, while excellent for short-range and medium-range interactions (&lt;10 Mb), declines sharply for very long-range interactions. This suggests that CLAMP is  less  adept  at  identifying  interactions  governed  by  higher-order  structural principles,  such  as  confinement to specific nuclear compartments or TAD-TAD associations. Improving the prediction of these long-range interactions represents a critical direction for future development. This could involve incorporating features that describe the broader genomic context to better capture the multi-scale nature of genome organization. Fourth, our CoVE analysis revealed that while genomic distance is a critical predictor, its feature contribution is less stable than that of sequence or epigenetic signals. Our current model encodes this via a logarithmic transformation followed by a learned embedding network. However, a systematic exploration of alternative distance encoding  strategies,  such  as  binning  distances  into  discrete  categories  or  employing  more advanced learned relative positional embeddings, could further enhance model robustness and performance. Such an investigation represents an important avenue for future refinement  of  the  model,  potentially  providing  deeper  insights  into  the  non-linear impact of distance on chromatin looping.

Future  work  could  extend  CLAMP  to  process  structural  variants,  predict  higherorder chromatin structures, and incorporate allele-specific signals, further broadening its utility in deciphering genome architecture. Predicting higher-order structures represents a particularly important challenge. This would necessitate architectural evolution beyond the current pairwise model. Promising strategies include representing genomic loci  as  nodes in a graph and employing graph-based attention mechanisms to predict interaction edges, or using set transformer architectures designed to model unordered collections of elements. The primary limitation for such work is the scarcity of deeply annotated, high-resolution datasets required for supervised training on these complex interactions, but this remains a critical frontier for future development.

### Conclusions

We developed CLAMP, a versatile framework leveraging a novel chromatin accessibility language model for accurate chromatin loop prediction. By integrating multi-modal genomic data and employing parameter-efficient fine-tuning, CLAMP achieves superior performance  and  generalizability  across  diverse  species  and  conditions  compared  to existing methods. The accompanying CoVE explainer provides insights into the contextdependent mechanisms underlying loop formation. CLAMP overcomes key limitations of previous methods, providing a scalable, adaptable, and interpretable resource to significantly advance the study of 3D genome organization and its role in gene regulation.

### Methods

#### Data collection and preprocessing

##### Pre-training dataset

Previous studies have shown that incorporating multiple species dataset during DNA language model pre-training improves downstream task performance [28, 32, 36]. However, this approach is computationally intensive. De et al. [59] demonstrated that training DNA foundation model (SegmentNT) with just 5 species spanning certain evolutionary distances achieved remarkable results. To balance computational efficiency and model performance, we collected 59 ATAC-seq datasets across seven species with certain evolutionary distances (human, mouse, bovine, zebrafish, medaka and chicken) at different developmental  stages,  totaling  8,483,660  peak  regions.  These  datasets,  sourced  from Ouyang et al. [60] (Additional file 1: Fig. S12), provide potential anchor regions for chromatin loop formation, while their developmental dynamics help capture chromatin state changes.

##### Fine-tuning dataset

Our fine-tuning datasets comprise 56 chromatin loop datasets across 10 species, including 37 HiChIP experiments, 19 ChIA-PET experiments, 18 protein factors and 24 cell types (Additional file 2: Table S1). We collected HiChIP, ChIA-PET and corresponding epigenetic sequencing data (such as ATAC-seq and DNase-seq) from multiple repositories including GEO, ChromLoops [61] and ENCODE. All HiChIP and ChIA-PET data were processed uniformly using ChIA-PET Tool (V3) [62] following the ChromLoops workflow, while epigenetic sequencing data were processed using the official ENCODE pipeline (https://  www.  encod  eproj  ect.  org/  atac-  seq/).

For training dataset, we focused on intra-chromosomal interactions. Positive samples were identified where anchor regions overlapped with epigenetic signal summits. From these overlapping regions, we extracted DNA sequences and corresponding epigenetic signals extending 1,500 bp upstream and downstream. To better train the model to distinguish  genuine  loops  from  background  noise,  we  sampled  from  all  positive  sample anchor regions, selecting anchor pairs with characteristics similar to positive samples but without detected loop formation as negative samples. We maintained a 1:1 ratio of positive to negative samples, using consistent sequence and signal extraction methods for both. For each chromosome, we randomly selected up to 2,000 positive and 2,000 negative  samples.  To  prevent  data  leakage,  datasets  were  divided  by  chromosome  for training, validation and testing, with allocation strategies varying by species (Additional file 2: Table S2).

##### Hi-C dataset

To  assess  adaptability  of  CLAMP  to  different  3 C  technologies  and  resolutions,  we analyzed  eight  datasets  from  in  situ  and  intact  Hi-C  experiments  obtained  from  the ENCODE portal  (Additional  file  2:  Table  S3).  Data  were  processed  using  the  official ENCODE pipeline (https://  www.  encod  eproj  ect.  org/  hic/), with sample construction following the same method as our fine-tuning dataset.

##### Benchmark dataset

For  comprehensive  performance  evaluation,  we  established  the  following  benchmark datasets:

- (1)  Chromatin loop dataset (Additional file 2: Table S4): a. Bacon dataset [43]: a gold standard loop dataset comprising 22 groups of data from 2 species, 17 proteins and 9 cell types, used to compare with existing models as independent external datasets;  b.  CLAMP  dataset:  a  subset  of  our  complete  dataset  containing  31  groups of data across 7 species, 15 proteins and 15 cell types, designed to evaluate model performance across diverse biological contexts; c. Bacon-swap dataset: generated by swapping the order of anchor pairs in the Bacon dataset to rigorously test the model's ability to handle chromatin loop symmetry.
- (2)  Genomic tasks dataset: we selected seven tasks from the GenBench GUE dataset [29,  63],  including  Promoter  Detection,  Core  Promoter  Detection,  Transcription Factor  Prediction,  Promoter  Detection,  Splice  Site  Detection,  Epigenetic  Marks Prediction and Covid Variant Classification.

#### CLAMP model

##### Architecture design

- (1)  DNA Sequence Encoder: Built on the Transformer architecture, this encoder contains 12 layers with a 768-dimensional space and 12 self-attention heads per layer. It  incorporates  GELU  activation  functions  and  positional  encoding  to  preserve sequence order information.
- (2)  Epigenetic Signal Encoder: Each 1,500 bp anchor region is segmented into 64 equalwidth windows, within which average epigenetic signal intensities are calculated to produce a 64-dimensional vector. This vector passes through a network of linear transformations with ReLU activation, layer normalization and dropout regularization, generating a 128-dimensional embedding.
- (3)  Loop Anchor Distance Encoder: Genomic distances between anchor pair centers undergo logarithmic  transformation  before  processing  through  a  network  of  linear transformations and non-linear activations, yielding a 64-dimensional distance embedding.
- (4)  Loop  Prediction  Module:  To  maintain  interaction  symmetry,  sequence  and  signal  embeddings  from  both  anchors  are  separately  concatenated  then  processed through a symmetric multi-head attention module. The resulting output, combined with the distance embedding, feeds into a multilayer perceptron classifier that predicts chromatin loop formation probability.

##### Pre-training strategy

Pre-training aims to develop universal chromatin language representations from unlabeled  DNA  sequences.  We  employed  a  masked  language  model  approach,  randomly masking 15% of input DNA sequence tokens (k-mers) and training the model to predict these masked elements from context. We used cross-entropy loss and enabled FP16 half-precision to accelerate computation and reduce memory usage. Training was distributed across four NVIDIA A100 (40G) GPUs using the AdamW optimizer with an initial learning rate [64] of 1 × 10 -4 .  We implemented early stopping when loss showed no improvement for 2000 consecutive steps. Batch sizes and training steps for different configurations are detailed in Additional file 2: Table S5.

To  investigate  how  sequence  length  and  tokenizers  affect  model  performance,  we extended the central region of each peak to lengths ranging from 200 bp to 1,500 bp for pre-training input and used the k-mer or BPE strategy [65, 66] as tokenizer (Additional file 3: Text S1). Finally, all fine-tuning experiments were performed based on a 6-mer1500 bp CLAMP.

##### Fine-tuning strategy

Fine-tuning adapts the pre-trained model to specific chromatin loop prediction tasks. We implemented a parameter-efficient technique (IA) 3 [41], which introduces learnable scaling  factors  to  adjust  internal  activations  while  preserving  pre-trained  knowledge. In CLAMP, (IA) 3 introduces only approximately 64,500 trainable parameters (0.0656% of  the  model's nearly 98.3 million total parameters). For each chromatin loop dataset, we used binary  cross-entropy  loss  and  the  AdamW  optimizer  with  a  learning  rate  of 3 × 10 -5 . Training employed early stopping after 5 epochs without improvement, with a maximum of 50 epochs and a batch size of 24.

#### Generalization experiment

To assess model generalization, we conducted three cross-validation experiments (Additional file 2: Table S7):

- (1)  Cross-protein: Training with loops mediated by protein A and testing on protein B within the same cell type.
- (2)  Cross-cell type: Training with a specific protein in cell type X and testing on the same protein in cell type Y of the same species.
- (3)  Cross-species: Training with a specific (or functionally homologous) protein in one species and testing on the same protein in another species.

For all experiments, we used epigenetic signals from training dataset and evaluated performance on the complete target dataset using the MCC. This methodological choice was designed to specifically test the hypothesis that the model can generalize learned sequence and distance patterns from one biological context to another, while holding the epigenetic landscape constant. This approach is complementary to the experiments in Fig. 3b, which test the model's ability to adapt to new, target-specific epigenetic contexts.

#### Variable epigenetic signal experiment

We tested the ability of CLAMP to integrate new epigenetic signals without retraining. After  fine-tuning  the  model  on  source  data,  we  fixed  all  parameters  and  input  epigenetic signals from new target conditions (including different cell types, Hi-C resolution, or  sequencing  technologies  like  scATAC-seq).  We  then  evaluated  performance  on independent dataset  corresponding  to  these  new  conditions  (Additional  file  2:  Tables S8-S10).

For  predictions  based  solely  on  peak  information  (Additional  file  2:  Table  S11),  we generated candidate anchor pairs by combining genomic peak regions. We applied initial filtering using statistical thresholds (q-value &lt; 0.05, p -value &lt; 0.05) and signal intensity  (top  10%  signalValue),  excluding  regions  overlapping  with  training/validation  sets to prevent data leakage. From the large candidate pool, we retained all positive samples (experimentally validated loops) and randomly selected negative samples at a 10:1 ratio. For these imbalanced datasets, we used area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) as the evaluation metric.

#### Benchmark

We evaluated CLAMP through two benchmark approaches:

- (1)  Chromatin loop prediction comparison: We benchmarked the complete CLAMP model against six open-source chromatin loop prediction models published since 2021: CapsNetYY1 [20], ChINN [57], CLNN-loop [19], Deep-loop [17], DeepLUCIA [18], DLoopCaller [58]. For testing on Bacon and CLAMP dastaset, all models were trained and validated on the same training set and validation set, and tested on the same test set. For robustness test, models were trained on the original Bacon dataset and evaluated on the Bacon-swap dataset. Additional file 2: Table S6 details all hyperparameter settings.
- (2)  Genomic tasks evaluation: To fairly compare sequence representation quality, we connected the pre-trained CLAMP to linear layers and evaluated it against other DNA  language  models  (DNABERT  [67],  DNABERT-2  [29],  GENA-LM  [68], Nucleotide Transformer (NT)-V2 [32], HyenaDNA [69], Caduceus [70]) and traditional CNN model. All evaluations settings and results followed GenBench framework standards, using consistent hyperparameters (learning rate 3 × 10 -5 ,  cosine decay scheduler, AdamW optimizer).

#### CoVE explainer

Traditional  interpretability  methods  like  SHAP  [71],  LIME  [72]  and  gradient-based  [73] approaches  present  three  key  challenges  when  applied  to  large-scale,  multi-modal  biological CLAMP framework. First, nearly 100 million parameters of CLAMP create computational  inefficiencies  for  classical  methods.  Second,  multi-modal  fusion  architectures complicate the allocation of contribution values across different modalities [74]. Third, conventional methods lack sensitivity to biological data's unique properties [75], where chromatin interaction data exhibits highly non-linear, context-dependent characteristics [76].

To  elucidate  the  decision-making  mechanisms  of  CLAMP  for  chromatin  loop  prediction, we developed the CoVE explainer. CoVE extends the statistical concept of coefficient of variation [77] (CV, defined as σ/µ )  to  quantify  the  importance and stability of model input features. This lightweight post-hoc analysis method efficiently evaluates learned feature representations without requiring access to internal gradient information. Rather than directly applying standard CV, CoVE employs a specialized measure combining mean ( µ log ) and standard deviation ( σ log ) in logarithmic space. This approach appropriately handles the approximately log-normal distribution characteristics of biological data, comprehensively assessing different modal features (DNA sequence, epigenetic signals, genomic distance) by integrating central tendency e µ log with relative dispersion ( 1 + σ log ) .

The core analytical process of CoVE evaluates feature importance by quantifying variation coefficients of key intermediate layer representations extracted from CLAMP . These representations include sequence embeddings S 1, S 2 ∈ R 768 from the pre-trained encoder, signal  embeddings Sig 1 , Sig 2 ∈ R 128 from  the  signal  encoder,  and  distance  embedding D ∈ R 64 derived from logarithmic transformation of genomic distance between anchors.

#### Ablation experiment

To  quantify  each  input  modality's  contribution,  we  conducted  ablation  experiments across  8  representative  datasets  spanning  2  species,  6  proteins,  and  2  sequencing technologies  (Additional  file  2:  Table  S12).  We  separately  removed  inputs  from  DNA Sequence Encoder, Epigenetic Signal Encoder, or Loop Anchor Distance Encoder, then retrained and evaluated the model on test set using consistent hyperparameters.

#### Enrichment analysis

To  explore  the  biological  significance  of  high-confidence  predictions  of  CLAMP, we selected specific datasets (Additional file 2: Table S13) and identified chromatin loops with high interaction intensity (pet count &gt; 120) and top 60% CLAMP prediction scores. We performed KEGG pathway enrichment analysis ( p -value &lt; 0.05) on genes associated with these loop anchor regions following Wei et al [54]. We used the enrichKEGG function from the clusterProfiler R package [78]. Specifically, this function uses a hypergeometric test to assess the significance of pathway enrichment. To account for multiple testing across all pathways, the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was applied to control the False Discovery Rate (FDR). For comparison with CLAMP approaches,  we  performed  identical  analyses  without  applying  the  CLAMP  highconfidence filtering criteria. Pathway clustering and gene-pathway relationships were analyzed using plotthis [79].

#### CLAMP + web platform

We developed   CLAMP + ,  a  web  platform  to  extend  the  accessibility  of  CLAMP  for the broader research community. Built on PHP 8 with HTML, CSS, and JavaScript, it integrates core CLAMP functionalities including DNA embedding analysis and chromatin loop prediction with support for custom epigenetic signal uploads.   CLAMP + processes loop data and returns interactive visualizations and downloadable results. To protect user privacy, uploaded epigenetic signal files are immediately deleted after the report is generated.
