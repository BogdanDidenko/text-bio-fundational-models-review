### Abstract

Multimodal imaging-based on single-cell genomics and spatial transcriptomics has shed new light on the taxonomy of genetically defined cell clusters in the mammalian brain. While transcriptomic approaches have revolutionized our ability to classify brain cells, their true value emerges when they are interpreted in conjunction with anatomical, physiological, and  translational  frameworks. Accordingly,  significant  progress  has  been  made  to  elucidate  relationships  between  gene expression, electrical and morphological properties of some of these clusters. This rapidly growing body of work shows not only that the cell cluster composition varies across brain regions but also evolves over time and changes during the progression of disease states like Alzheimer's disease. Given this complexity, integrating transcriptomic, structural, and functional data is now becoming essential for drawing meaningful comparisons across studies. In this review, we summarize these findings and discuss how this knowledge base is shifting towards more integrative approaches, quickly chal -lenging current ideas regarding the genetic, molecular, and cellular underpinnings of Alzheimer's disease.

### Introduction

Transcriptomics  began  in  the  mid-1990s  with  the  development of DNA microarrays and evolved significantly in the late 2000s with the advent of RNA sequencing (RNAseq). The field advanced even further in the 2010s with the emergency of single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics. Over the past five years, these technologies have generated an increasingly granular view of the brain, highlighting a level of cellular diversity that was difficult to envision just a few years ago (Maynard et al. 2021; Yao et al. 2023, 2021 ). These high-resolution datasets have deepened our understanding of brain organization under physiological conditions and opened new avenues for exploring disease mechanisms,  including  those  underlying Alzheimer's  dis -ease (AD) (Gabitto et al. 2024; Grubman et al. 2019; Liang et al. 2025; Mathys et al. 2019 ). In particular, transcriptomic profiling has been instrumental to characterize the brain cel -lular composition in animal models of AD and in patients affected by this disease, offering insights into how neuro -degeneration alters brain architecture (Gabitto et al. 2024; Grubman et al. 2019; Liang et al. 2025; Mathys et al. 2019 ).

<!-- image -->

Despite these advances, transcriptomic approaches also raise  new  challenges  and  limitations  (Ecker  et  al. 2017; Scala et al. 2021; Tasic et al. 2018 ). A fundamental issue is that genetic profiles alone may not fully capture cell identity, because gene expression patterns, and the composition of cell clusters defined by these patterns, are not fixed (Ecker et  al.  2017;  Scala  et  al.  2021;  Tasic  et  al.  2018 ).  Instead, they vary dynamically across cell types, brain regions, species, stages of disease progression, and in the presence of comorbidities (Ecker et al. 2017; Scala et al.  2021; Tasic et al. 2018 ). These sources of variability can hinder repro -ducibility,  complicate  cross-study  comparisons,  and  limit the  generalizability of findings across biological contexts. These challenges are particularly relevant in the AD field, where understanding disease progression demands an accurate  and  stable  definition  of  cellular  phenotypes  across diverse  datasets.  For  example,  it  remains  unclear  how  to account for activity-dependent changes in gene expression that  may  shift  cell  cluster  identities,  or  how  the  presence of AD pathology influences the transcriptional signatures of specific  cell  populations.  Furthermore,  meaningful  crossspecies comparisons are difficult to achieve, as homologous cell types may respond differently to pathological processes in humans and animal models.

To  overcome  these  limitations,  there  is  an  increasing consensus  that  future  research  must  go  beyond  transcriptomics and adopt multimodal approaches (Ecker et al. 2017; Gabitto  et  al.  2024;  Scala  et  al.  2021;  Tasic  et  al.  2018 ). These  include  the  integration  of  gene  expression  profiles with complementary datasets that reflect cellular structure, connectivity, electrophysiological properties, and functional roles within circuits. Such integrative strategies are essential to develop a more comprehensive and physiologically relevant taxonomy of brain cell types. Importantly, recent multimodal studies have begun to identify specific neuronal populations that are disproportionately affected during the early stages of AD (Gabitto et al. 2024 ). Glutamatergic neu -rons  and  distinct  sub-classes  of  GABAergic  interneurons have emerged as the most vulnerable in the pseudo-progression  of  mild  cognitive  impairment  and  neurodegeneration (Gabitto et al. 2024 ). These findings underscore the poten -tial  of  combining  transcriptomic  data  with  structural  and functional readouts to pinpoint cellular vulnerabilities and circuit dysfunctions that contribute to cognitive decline.

The major goals of this perspective are: (i) to synthesize emerging findings from recent transcriptomic studies, pub -lished since the advent of bulk and scRNA-seq, on how AD influences cell cluster representation across brain regions; and (ii) to highlight important caveats and limitations that may affect data interpretation, particularly in the context of disease complexity, interindividual variability, and technical  constraints.  Ultimately,  we  support  shifting  towards integrative frameworks that more fully capture the multidimensional nature of brain cell identity in health and disease.

### Pathological Hallmarks of AD

AD  is  a  neurodegenerative  disease  characterized  by  loss of  short-term  memory  and  progressive  cognitive  decline. The main neuropathological hallmarks include extracellular deposition of amyloid-β peptide and intracellular accumula -tion of abnormally phosphorylated tau protein in neurons. In many cases, AD pathology is accompanied by vascular lesions, intracellular accumulation of phosphorylated transactive-response DNA-binding protein 43 (TDP-43), and/or α-synuclein aggregates. These pathological processes lead to  brain  atrophy  caused  by  neuronal  loss,  synapse  degeneration  and  alterations  in  glial  cell  phenotype,  indicating that both cell types are likely implicated in AD pathology (Tzioras et al. 2023 ). A common model of AD progression is  based  on  the  Braak  staging  of AD  tauopathy,  based  on post mortem examination of human brain tissue (Braak &amp; Braak 1991 ). According to the Braak staging, AD begins in sub-cortical  regions  (e.g.,  lower  brainstem,  olfactory  sys -tem) and progresses toward a widespread invasion of the neocortex (Braak et al. 2006 ):

- Stage  I:  Lesions  appear  in  the  trans-entorhinal  region (i.e., locus coeruleus , magnocellular nuclei of the basal forebrain)
- Stage II: Involvement of the entorhinal and hippocam -pal region
- Stage III: Spread to neocortical areas of the fusiform and lingual gyri
- Stage IV: Extension into neocortical association areas
- Stage V: Further spread into frontal, superolateral, and occipital regions, including the peristriate region
- Stage VI: Involvement of primary and secondary neo -cortical  areas  and,  in  the  occipital  lobe,  spread  to  the striate area

More recently, analysis of large-scale magnetic resonance imaging  (MRI)  databases  and  whole-brain  segmentation of  fine-grained  structures  using  a  large  ensemble  of  deep neural networks has led to the development of an in vivo staging  scheme  (Planche  et  al. 2022 ).  Unlike  Braak  stag -ing, which reflects microscopic tau pathology, MRI staging captures macroscopic patterns of brain atrophy, revealing a similar but not identical temporal progression:

- Stage I: affects the hippocampus and amygdala
- Stage II: affects the middle temporal gyrus
- Stage III: affects the entorhinal cortex, parahippocampal cortex and other temporal areas
- Stage IV: affects the striatum and thalamus
- Stage V: affects the middle frontal, cingular, parietal and insular cortices

These  staging  systems  are  complementary:  Braak  stag -ing  explains  where  the  pathology  originates  and  spreads, whereas  MRI  staging  shows  where  the  brain  is  damaged due to the AD pathology. While they converge on many of the same regions, the order in which they are listed in the staging system may differ. Both systems are used to classify the stage of progression of early-onset AD (EOAD), which affects 5-10% of patients aged &lt; 65 years and is caused by variants in the APP , PSEN1 ,  and PSEN2 genes,  and  lateonset AD (LOAD), which affects 90-95% of patients and does  not  have  a  single  dominant  genetic  cause  (ValdezGaxiola  et  al.  2024 ).  However,  whereas  LOAD  typically follows  the  Braak  progression,  EOAD  often  shows  tau pathology that spreads in a more diffuse and less Braak-like pattern, especially in atypical variants. Taken together, these neuropathological  and  imaging-based  staging  frameworks highlight the spatial and temporal heterogeneity of AD progression  and  underscore  how  genetic  background,  age  of onset, and clinical phenotype can shape disease trajectories. Recognizing these distinctions is critical not only for accu -rate disease classification, but also for improving the inter -pretation of multimodal biomarkers in different cell clusters across different brain regions.

### Genetic Associations and Definition of Brain Cell Classes, Subclasses, and States

Numerous genome-wide association studies (GWASs) have implicated a variety of genetic loci in AD, although their  functional  roles  sometimes  remains  unclear  because many  lie  in  non-coding  regions  (Bellenguez  et  al. 2022; Boix  et  al.  2021;  Jansen  et  al.  2019;  Kunkle  et  al.  2019; Lambert et al. 2013 ; Schwartzentruber et al. 2021 ). Known causal variants for AD include the ε4 haplotype in APOE , the  strongest  genetic  risk  factor  for  LOAD,  and  a  common non-synonymous variant that strongly alters splicing of CD33 exon  2  (Malik  et  al. 2013 ).  Rare,  likely  causal non-synonymous variants have also been discovered in TREM2 , PLCG2 and ABI3 (Guerreiro et al. 2013; Sims et al. 2017 ). Because these genes are expressed in microg -lia, these findings have pointed to a potential causal role of microglial activation in AD (Schwartzentruber et al. 2021 ).

Pathway  enrichment  analyses  have  implicated  several gene categories in AD, including: (i) genes that modulate the metabolism of the amyloid precursor protein (APP) (e.g., APH1B , ICA1L, DGKQ, ICA1, DOC2A, WDR81, LIME1 ); (ii) genes that are part of the non-amyloidogenic pathway for APP (e.g., ADAM17, TSPAN14 ); (iii) genes involved in TNF-α  signaling  pathway  (e.g., SPPL2A )  (Bellenguez  et al.  2022 ;  Schwartzentruber  et  al. 2021 ).  Notably,  several genes of the TNF-a signaling pathway are expressed at low levels in microglia (e.g., ICA1L, EGFR, RITA1, MYO15A, LIME1,  APP ),  suggesting  that  although  genes  implicated with innate immunity and microglia activation contribute to AD and related dementias (e.g., RHOH, BLNK, SIGLEC11 and LILRB2,  RASGE1FC ),  AD  pathogenesis  cannot  be attributed  solely  to  microglial  mechanisms  (Bellenguez et  al.  2022 ). These changes  are  critical,  as  synaptic  dysfunction  is  a  primary driver of cognitive decline in AD. Genetic analysis has also revealed disruptions in pathways related to energy metabolism, inflammation, myelin-axon interactions, protein mis -folding, cell proliferation and transcription factors (Blalock et al. 2004; Colangelo et al. 2002; Ginsberg et al. 2000; Hata et al. 2001 ; Lin &amp; Beal 2006; Loring et al. 2001; Miller et al. 2008 ). In parallel, genes associated with angiogenic pro -grams (e.g., EGFL7, FLT1, VWF )  and  amyloid  clearance (e.g., APOE4, LRP1, ABCG2 ), which contributes to bloodbrain barrier dysfunction and altered blood flow, have also been shown to be upregulated in AD (Lau et al. 2020 ).

These  changes  do  not  occur  throughout  the  brain  in a  uniform  manner  and  change  over  the  course  of  disease progression (R. H. Hao et al. 2024a, b ).  For example, the MAPKKK cascade and  the  transforming  growth  factor-ß signaling pathway show progressive increased expression in the hippocampus (Miller et al. 2013 ). This is not surprising, as AD follows a well-characterized evolution, with pathol -ogy beginning in brain areas involved in learning, memory, perception,  and  emotion,  such  as  the  entorhinal  cortex, amygdala,  and  hippocampus,  before  spreading  throughout  the  cortex  (Braak  et  al. 2006 ;  Braak  &amp;  Braak 1991 ). In the hippocampus, CA1 pyramidal neurons and affected earlier and more severely than CA3 pyramidal cells (Miller et  al.  2013 ).  Given  all  these  complexities,  the  emergency of  single-cell  omics  approaches  has  been  instrumental  in contextualizing these genetic risk variants in different brain regions, AD stage, and across different cell types (Morabito et al. 2021; Otero-Garcia et al. 2022; Shi et al. 2025 ).

To navigate through how AD affects different brain cells and understand how the complex crosstalk between differ -ent cell types evolves in AD, it is useful to first define what is  a  'cell  type'.  Historically,  pioneering  anatomists  and pathologists like Santiago Ramon y Cajal (1852-1934), Otto Friedrich  Karl  Deiters  (1834-1863),  Pío  del  Río-Hortega (1882-1945),  and  Rudolf  Virchow  (1821-1902)  used  the term cell type to classify cells based on their morphology, location and connectivity (Boullerne &amp; Feinstein 2020; Cajal et  al.  1995 ;  Deiters  &amp;  Guillery 2013; Masic 2019 ).  Their foundational work led to the identification of neurons, astro -cytes, oligodendrocytes, and microglial cells (all of which were considered cell types) (Boullerne &amp; Feinstein 2020; Cajal et al. 1995 ; Deiters &amp; Guillery 2013; Masic 2019 ). In the mid-twentieth century, classification schemes expanded to include information about the functional and molecular properties  of  cells  such  as  firing  pattern,  neurotransmitter identity, expression of calcium-binding proteins, receptors and transporters, and projection targets. Even then, morphological,  molecular  and  electrophysiological  properties  did not always align, indicating that no single framework can replace the value of integrating multiple sources of information.  More  recently,  high-throughput  scRNA-seq  and single-nucleus  RNA-seq  (snRNA-seq)  have  transformed cell classification by providing information on cell specific gene expression profiles, prompting efforts to integrate this knowledge with information about the anatomy, cell morphology, developmental origin, connectivity, and activity of different cells. We need to remember, however, that snRNAseq  only  recapitulates ∼ 20%-50%  of  the  cellular  tran -scriptome, and does not include extranuclear content (e.g., synaptic profiling) (Bakken et al. 2018; Berson et al. 2025 ). To standardize  this  emerging  complexity,  the Allen  Brain Institute  developed  a  hierarchical  taxonomy  and  nomenclature  system  to  standardize  the  classification  of  cells  in the  mammalian  brain,  known  as  the  Common  Cell  type Nomenclature (CCN) (Miller et al. 2020; Yao et al. 2021 ). In this taxonomy, 'cell type' is considered an informal term, while the taxonomy consists of four formal tiers, originally applied to the classification of the mouse cortex (Yao et al. 2021; Yuste et al. 2020 ) (Fig. 1 ):

- Classes: broad categories (e.g., excitatory neurons, in -hibitory neurons, glial cells)
- Subclasses:  molecularly  defined  groups  of  cells  (e.g., somatostatin or Sst interneurons)
- Supertypes: closely related clusters with common core gene expression profiles,  anatomical  location,  and  de -velopmental  lineage  (e.g., Sst-chondrolectin or SstChodl interneurons)
- Clusters: fine-resolution, computationally defined tran -scriptomic  groups  identified  based  on  subtle  gene  ex -pression differences (e.g., Sst-Chodl-1 interneurons)

Although  not  part  of  the  original  four-tier  hierarchy,  the notion of cell state has been introduced to describe contextdependent phenotypes that do not align neatly with any one cluster:

- States: cells with mixed genetic profiles that reflect dy -namic shifts due to activity, environment, development, or disease

While this classification system is powerful, it also raises challenges. Gene expression is dynamic and context-sensitive, and large transcriptomics datasets may identify thousands of clusters whose biological significance is uncertain. While some warn of the risks of oversimplifying the brain's cellular landscape by failing to account for the continuous variability  within  and  across  traditional  cell  type  categories, others caution against overcomplicating it by leaning too heavily on the fine granularity of gene expression data (Cembrowski &amp; Menon 2018; Li et al. 2020 ;  Luecken &amp; Theis 2019; Yu et al. 2022 ). Indeed, whether these myriad new profiles represent functionally distinct cells or simply represent known cell clusters in different functional states remains an open question that may need to be answered on a case-by-case basis. A related concern is that if cell identity is defined solely by gene expression, the downregula -tion  or  disappearance  of  key  cellular  markers  could  be misinterpreted as cell loss rather than a transcriptional shift (i.e., a state change). One could say that whereas the field once focused on making sense of the brain, we now often find ourselves trying to make sense of our own datasets, at times losing sight of whether these efforts will ultimately advance our understanding of the brain's core mysteries. As we continue to grapple with this challenge, it is important to  strike  a  balance  between  data  richness  and  biological interpretability.

Establishing  general  criteria  for  cell  classification  is particularly  challenging  when trying to define operational principles  that  allow  making  comparisons  across  species (Bakken et al. 2021 ). The human brain contains ~ 86 billion neurons, nearly a thousand times more than the ~ 70 million found in the mouse brain, leading to a markedly higher neuronal density (Herculano-Houzel 2009 ;  Herculano-Houzel et  al.  2006 ).  The  number  of  glial  cells  (astrocytes,  oligo -dendrocytes, microglia) is roughly equivalent to the number of neurons in the human brain, although the density varies significantly across different brain regions (Herculano-Hou -zel 2014 ). In contrast, the mouse brain has a lower glia-toneuron ratio, with glial cells comprising about one-third of neurons. Astrocytes, for instance, make up 20-40% of glial cells in the human brain and ~ 20% in rodents (Verkhratsky et  al.  2017 ).  Oligodendrocytes,  particularly  enriched  in white matter, account for 20-40% of glial cells in humans and 10-20% in mice. This difference may partly reflect the higher white-to-gray matter ratio in the human brain (Pelvig et al. 2008 ). Microglia, an especially relevant glial subtype in  the  context  of AD,  are  far  less  abundant  than  neurons in  both  species,  with  neuron-to-microglia  ratios  ranging from 0.17 to  0.3  in  humans  (depending  on  brain  region), and as low as 0.05 in mice. Together, these differences in cell  abundance  and  classification  underscore  the  need  for caution when extrapolating results from animal models to humans, especially in the study of complex brain disorders, where animal models may not fully recapitulate key aspects of human pathology (Dos Santos et al. 2020 ).

<!-- image -->

To navigate these complexities, in this review, we focus on  human  data  and  adopt  a  set  of  working  definitions  to guide  our  discussion.  While  we  use  the  CCN  taxonomy framework, we also restrict our analysis to a subset of major cell classes. Clearly this approach may not be exhaustive, but it provides a practical and interpretable framework for the present discussion. Having established the rationale for navigating  through  human-specific  data,  we  now  turn  to how these frameworks intersect with molecular alterations revealed through brain-wide transcriptomic profiling in AD.

### Brain-Wide Profiling of the Human Alzheimer's Disease Brain

Recent transcriptomic studies have enabled brain-wide profiling of gene expression changes in AD, revealing molecu -lar  signatures  that  may  underlie  the  cellular  and  regional vulnerability  observed  in  the  disease.  AD  affects  every major  cell  class  in  the  brain.  With  human  AD  transcriptomics  data  now  available  (Bertram  et  al. 2007;  Lambert et al. 2013 ),  analyses of cell class enrichments of suscep -tibility genes across brain regions have begun to yield key insights (Skene &amp; Grant 2016 ).  For  example, it is  widely accepted  that  most  differentially  enriched  genes  (DEGs) affect specific cell classes in AD, with only very few over -laps among different ones (Saura et al. 2023 ). From this the current  literature  supports  three  main  conclusions. First , interneurons  and  pyramidal  neurons  show  enrichment  for susceptibility genes that are down-regulated in AD. Second , astrocytes,  oligodendrocytes,  microglial  and  endothelial cells are enriched with susceptibility genes that are upregulated in AD. Third , among the five major cell classes (excit -atory and inhibitory neurons, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes and microglia), microglia exhibit the largest enrichment for upregulated AD  susceptibility  genes  (Mhatre  et  al. 2015; Skene &amp; Grant 2016 ).  These  findings  are  consistent  with key  pathological  features  of AD,  which  include  neuronal loss, astrogliosis, neurovascular dysfunction, inflammation and  endothelial  activation  (Grammas 2011;  Mhatre  et  al. 2015 ; Wyss-Coray &amp; Rogers 2012 ).

In a brain region like the hippocampus, important in the context of AD, bulk transcriptomics, gene co-expression and transcriptome-wide  association  studies  have  identified  24 genes that are  related  to  amyloid  processing,  tau  phosphorylation, neuronal apoptosis, neurogenesis, chaperone-mediated autophagy, transcription and telomerase regulation (Brokaw et al. 2020; Galea et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2021; Morabito et al. 2020; Mostafavi et al. 2018; Otero-Garcia et al. 2022; Patir et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2016; Zhang et al. 2013 ). Additional insights into DEGs in AD came from a combined bulk and scRNA-seq analysis of 230 human dorsolateral pre-frontal cortex (PFC) samples from 129 individuals with LOAD and 101 non-demented healthy controls, using tissue obtained from the Harvard Brain Tissue Resource Center (Williams et  al.  2021 ).  Gene  Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of these  data  showed  that  the  most  upregulated  pathways  in AD  were  those  involved  in  immune  response  activation via  cytokine-mediated  signaling,  cell  adhesion  and  structural organization, and cell death (Williams et al. 2021 ). By contrast, the most prominently downregulated genes were associated  with  synaptic  signaling,  synapse  organization, ion  transport,  neurotransmitter  secretion  and  glutamatergic synaptic transmission (Williams et al. 2021 ).  Notably, genes expressed in presynaptic terminals, particularly synaptic  vesicle-related  genes,  showed  some  of  the  greatest reductions. Hub gene analysis identified genes critical for synaptic vesicle exo- and endo-cytosis, glutamatergic and GABAergic transmission, and genes required for calcium binding to synaptic vesicles and the formation of the SNARE complex and synaptic vesicle fusion with the plasma membrane (e.g., SYT1, SNAP-25 , STXBP1 and VAMP2 ). The hub analysis also showed that the downregulated genes encode postsynaptic receptors, transporters and enzymes mediating glutamatergic  and  GABAergic  transmission  in  AD  (e.g., GRIA1, GRIA2, GRIN2A, SLC17A6 and GAD2, GABRB2 , SLC6A1 ) (Williams et al. 2021 ). These transcriptomics data suggest  a  specific  reduction  in  presynaptic  gene  expres -sion  in  glutamatergic  and  GABAergic neurons in LOAD. Importantly,  most  of  these  transcript-level  trends  are  also confirmed at the protein level (Wingo et al. 2019 ). Many of the proteins that are downregulated in LOAD are important to ensure cognitive function, suggesting that they could be important targets for disease prevention (Wingo et al. 2019 ). Once again, this pattern is not observed uniformly across the brain. For example, inhibitory neurons in the entorhinal cortex  and  superior  frontal  gyrus  do  not  show  significant changes in gene expression (Leng et al. 2021 ).

These  altered  gene  expression  profiles  in  specific  cell classes change depending on pathology severity, cognitive status, and sex, which suggests the existence of a complex relationship  between AD  progression  and  the  representation  of  specific  cell  classes  and  subclasses  across  brain regions.  This  underscores  an  important  point:  that AD  is not  a  uniform  disease  process  at  the  molecular  level  but rather involves cell type- and region-specific transcriptomic alterations that evolve with disease severity and vary across individuals. To capture a more encompassing spectrum of AD pathological processes, it is therefore important to use multimodal  approaches.  Tailored therapeutic strategies, informed by multimodal studies, may also be necessary to address  the  diverse  molecular  and  cellular  trajectories  of different patients.

Most cases of AD, especially in cases older than 75 years, exhibit other co-morbid pathologies in addition to the characteristic distribution of plaques and tangles found in AD ("2024 Alzheimer's disease facts and figures," 2024). Most commonly  found  co-morbid  pathologies  include  cerebrovascular lesions and Lewy body disease, which may overlap with AD lesions (Jellinger 2013 ). The broad spectrum of co-morbidities associated with AD may add another layer of complexity in our quest for novel tools to prevent disease progression, as lesion specific and interaction effects may come into play in such cases. While therapeutic strategies may need to be personalized for AD patients, the integra -tion of findings across multimodal studies can help identify reproducible molecular signatures that are consistent across individuals.  These  shared  features  have  the  potential  to serve as biomarkers for early diagnosis and guide the refine -ment  of  animal  models  to  better  recapitulate  the  diverse pathological hallmarks of AD. Ultimately, the convergence of multimodal evidence will be instrumental in advancing our understanding of disease onset, progression, and treatment responsiveness.

### The Transcriptome of AD Excitatory and Inhibitory Neurons

AD is  associated  with  disruption  in  the  balance  between neuronal excitation and inhibition. Loss of Sst interneurons occurs during the early stages of AD and is correlated with a decline in episodic and semantic memory, and perceptual orientation (Gabitto et al. 2024; Mathys et al. 2023 ). Broad but neuron-specific changes in AD include gene expression changes  related  to  calcium  signaling,  ubiquitination  and mitochondrial function (Morabito et al. 2020 ). For example, there  are  subclusters  of  excitatory  and  inhibitory  neurons that are enriched for autophagy and responses to hormones, lipids,  and  topologically  incorrect  proteins  (Grubman  et al. 2019 ). In recent years, detailed analyses have begun to uncover how AD differentially affects excitatory and inhibi -tory  neurons  at  the  transcriptomic  level  (Fig. 2 ).  This  is potentially very important, as specific cell signatures in dif -ferent cell populations can point to more precise molecular processes  and  targets  for  therapeutic  intervention.  Excitatory neurons are especially vulnerable in AD and show dramatic changes in their gene expression profile. According to multiple studies, among the most upregulated genes in these cells are those involved in protein folding, autophagy, apoptosis  and  stress  response  pathways (Grubman et al. 2019; Lau et al. 2020; Mathys et al. 2019; Morabito et al. 2020 ). Among the top DEGs is LINGO1 , which encodes a signaling protein that inhibits myelination. In contrast, genes related to  axonal  outgrowth,  regeneration  and  myelination  and amyloid fibril formation are notably downregulated in PFC excitatory but not inhibitory neurons (Grubman et al. 2019; Mathys et al. 2019; Morabito et al. 2020 ). These results are generally consistent with those presented in a comparative analysis  of  DEGs in cortex samples from AD individuals and normal controls (Wang &amp; Li 2021 ).. Downregulation of genes implicated with synaptic transmission (e.g., SNAP25 , RIMS1 ) also occurs in excitatory neurons (Grubman et al. 2019 ).

Inhibitory  neurons,  on  the  other  hand,  show  upregulation of genes associated with the ERBB2 signaling, glucose transport,  mRNA  processing  regulation  and  neuronal  cell death. Genes involved in synapse organization and signal -ing, cell adhesion, post-translational protein modifications (e.g.,  phosphorylation,  protein  cleavages,  ubiquitination, nitrosylation)  and  membrane repolarization (e.g.,  voltagegated potassium channels) are downregulated in these cells (Grubman et al. 2019 ). Examples include CCK, SST, RELN, VIP, and KCNIP4 (Grubman et al. 2019 ).

At present, one thing that remains unclear is how these changes  in  gene  expression  affect  specific  subclasses  of excitatory  and  inhibitory  neurons  (Saura  et  al. 2023 ).  In addition,  there  are  indications  that  some  DEGs  could  be accounted for by individual or sex differences (Grubman et al. 2019 ).

Therefore,  on  one  hand  we  appreciate  that  neuronal vulnerability in AD is not uniform, but shaped by distinct, cell type-specific gene regulatory programs, indicating that precise cell type characterization will be critical for under -standing disease mechanisms  and  developing targeted interventions.  On  the  other  hand,  future  studies  on  larger patient cohorts may help us advance our understanding of the relative contribution of specific genetic factors to differ -ent aspects of AD pathology.

### Gene Expression Changes in AD Astrocytes

A few works show that AD is associated with changes in gene  expression  in  astrocytes.  In  the  PFC,  for  example, astrocytes  show  increased  expression  of  genes  implicated with  stress  responses  ( CRYAB, GFAP, LINGO1 )  and  syn -aptic assembly, coupled to downregulation of genes implicated  with  glial  differentiation,  glutamate  transport  and metabolism  ( SLC1A2,  GLUL )  (Lau  et  al. 2020;  Mathys et  al.  2019 ).  In  the  entorhinal  and  somatosensory cortices there  is  an  increased  expression  of  genes  associated  with proteostasis,  mitochondria  oxidation,  inflammation,  glial cell differentiation  and  myelination  pathways,  coupled to  decreased  glutamate  transport  and  synapse  remodeling  (Grubman  et  al. 2019;  Leng  et  al.  2021;  Smith  et  al. 2022 ). Some of the genes that show increased expression in astrocytes, also show increased expression in neurons and oligodendrocytes  (e.g., BIN1 , LINGO1 and NEAT1 ).  By contrast, APOE ,  a  major genetic risk factor for LOAD, is downregulated in AD astrocytes, oligodendrocytes and oligodendrocytes precursor cells  (OPCs),  but  upregulated  in microglia (Grubman et al. 2019; Mathys et al. 2019; Saura et  al.  2023 ).  One  potential  caveat  of  these  studies  is  that astrocytes were under-represented, making up only 3-18% of  all  cell  nuclei. This  raised  the  concern  that  some  studies  may have missed lowly abundant but biologically relevant astrocyte subclasses (Del-Aguila et al. 2019; Gerrits et al. 2021; Grubman et al. 2019; Lau et al. 2020; Leng et al.  2021;  Mathys et al. 2019; Zhou et al. 2020 ).  To  over -come this potential limitation a later study used snRNA-seq datasets  to  characterize  astrocytes  and  oligodendrocytes from  human  postmortem  PFC  from AD  patients  carrying the APOE ε2/3 allele (abundantly expressed in astrocytes), and  age-matched  non-symptomatic  (NS)  patients  (Sadick et  al.  2022 ).  This  work  also  re-analyzed  and  compared three  prior  publications  (Grubman  et  al. 2019;  Mathys  et al. 2019; Zhou et al. 2020 ). This led to the identification of nine astrocyte subclasses with unique transcriptomic signa -tures (Sadick et al. 2022 ), with some level of overlap with prior works (Grubman et al. 2019; Mathys et al. 2019; Zhou et  al.  2020 ). Across all clusters, AD astrocytes upregulate transcripts  including HPSE2,  SLC39A11,  PFKP,  NEAT1, RANBP3L,  PLPP1 , PLCG2 and  downregulate  transcripts including SLC14A1, C1ORF61, CIRBP ,  and SAT1 .  Other changes  affect  only  some  astrocyte  subclasses  (Table 1 ). As  in  the  case  of  neurons,  astrocytic  involvement  in AD is highly heterogeneous, with shared and cell type-specific transcriptomic alterations that may differentially contribute to  disease  progression.  Integrative  analyses  across  datasets,  like  the  one  used in this study, are therefore essential to make sense of transcriptomic data coming from different laboratories,  to  generate  a  broader,  mechanistic  and  more informative  consensus  on  the  pathogenesis  and  clinical progression of AD. Since astrocytes appear to be the major cell mediators of AD resilience (i.e., the ability to preserve cognitive function despite significant AD pathology) across cerebral  regions,  the  development  of  astrocyte-targeted therapies  is  an  option  that  continues  to  draw  attention  to treat AD pathology (Berson et al. 2025 ).

### More Than Genes: Accounting for Functional and Spatial Context of Transcriptomic Data

Transcriptomics  provides  a  scalable  approach  to  classify neuronal and non-neuronal cells and capture cellular diversity  (Gouwens  et  al. 2020 ).  However,  a  key  challenge  is that  distinct  transcriptomic  profiles  do  not  always  corre -spond  to  distinct  structural  and  functional  characteristics. For example, in many cases: (i) mRNA abundance does not correlate with protein levels due to post-transcriptional and post-translational  modifications; (ii) gene  expression  does not measure protein activity, localization, and modifications (Liu et al. 2016 ; Vogel &amp; Marcotte 2012 ). Moreover, bulk and scRNA-seq disrupt tissue architecture, and do not mea -sure RNA in specific sub-cellular compartments (e.g., den -drites,  axons),  making  it  difficult  to  determine  the  spatial distribution of different transcripts within cells. Epigenomic profiling  (e.g.,  snATAC-seq,  methylome)  reveals  changes in chromatin accessibility, enhancer-promoter interactions, and DNA methylation that regulate gene expression and help link GWAS risk variants to specific cell types and transcriptional  programs  (Liu  et  al. 2024 ).  Spatial  tran -scriptomic  techniques,  instead,  retain  anatomical  context, but often at the cost of reduced throughput and resolution. Accordingly, the current methods for spatial transcriptomics (e.g., 10  ×  genomics, MERFISH) vary in spatial resolution and  molecular  sensitivity,  which  can  constrain  the  detection of low-abundance transcripts and fine subcellular fea -tures  (Asp  et  al. 2020;  Larsson  et  al.  2021;  Longo  et  al. 2021 ). When applying these tools to post mortem samples, it  is  important to remember that RNA degradation in post mortem tissue remains a significant challenge, often reduc -ing data quality and interpretability. With these caveats in mind, we can appreciate how the continued optimization of sample preservation, molecular capture efficiency, and ana -lytical pipelines is essential to overcome these barriers. It is also worth noting that methods that rely chiefly on gene expression may lead to the identification of transcriptional profiles that may simply reflect different cell states (or even traits )  rather  than  different  subclasses  (Mulas  et  al. 2021; Yuste et al. 2020; Zeng 2022 ). To resolve this complexity integrating transcriptomic data with protein-level and functional analyses is essential (Cardona-Alberich et al. 2021; Martini et al. 2024; McFarlan et al. 2023 ). Proteomic analy -sis  complements  transcriptomic  data  by  capturing  protein abundance  and  localization.  Functional  studies  in  living tissue,  capture  the  effects  that  different  levels  of  protein expression  and  localization  have  on  the  signaling  proper -ties  of  different  cell  types. At  present,  there  are  only  few multimodal studies that combine transcriptomic data with protein-level and functional analysis is very short and point to potentially brain region and cell-specific abnormalities in AD (see Table 4 ).  Such multimodal approaches provide a more comprehensive view of brain organization and are par -ticularly important for unraveling the cellular underpinnings of neurodegenerative diseases like AD.

Some of the ambiguities of transcriptomic classification can be resolved, at least in part, by patch-clamp sequenc -ing  (Patch-seq),  a  technique  that  combines  whole-cell patch-clamp  recordings  with  scRNA-seq.  Adapted  from traditional  electrophysiology  protocols,  Patch-seq  enables the  sequencing of mRNA from a cell that has been func -tionally characterized, allowing direct correlations between gene expression and electrophysiological properties. When biocytin is added to the patch pipette solution, one can also reconstruct the morphology of the patched cell, providing a  multidimensional  profile  that  links  transcriptomic  iden -tity  with  physiological  and  anatomical  features  (Cadwell et al. 2016; Foldy et al. 2016; Lipovsek et al. 2021 ). Ana -lytical tools to analyze heterogeneity in neuronal excitabil -ity  states  in  the  context  of  multimodal  datasets  continue to evolve (Martini et al. 2024 ).  Meanwhile, Patch-seq has been applied to classify neurons in the human brain, particularly the middle temporal gyrus (MTG), the brain region that is most accessible during neurosurgeries. For example, Patch-seq analysis of layer 2-3 glutamatergic supra-gran -ular  neurons  has  shown  that  these  cells  are  comprised  of 5  transcriptomic  types  (t-types: LTK,  GLP2R,  FREM3, CARM1P1 and COL22A1 ).  These  t-types  differ  for  their dendritic size, relative apical versus basal lengths, passive, single action potential and sustained firing properties (Berg et  al.  2021 ).  Among  them, FREM3 t-type  glutamatergic neurons  are  particularly  noteworthy,  because  their  transcriptomic profile varies with the depth of their soma within layers  2-3.  Deeper FREM3 cells  exhibit  a  larger  voltage sag, greater action potential upstroke/downstroke ratio, and shorter action potential latency to spike at rheobase (Berg et al. 2021 ). These findings indicate that, at least for FREM3 cells, transcriptomic identity can robustly predict the location, electrophysiological and morphological properties of a cell. In other t-types, however, such clear correspondence is less evident.

The Seattle AD Brain Cell Atlas (SEA-AD) consortium has  used  a  multimodal  approach  to  profile  AD  progres -sion based on the analysis of MTG samples from a cohort of 84 aged donors spanning the spectrum of AD pathology (Gabitto et al. 2024 ) (see Table 5 for accession numbers). This analysis captured two main phases in AD progression: (i) an  early  phase  with  slowly  increasing  neuropathology and (ii) a late phase with exponentially increasing neuropathology. The early phase was characterized by loss of SST interneurons in superficial cortical layers of the MTG (asso -ciated  with  increased  amplitude  of  the  hyperpolarization activated mixed cationic current I h and increased expression of  the  HCN1  channel,  loss  of  oligodendrocytes  and  OPC proliferation and differentiation (Gabitto et al. 2024 ). In the later  phase, there is a loss of Layer 2/3 intratelencephalic (L2/3IT)  neurons, PVALB and VIP interneurons,  associated with increases in inflammatory microglia and astrocyte states  and  a  decreased  expression  of  the  OPC  differentia -tion  program  and  expression  of  oligodendrocyte  myelinassociated proteins (Gabitto et al. 2024 ). The early loss of SST interneurons  could  alter  coordinated  excitation  and inhibition of the superficial cortical layers, while the early microglia activation could trigger reactive astrogliosis and oligodendrocyte loss (Gabitto et al. 2024; Liddelow et al. 2020 ). As it often happens with brain human samples, these tissues often come from patients with multiple comorbidities, for example micro-infarcts, macro-infarcts and cerebral angiopathy and ischemia or hypoxia induced by these events can lead to widespread gene expression changes, metabolic dysregulations and changes in the representation of different cell  subclasses (Kapasi &amp; Schneider 2016; Montine et al. 2012; Schneider et al. 2007 ).

Despite its numerous strengths, Patch-seq remains laborintensive, low-throughput, and technically demanding, making it difficult to scale for whole-brain or cross-species comparisons. For example, most human brains are collected post mortem and remain viable for only about 12 h, limiting the number of high-quality recordings per brain (Beau -lieu-Laroche et al.  2018;  Gidon  et  al.  2020;  Kalmbach  et al.  2018 ;  Szabadics et al. 2006 ).  Other challenges include RNA  degradation,  the  technical  difficulty  of  amplifying small RNA quantities, and the risk of contamination during sample  collection.  While  appropriate  control  experiments can help mitigate these concerns, these factors can affect the Accession numbers for transcriptomic datasets deposited or used by the manuscripts cited in this work reliability and interpretability of scRNA-seq transcriptomes obtained via Patch-seq (Tripathy et al. 2018 ). Another con -sideration is that whole-cell patch-clamp recordings result in dialysis of the cellular cytoplasm, which in some cases has been shown to alter the detected functional properties of recorded cells. This effect can be avoided using perfo -rated patch-clamp recordings (Lahiri &amp; Bevan 2020; Lamsa et  al.  2007 ).  From  an  electrophysiological  standpoint,  it is  also  important  to  note  that  the  cellular  function  is  not defined solely by passive and active membrane properties of a cell (e.g., resting membrane potential, firing pattern), the  parameters  commonly  included  in  Patch-seq  analysis, but  also  by  its  ability  to  integrate  excitatory,  inhibitory and  modulatory  inputs  and  to  undergo  activity-dependent changes in synaptic plasticity. These properties, in addition to  gene  expression,  cell  morphology,  and  electrical  excitability, should be included as part of the classification sys -tem (Fig. 1 ).

To  achieve  meaningful  integration  of  transcriptomic, morphological, and electrophysiological data, several computational frameworks, more traditional and deep learningbased, are currently being employed (Lotfollahi et al. 2024 ). For example, non-deep learning tools like Seurat v5 supports cross-modality mapping by using a multi-omic dataset  as  a  'bridge',  transforming  multi-omic datasets into a shared feature space without making biological assumptions (Y. Hao et al. 2024a, b ). StabMap performs cross-modality mapping by identifying the shortest paths across a mosaic single-cell data topology (Ghazanfar et al. 2024 ). Harmony also  provides  efficient  batch  correction  and  integration across  diverse  modalities.  However,  these  algorithms  can encounter scalability issues with very large datasets, making  analysis  of  multi-modal  information  computationally challenging (Lotfollahi et al. 2024 ). To address these limita -tions,  deep  learning-based  latent  space  mapping  methods, which represent high-dimensional gene expression data in a compressed, lower-dimensional 'latent space', offer pow -erful  new  ways  to  capture  shared  representations  across molecular and physiological domains, enabling a more unified understanding of neuronal identity and function. Even with these tools at hand, however, reference mapping datasets from diseased samples using cell atlases from healthy individuals comes with distinct challenges for robust detection of compositional or cell-state alterations, for example if  diseased  sample  display  variance  that  is  not  evident  in healthy references (Dann et al. 2023; Lotfollahi et al. 2024 ).

Finally,  it  is  important  to  acknowledge  that  part  of  the apparent cellular diversity observed in transcriptomic studies may stem from differences in analytical and statistical pipelines, rather than biological reality. As we aim to characterize cell census changes in AD, our goal is to identify robust  consensus  patterns  across  transcriptomic  datasets, while being mindful of methodological variability.

### Humans vs Mice: How Much Does Human Data Matter to Generate Better Mouse Models of AD?

The degree to which transcriptomic data from mouse models of AD recapitulate the human condition has been a major focus of recent studies (Conway et al. 2018; Cummings et al. 2015; Gjoneska et al. 2015; Grubman et al. 2019; Matarin et al. 2015; Mathys et al. 2019; Mostafavi et al. 2018; Zhang et al. 2013 ). First, at  the  transcriptome level, most AD mouse models show limited correspondence to human AD, particularly in pathways related to neuronal dysfunction,  synaptic  loss,  and  disease  progression  (Wan  et  al. 2020 ).  When  similarities  exist,  they  are  typically  limited to  genes  enriched  for  microglia  activation  and  neuronal stress,  which  may  reflect  shared  neurodegenerative  pro -cesses  rather  than  disease-specific  mechanisms.  Interest -ingly,  certain  non-AD mouse models also show that gene expression overlaps with human AD. For example, mouse models of familial encephalopathy with Neuroserpin inclusion bodies display transcriptional similarities to human AD brains, perhaps because they both involve protein aggregation  and  neurodegeneration  (Wan  et  al. 2020 ).  This  sug -gests  that  some  of  the  molecular  signatures  observed  in AD may reflect general responses to neuronal injury, rather than unique features of amyloid or tau pathology. Second, the  transcriptome-proteome  correlations  are  modest,  with R = 0.40 in humans and R = 0.46 in mice (Yarbro et al. 2024 ). The analysis of 5xFAD and APP NLGF mouse models of AD has shown a substantial overlap for differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) in these models, with 610 proteins. How -ever, when examining the proteomic profile across five AD mouse models (5xFAD Eimer &amp; Vassar 2013; Oakley et al. 2006 ), APP knock in mice including APP NLF and APP NLGF mice (Saito et al. 2014 ), 3xTG (Oddo et al. 2003 ) and BiG mice (Chen et al. 2022 )), each model shared &lt; 25% of DEPs with human AD datasets. Moreover, the proteomic correlation between mice and humans is typically modest, reported as R = 0.32 in 5xFAD and R = 0.23 in APP NLGF mice (Yarbro et al. 2024 ). In APP and MAPT mouse models, only a small subset  of  genes  within  neuronal  and  microglial-enriched co-expression  modules  show  concordant  transcriptional changes between mice and humans (i.e., both up- or downregulated). However, these concordant changes are not spe -cific to  AD pathology (Wan et al. 2020 ). More recent analysis have highlighted the SST gene, whose role in AD pathology appears  to  be  conserved  across  species,  shows  consistent alterations in 5xFAD, APP/PS1, and humanized amyloid-β (hAβ)  knock-in  mice,  as  well  as  in  EOAD  and  LOAD patients (De Bastiani et al. 2024 ). In contrast, ZNF461 and FOXC2 ,  which  encode  transcription  factors,  are  altered only  in  hAβ  knock-in  models  and  in  the  hippocampus  of LOAD individuals (De Bastiani et al. 2024 ). Additionally, SOX9 and PARK2 , both encoding transcription factors, are consistently activated in both mouse models and human AD (De Bastiani et al. 2024 ). Importantly, these findings do not imply  that  the  observed  transcriptomic  changes  are  specific to AD, which remains another major challenge in the field. Third , comparisons between the brains of human AD patients and those of mouse AD models often involve comparing aged human brains and predominantly middle-aged rodent brains, which may introduce age-related confounds and challenge the validity of these comparisons.

Together,  these  findings  highlight  a  critical  caveat  in interpreting data from mouse models: although animal mod -els  remain valuable resources for mechanistic studies and hypothesis testing, their translational relevance is limited. The poor predictive  power  of  many  preclinical AD  models  in  therapeutic  development may stem, at least in part, from this fundamental transcriptomic mismatch. In an ideal world, mouse models-which allow for controlled experimental  manipulations-should  recapitulate  the  molecular complexity of human AD and be specifically and reliably predictive  of  human  disease  pathology.  However,  inherent species differences remain a major barrier to accurately modeling AD in mice. For example,  most  mouse  models have  variable  degrees  of  neuronal  loss,  whereas  substantial neurodegeneration is a defining hallmark of human AD (Yarbro et al. 2024 ). A different approach that we decided to use is relying on AAV models, that are not intended to serve as models of AD, but allow us to isolate the effects of pathogenic proteins implicated with AD (e.g., Aβ 42 ) without altering the endogenous expression of genes such as APP or PSEN1/PSEN2 (Wehrle et al. 2025 ).

Moving  forward,  refining animal models  based  on human-derived  molecular  signatures,  and  incorporating humanized or chimeric systems, may provide better align -ment between model systems and the human disease. Furthermore,  the  systematic  use  of  standardized  metrics  of cross-species concordance, such as transcriptomic correlation coefficients (e.g., R, R 2 ), pathway or module overlap, and enrichment statistics, would provide a more quantitative framework for evaluating how well mouse models reflect human AD pathology. Integrating such metrics across studies would help to harmonize findings, enable more objective comparison  across  models  and  disease  stages,  and  guide the  development  of  next-generation  mouse  models  with improved translational value.

### Conclusions

The recent advances in transcriptomic profiling underscore the growing need for integrated, multimodal approaches to understand the cellular basis of AD. While different tran -scriptomics  approaches  have  revolutionized  our  ability  to classify brain cells, their true value emerges when they are interpreted  together  with  anatomical,  physiological,  and translational  frameworks.  As  the  field  progresses,  a  sus -tained  focus  on  analytical  rigor,  cross-species  validation, context-aware  interpretation,  and  integration  of  different datasets will be essential to avoid generating sparse information and bridge the gap between molecular profiles and disease mechanisms, ultimately enabling the development of  better  models  and  treatments  for AD. A  key  challenge will be determining whether the molecular changes detected through multimodal analyses are drivers or consequences of AD pathology, and identifying molecular features that confer resistance to AD. Therefore, advancing from descriptive to correlative and causal studies has become imperative to ensure  that  findings  are  biologically  meaningful,  transla -tionally relevant, and useful for identifying biomarkers and refining animal models. With these considerations in place, we believe the future is cautiously bright, with the potential to yield transformative insights into AD and open promising new avenues for its treatment. Perhaps acquiring mul -timodal 'big data' will become easier and more affordable, but making sense of it will continue to be priceless.
