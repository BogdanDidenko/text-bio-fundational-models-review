### Abstract

In recent years, the rapid advancement of high-throughput technologies has led to the generation of vast and complex multi-omics datasets that are valuable for characterizing and understanding complex cell signaling network systems. On the other hand, large language models (LLMs), domain-specific foundation models (FMs) and AI agents, have achieved significant breakthroughs and have been revolutionizing scientific research. The convergence of these two trends is catalyzing a new era for biomedical research to augment and speed up scientific discovery and the development of precision medicine. In this study, we examine the largescale omics datasets, emerging applications, and challenges at the intersection of massive omic datasets and related AI models and agents, highlighting how their integration is reshaping the landscape of biomedical research and precision medicine.

### Key Words

Biomedical Research, Systems Biology, Multi-Omics, Precision Medicine, Signaling Network, Large Language Models, Foundation Models, Agentic AI, AI Agents.

### Introduction

The recent and continued advances and breakthroughs in AI foundation models, e.g., large language models (LLMs), agentic AI and AI agents, domainspecific foundation models, are revolutionizing and transforming scientific data analysis and scientific knowledge discovery. In AI4Science, AI is not just our tool, AI is our collaborator for scientific discovery. AI models are becoming comparable with human scientists in many aspects of the scientific discovery process, such as automated data retrieval, coding for data analysis, large-scale literature search and knowledge synthesis, and hypothesis or idea generation. 1-8

In life science, omics-data driven mechanism discovery and precision medicine studies represent the most fundamental and challenging barriers to both the theoretical and practical applications of AI, considering that there is a lack of effective or curative therapies for most diseases, and the incomplete understanding of the complex and large-scale biological and disease causal molecular signaling systems. In recent years, large-scale and massive omics datasets of diseases have been generated and publicly accessible to characterize and profile the dysfunctional biological processes in diseases from multi- level views measured by using multi-omics data, like genetics, epigenetics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolic datasets. Then, the meeting and convergence of LLMs, AI agents, foundation models and the massive omics datasets are speeding up scientific discovery in AI for precision medicine (AI4PM). Human scientists are using and collaborating with AI agents and foundation models in biomedical research and precision medicine studies (Figure 1), to address fundamental challenges in the field by elucidating disease pathogenesis and guiding the development of effective and curative medications and therapies.

<!-- image -->

However, AI4PM is still in its early stages. There are some fundamental challenges to make significant breakthroughs. Specifically, it remains challenging to curate, annotate, and format the massive, heterogeneous, multi-level datasets into biologically meaning and AI-ready data structures to facilitate the development of AI foundation models. 3,9 The success of LLMs is based on the transformer architectures and the civilization-level human-understandable language/text datasets. Unlike human-defined grammars for using words in a language-meaningful manner, it is unclear how to organize multiomics data in a biologically meaningful manner (data structure) to decode the complex signaling systems of cells to diseases. Moreover, there is limited label/annotation data, e.g., disease causal mechanisms, effective therapeutic targets, effective drugs, and synergistic cocktails, associated with the multi-omics data. Therefore, it is limited to directly applying the transformer-based language or numeric models to the omics data for novel scientific discoveries in precision medicine. In other words, novel hypothesis generation or scientific discovery in precision medicine remains an open process and represents one of the most fundamental challenges in AI4PM. Though some AI models bring fundamental breakthroughs, 1-3 novel theoretic and application innovations in AI are urgently needed to solve these challenges. This study reviews the availability and resources of large-scale omics datasets, as the key foundation for developing novel AI models, and reported domain specific AI foundation models, agentic AI and AI agents, as well as the fundamental challenges.

### Massive Omics Datasets Are Publicly Accessible

Along with the advancement of next generation sequencing (NGS) technology, massive omics datasets of diverse diseases have been generated and publicly accessible. These large-scale omics datasets are valuable and essential for the development of novel and transformative AI models in the field of life science and precision medicine to understand pathogenesis and mechanisms of diseases, identifying novel therapeutic targets to guide the development of effective medications in precision medicine. However, it remains a challenging task and open problem to integrate and interpret the complex and heterogeneous massive datasets to mine knowledge embedded in these datasets. Though the success of foundation models (FMs) in language and computer vision, novel and transformative AI foundation models are urgently needed for analyzing and mining these datasets to mine the key disease targets, signaling pathways and effective medications for diverse diseases.

### Tissue-Level Omics Datasets

Among diseases, cancer is one of the leading causes of death worldwide. To investigate the complex pathogenesis and molecular mechanisms of cancer for the development of precision medications, large-scale omics datasets have been generated. For example, in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) project, 10,11 multi-omics, i.e., genetic, epigenetic, transcriptomic, and proteomic data of a large number of cancer samples from about 33 cancer types from different organs have been generated, which are accessible via the Genomic Data Commons Data Portal. 12 Analysis of these valuable datasets have identified numerous cancer specific targets and pathways. 10 In addition, the complete set of multi-omics data of 1,000 cancer cell lines were generated in the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) project. 13 Different from human omics data, the omics data of cancer cell lines can be paired with rich and massive perturbation data of these cancer cell lines. The integration of multi-omics data and massive perturbation data are valuable to uncover the vulnerable targets and understand the signaling pathways and molecular mechanisms of cancer cells, as well as drug response to given drugs and drug combinations, which are publicly accessible via cancer dependency map (DepMap) project. 14,15 To understand the mode of action (MoA) of drugs, the connectivity map (CMAP) project generated the gene signatures of thousands of drugs/compounds on a set of cancer cells, 16-18 which are widely used in drug prediction and repositioning. Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a progressive neurological disorder. In the United States (US), there are about 7.2 million people are living with Alzheimer's disease (AD) in US, 19 and the healthcare cost is 384 billion, which will increase to 1 trillion by 2050. 19 However, the underlying pathogenesis that leads to AD remain unclear.

To investigate the AD molecular mechanisms, multi-omics data of AD are generated, e.g., in the ROSMAP project, 20  which are publicly available via the synapse platform. 21  Compared with the AD cohorts, there is a rare cohort, who live exceptionally healthy and long life. The genomics profiles of the cohort are thus important to uncover the protective targets and mechanisms of aging and longevity. In the exceptional longevity (EL) program, like the Long-Life Family Study (LLFS) project, 22 have generated large-scale multi-omic datasets to identify protective biomarkers and pathways for aging and longevity. In addition to these disease specific datasets, as indicated in the recent study, 23 there are omics data of 6.5 million samples from over 200,000 studies, covering more than 6,000 organisms available in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database.

### Single Cell-Level Omics Datasets

Compared with tissue-level omics data that measures the average signals across heterogeneous cell populations, single cell omics data is necessary to identify disease specific cell-types or cellsubtypes, cell-specific signaling system patterns, and investigate signaling communications within disease MEs. 24,25 Cells are the basic structural and functional units of the human body. Though they share the same genetic blueprint, the process of differentiation transforms them into various cell types and subtypes with distinct functions, 6 which form tissues, organs, and organ systems, creating an organized and coordinated body structure.

In diseases, the dysfunction of specific cells or subpopulations will alter the healthy body or organ cellular architectures and signaling systems. For example, the immune and inflammatory signaling processes within disease microenvironments (MEs) or niche, with the signaling communications between the activated immune cells and damaged or dysfunctional non-immune cells, play important roles in many diseases. 27 The systematic chronic inflammation (SCI) is believed as one of causal factors of 50% of all deaths. 27 It is known that intermittent activated inflammation can eliminate pathogens and promote tissue repair and recovery. Whereas, SCI (complex inflammatory disorders throughout the life cycle) might cause irreversible and lethal damages of most organs in a long and chronic process overtime, e.g., Alzheimer's disease (AD), 28,29 pancreatitis, 30-32 liver fibrosis and cirrhosis, 33,34 and diabetes. 35

To investigate single-cell signaling patterns, many single cell omics datasets have been generated. For example, the Human Cell Atlas (HCA) is a global consortium, 24  with members from about 100 countries, and generates single-cell omics data to study every cell type in the human body. Currently, there are omics data of more than 70.5 million cells from 11.2 thousand donors. In the Human Tumor Atlas Network (HTAN), 25,36  omics profiles of single cells from approximately 20 organspecific cancers have been generated to investigate the evolution of tumor signaling within the tumor microenvironment (TME).

The AD Knowledge Portal hosts single cell and spatial omics datasets related to Alzheimer's disease and related dementia's for public access. 37,38 The Brain Cell Atlas unifies 26.3 million single cell omics data across studies and diseases. 39 Many of the datasets were organized in the CZ CellxGene Database repository for public access. 40 In addition, numerous disease specific single-cell datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) and other public repositories were incorporated. 41,42 With the combination of perturbation and single cell RNAseq technology, massive cell-specific perturbation transcriptomic profiles are being generated. For example, in the Arc institute's virtual cell atlas project, the Tahoe-100M (100 million single cell transcriptomic profiles) dataset was recently released to study and understand the transcriptomic variations caused by 1,100 small molecules across 50 cancer cell lines. 43 In another recent study, genome-scale pertub-seq (perturbing 12,748 genes) on 22 million primary human CD4+ T cells were generated to study the gene regulatory network (GRN) of CD4+ T cells, regulators of T cells and immune cytokines. 44 It is expected that more and more large-scale cell type specific perturbation profiles will be generated rapidly.

In omics-data driven scientific discovery, BioMedGraphica (BMG) synthesized humanunderstandable textual prior knowledge related to the entities measured in omics data, 45 which are important and should be integrated with omics data analysis for scientific discovery. Then OmniCellTOSG was reported to integrate the comprehensive textual data with a hundred million single-cell omics data using the novel Text-Numeric Graph (TNG) data structure. 7,9 Figure 2 shows a UMAP visualization of immune cells derived using the OmniCellTOSG dataset, 7,9 including macrophages, fibroblasts, B cells, and T cells, revealing their overall distribution and transcriptional separation. Figures 2b-e further show the UMAP projections of each cell type individually. These massive single cell omics datasets provide the basis for developing novel AI foundation models to understand the cell type/subtype-specific signaling patterns and uncover the complex cell components and multi-cell signaling interactions within the complex disease niches and microenvironment.

### Artificial intelligence Foundation Models

There are rich computational tools and models for single-cell RNAseq (scRNAseq) data analysis, e.g., Seurat, 46,47 Scanpy, 48 cellchat, 49 to identify cell types and subtypes (cell clustering and cell type annotation based on known cell type biomarkers), 47,50,51 uncover differentially expressed genes and enriched pathways, 47,49,52 and elucidate the multi-cell signaling communications among the diverse cell subtypes within disease-MEs. 49,53,54 However, it remains an open problem to mine knowledge and generate testable hypotheses from omics data analysis, e.g., to identify key signaling targets and core dysfunctional signaling pathways to guide the development of novel therapeutic targets, drugs and drug combinations.

In recent years, there have been continued breakthroughs in AI, which have been transforming scientific research and technological development. Specifically, the breakthroughs begin with diverse and transformative AI neural network architectures, e.g., deep neural networks (DNN), 55 convolution neural networks (CNN), 56 recurrent neural networks (RNN) with attention mechanism, 57 residual neural networks (ResNet), 58 graph convolution/neural networks (GCN/GNN), 59,60  and transformer. 61 The neural network architectures introduced new computational paradigms for solving complex tasks. Based on these model architectures, AI further evolved toward large FMs, especially the LLMs, like ChatGPT, which are pretrained on 'civilization-scale' datasets using self-supervised learning (SSL). 62,63 These models can be adapted to downstream tasks and exhibit significantly improved predictive performance and generalization capability. Then, AI agents and agentic AI (with multi-agents working together) further expand LLMs' capability to autonomously and automatically complete complex tasks, which use LLMs (as the brain for decision-making) and tools to perceive and interact with the data, internet and real world. 64,65

However, in AI for biomedical science (automatic scientific discovery), there remains a lack of effective omics data AI model architectures (for representing omics and prior knowledge), and foundation models (for understanding and modeling omics data), and agentic AI/AI agents (for autonomously and automatically discovering knowledge from omics data, and generating testable hypotheses to speed up the discovery of disease mechanisms, novel therapeutic targets, and effective drugs and synergistic cocktails). In the following sections, a set of existing AI models are surveyed, which mitigate the fundamental challenges in AI for life science and precision medicine.

### Foundation Models for Omics Data Analysis

One of the main aims of AI foundation models (FMs) is to understand the coding system and instructions embedded in DNA, and the complex cellular signaling systems, which can be partially measured using multi-omics data. 3 The understanding makes it possible to identify causal dysfunctional targets and signaling pathways and medications for disease prevention and treatment.

There are some sequence-based foundation models. For example, based on the amino acid sequence of proteins, AlphaFold revolutionizes protein structure prediction, 1 providing an architectural backbone for structure-informed reasoning. Yet it largely captures static conformations and remains limited in modeling molecular dynamics, complex assemblies, or posttranslational modifications. 66  AlphaGenome can take up to 1Mb (1 million base) DNA sequence as input and predict 11 outputs, 2 like gene expression, ATAC, histone modification and TF binding. To learn the DNA sequence patterns in genome level, Evo was developed, which can predict mutational effects on protein, 3 activities of regulatory DNA, generate new crispr-cas molecule complexes, and generate DNA sequences at genome scale. Using EVO, novel bacteriophage genomes were generated and validated. 67

Using single cell omics data, some foundation models have emerged pretrained on roughly 30-100 million cells to learn transferable representations for cell-type annotation, perturbation prediction, pathway activity inference, and cross-dataset integration. 24

For example, scBERT, 68 introduced a transformer encoder trained on large-scale single- cell data to enable cross-tissue and cross-species transfer learning. Geneformer pioneered maskedlanguage pre-training on single-cell expression profiles, 69 learning gene-gene attention maps that capture biologically meaningful relationships. The Zero-shot benchmarking analyses revealed that, 70 while capturing biologically relevant gene-gene relationships, Geneformer underperformed on crossdataset and cross-cell-type generalization tasks. The scGPT frames single-cell learning as a generative pretraining task. 71 leveraging a transformer trained on over 30 million cells. It treated genes and cells in an analogous manner to tokens and sentences, enabling the model to learn gene and cell embeddings that enhance integration and downstream transfer. scFoundation extended the foundation-model concept to over 50 million cells, 72 employing a read-depth-aware masked reconstruction objective built on xTrimoGene. 73 This scaling enhanced model versatility but came with greater computational demands. However, recent benchmarking analyses revealed that even with large-scale pretraining, foundation models are still limited for some analysis tasks, like the post-perturbation RNA-seq prediction, showing limited generalization across perturbations and cell types as well as sensitivity to batch heterogeneity. 74 To learn the signaling interaction patterns based on the largescale signaling pathways and protein-protein interaction networks, OmniCellTOSG explores a graph foundation model using the text-omic-signalinggraph (TOSG) of massive single cells. 9

To capture the spatial-dependence information embedded in spatial transcriptomics, large-scale spatial omics corpora have been assembled to train foundation models for spatial transcriptomics. For example, the NicheFormer is pretrained on SpatialCorpus110M, 75  which consists of CosMx, MERFISH and Xenium platforms in the spatial part of the data. It includes spatially resolved profiles across multiple tissues and technologies. 76 Likewise, scGPT-spatial continually extends the single-cell foundation model using the SpatialHuman30M corpus, 77  which aggregates around 30 million cells/spots from Visium, Visium HD, Xenium, MERFISH, and related platforms. Complementing these token-based models, HEIST introduces a hierarchical graph foundation model trained on 22.3 million cells from 124 tissues across 15 organs, 78 jointly modeling spatial transcriptomics and proteomics as coupled cell-cell and gene-regulatory graphs.

However, the development of omics-based foundation models to decode and understand cellular signaling systems, like building AI virtual cells (AIVC) with multi-omics data, 79,80 is much more challenging than that of LLMs (foundation models) because, unlike human languages, omics data lack grammatical rules and the multi-omics datasets can only measure partial and static information or snapshots of the 3D spatial and dynamic cell signaling systems. Even for the analysis of existing omics data, it remains an open problem considering the challenges of integrating and representing multiomics data on a multi-scale and biologically meaningful manner. 79-82 For example, the current FMs of omics data arrange the genes and cells in a random manner with a limited focus on the learning of universal embeddings/ representations of individual genes and cells for prediction analysis tasks. 79 They are limited to deeply understand and decode the cellular omics signaling patterns. Cellular signaling network systems are rather complex because it consists of 3 billion base pairs long genome, about 20 thousand protein-coding genes, and tens of thousands of proteins and metabolites with millions of interactions at different biological levels. To model the large-scale and high-degree (high interactions among DNA-RNAprotein) cell signaling network systems, novel network/ graph AI models are needed to mitigate the oversquashing, high frequency omics signaling (omics heterogeneity), reliability and reproducibility challenges. 83-87 Therefore, novel foundation model designs are needed to mitigate and address these fundamental challenges.

### Agentic AI and AI Agents for Biomedical Research

The evolution of AI in precision medicine has progressed beyond static foundation models toward dynamic, autonomous AI agents and agentic AI systems capable of orchestrating complex multistep workflows with minimal human intervention. In scientific discovery, Agentic AI systems can, with human comparable capabilities (Figure 2), autonomously plan, execute, and adapt their actions to achieve specified goals, leveraging multiple specialized agents that coordinate through intelligent handoff mechanisms. 64 In the context of biomedical research, the emerging multi-agent systems (Figure 1) represent a paradigm shift from tools that require expert guidance at every step to autonomous platforms that can collaborate with human scientists and independently retrieve data, integrate knowledge, perform analyses, validate findings, and generate interpretable hypotheses (Figure 2). This section reviews the landscape of agentic AI systems for omics analysis, with particular emphasis on how multiagent architectures overcome the limitations of single-agent approaches by enabling comprehensive, cross-validated, and mechanistically grounded scientific discovery.

For general-purpose biomedical research, scientific discovery is generally an iterative process that includes data retrieval and analysis, literature search, and hypothesis generation with creativity. 4-7 To augment scientific discovery process, AI co-scientist was developed, 4 which have multiple agents working on hypothesis generation, reflection, ranking, review and evolution agents, literature search. The AI co-scientist was validated in drug repositioning, novel target discovery, and mechanism explanation. Biomni is a general purpose AI agent for biomedical data analysis. 5 In addition to the literature search and reasoning agents, it includes coding agents that can write code and call a set of biomedical data analysis tools for automated data analysis. Kosmos is another general purpose AI agent, 6 which can run code, analyze omics data, and synthesize literature reports. The evaluation results showed that the agent can reproduce reported discoveries in a set of studies. The omniCellAgent is a multi-agent system for omics-data driven scientific discovery. 7 It is built based on the omniCellTOSG dataset, 9 which includes single cell transcriptomics data of diverse organs, diseases and healthy controls, and cell types. Thus, users can conduct omics-data driven studies without preparing user-specific omics datasets. By chatting with OmniCellAgent, it can automatically and autonomously retrieve and analyze cell-type specific differential analysis and enrichment analysis. The literature search and prior knowledge agents will summarize the top ranked targets and pathways, and generate possible hypotheses. CellAgent extends this capability through a hierarchical plannerexecutor-evaluator architecture with self-iterative optimization that autonomously refines tool selection and hyperparameters. 88  CompBioAgent democratizes access through a web interface that translates natural language queries into interactive visualizations. 89 Moving beyond predefined tasks, CellVoyager independently explores scRNA-seq datasets to generate novel biological hypotheses, 90 having identified previously unreported links such as increased transcriptional noise and brain aging. These systems collectively illustrate the evolution toward scalable, accessible, and discovery-oriented autonomous omics analysis. STAgent focuses on spatial transcriptomics with multimodal VLM+LLM agents for end-to-end analysis. 91 The SpatialAgent tackles panel design through interpretation across spatial biology tasks. 92 Recent surveys/benchmarks map this landscape and evaluate agent frameworks on dozens of single-cell tasks. 93

Beyond chatting, recent agents for biological research can write, run, and critique analysis code end-to-end. For example, AutoBA exemplifies this trend by generating step-by-step analysis plans, 94 writing and executing code across multi-omics analysis tasks with minimal user input, supporting local deployment for privacy, and stabilizing runs via an automated code-repair loop. Systems like BIA agent autonomously conducts bioinformatics analyses from natural language instructions, 95 managing the entire analytical pipeline from data extraction to reporting, with strength in scRNAseq analysis. It can orchestrate data retrieval, pipeline selection, and report generation with autonomous code execution, but are vulnerable to planning errors/hallucinations and lack a dedicated coordination layer for multiagent stability. This gap is addressed in another work by Bio-Copilot, which adds agent-group management, human-agent interaction. Biocopilot coordinates multiple LLM agents for complex workflows. 96 BioAgents emphasizes privacy preserving, 97 local multi-agent setups using small LMs plus RAG, targeting reliability and reproducibility in code generation. EpiAgent targets single-cell epigenomics by pretraining on large scATAC-seq corpora to encode chromatin accessibility and support downstream tasks (e.g., cell typing, regulatory inference), 98 illustrating an agent/foundation-model approach specialized for epigenetic layers.

Agentic AI is still in its early stage for automatic scientific discovery in biomedical research and precision medicine. While these agent systems have demonstrated the feasibility of AI-driven automation in single-cell analysis and possibly scientific discovery, they remain constrained by critical limitations, e.g., reliance on user-provided datasets, user-specific questions/prompts, incomplete integration of external knowledge sources, data resources, and the need for iterative human oversight. The recent study showed that agentic AI systems can generate reliable and impressive results in demonstration case studies by setting predefined and static analysis plans based on known or predicted, case-specific steps or processes. 99 However, in real-world scientific discovery processes, research questions are often openended and involve many unpredictable and dynamic scenarios, such as code execution failures, tool misuse, or inappropriate data analyses, new hypothesis generation and revision, which frequently require adapting goals and plans based on feedback of agents perceiving the complex environment. 99 It is critical to train AI agents with dynamic planning and human-like creativity capabilities so that they can adapt their decision-making and tool invocation to handle unexpected scenarios, like human experts. 99,100

### Conclusion

Advances in large-scale multi-omics data generation and analysis have identified numerous molecular targets and elucidated parts of the key mechanisms involved in disease pathogenesis. However, the holistic and deep understanding of complex disease cell signaling systems remains limited. Consequently, effective and curative treatments are still lacking for most complex diseases, e.g., cancers, Alzheimer's disease, systematic chronic inflammation, lysosomal storage disorder diseases. In recent years, continuous breakthroughs in generative AI and foundation models have been transforming the paradigm of data analysis and scientific research. The convergence of transformative AI models with large-scale omics data marks the beginning of a new era in biomedical research and precision medicine.

The AI for biomedical research and precision medicine (AI4PM) is still in its early stages. Unlike building LLMs and foundation models based on human understandable language and image datasets, the integration, representation, analysis, interpretation, and applications of complex multiomics data using novel AI models represent some of the most challenging tasks in both AI and biomedical research/precision medicine. There are several fundamental challenges, e.g., problem definition and alignment (to map diverse and often vague, user-specific questions to appropriate, well-defined formulations and prompts that can effectively guide AI systems in subsequent analysis planning and task execution); analysis planning and adaptation (to handle unexpected results and feedback during iterative analysis processes); omics data retrieval and integration (to identify and integrate the right omics datasets, and analyze them with the appropriate models), knowledge search, synthesis and extraction (to synthesize and extract knowledge from large-scale literature reports and
