#### ABSTRACT

RNA splicing is an important post-transcriptional process of gene expression in eukaryotic organisms. Here, we developed a novel language model,  SpliceBERT, pre-trained  on the  precursor  messenger  RNA  sequences  of  72  vertebrates  to  improve  sequence-based  modelling  of RNA splicing. SpliceBERT is capable of generating embeddings that preserve the evolutionary information of nucleotides and functional characteristics  of  splice  sites.  Moreover,  the  pre-trained  model  can  be  utilized  to  prioritize  potential  splice-disrupting  variants  in  an unsupervised manner based on genetic variants' impact on the output of SpliceBERT for sequence context. Benchmarked on a multi-species splice  site  and  a  human  branchpoint  prediction  task,  SpliceBERT  outperformed  not  only  conventional  baseline  models  but  also  other language  models  pretrained  only  on  the  human  genome.  Our  study  highlighted  the  importance  of  unsupervised  learning  with  genomic sequences of multiple species and indicated that language models were promising approaches to decipher the determinants of RNA splicing.

### INTRODUCTION

Ribonucleic acids (RNA) splicing is a fundamental post-transcriptional process in eukaryotic gene expression, which removes introns from precursor  messenger  RNAs  (pre-mRNAs)  and  ligates  exons  into  mature  mRNAs.  Though  the  mechanism  underlying  RNA  splicing  is complex,  a  variety  of  studies  have  found  that  some  key  determinants  of  splicing  are  encoded  in  DNA  sequences  (1-3).  Therefore, deciphering splicing codes from RNA sequences by computational models is a promising approach and will facilitate the interpretation of genetic variants affecting RNA splicing.

Early studies mainly aimed to identify short sequence motifs related to splicing, including exonic splicing enhancers (4), branchpoints (5) and other splicing factors (6) with statistical models. Benefiting from accumulated high-throughput sequencing data, other studies were able to employ more advanced machine learning and deep learning models to directly predict RNA splicing events from sequences. For example, HAL (7) is a model trained on alternative splicing events from millions of random sequences for predicting the change of exon skipping and 5' alternative splicing induced by genetic variations. SPANR (3) is a Bayesian network model for predicting the Percent Spliced In (PSI or Ψ ) of alternatively spliced exons. More recently, deep learning models like MMSplice (8), SpliceAI (9) and Pangolin (10) employed deep convolutional  neural  networks  (CNN)  to  predict  alternative  splicing  events,  splice  sites  or  splice  site  usage.  These  methods  achieved superior performance as compared to earlier ones and have been widely utilized to analyze aberrant RNA splicing events caused by genetic variants. Though significant progress has been made in this field, there is still room for further improvements. For instance, these methods were developed to predict splice sites or  alternative splicing primary  sequences using primary  sequences,  while evolutionary information was not considered. Besides, other important splicing regulators like branchpoints (BPs) were less studied. Unlike splice sites, which can be reliably detected from RNA-seq data (11, 12), BPs are much more difficult to be experimentally identified (13), making it challenging to train deep learning models for BP prediction due to the lack of adequate high-confidence BPs.

To alleviate the problem of insufficient data, self-supervised learning (SSL) strategy utilized in large language models (LLM) like BERT (14) can be adopted. The SSL strategy has been utilized to learn representations of protein (15, 16), non-coding RNA (17) and prokaryote genome (18) sequences. These models were pre-trained on millions of sequences from a large number of species and therefore captured the evolutionary  information  that  is  critical  for  modelling.  Unfortunately,  these  methods  cannot  be  directly  applied  to  study  RNA  splicing because  eukaryotic  protein-coding  RNA  sequences  are  very  different  from  non-coding  RNAs  or  prokaryote  genome  sequences.  Though there are other genome language models that can be adopted to study RNA splicing (19, 20), they were pre-trained on the human genome, and thus it remains unclear whether pre-training BERT-like models on sequences from many species could improve sequence-based RNA splicing prediction.

Here, we developed a BERT-based model named SpliceBERT for studying RNA splicing. SpliceBERT was pre-trained on more than 2 million precursor messenger RNA (pre-mRNA) sequences from 72 vertebrates with gene annotations. We found that compared with models pre-trained on the human genome, SpliceBERT can better preserve evolutionary information in nucleotide embeddings. The hidden states and attention weights in SpliceBERT could reflect the functional characteristics of splice sites and branchpoints. Besides, we observed that splice-disrupting variants can induce a larger impact on their context than those with little effect on splicing and thus SpliceBERT  could serve as an unsupervised variant effect predictor for variants related to RNA splicing. On a multi-species splice site prediction task and  a human  branchpoint  prediction  task,  SpliceBERT  exhibited  superior  performance  to  conventional  models  without  pre-training  or  other available under aCC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license. ### MATERIAL AND METHODS

### The SpliceBERT model

Pre-training  data We  extracted  the  pre-mRNA  sequences  from  72  vertebrate  genomes  for  pre-training.  The  reference  genomes  in  fasta format and gene annotations in gtf format were downloaded from UCSC genome browser (21). The versions of the genomes and annotations are available in Table S1. The pre-mRNA sequences were extracted from genome sequences using 'bedtools getfasta' (22) based on gene annotations and overlapping transcripts were merged. In this way, we obtained a total number of 2011517 pre-mRNA sequences covering about  65  billion  nucleotides  (nt).  We  randomly  reserved  50000  sequences  for  validation  and  pre-trained  SpliceBERT  using  the  other sequences.

Model  architecture We  adopted  the  BERT  architecture  (14)  to  build  SpliceBERT.  SpliceBERT  is  consisted  of  6  Transformer  encoder layers,  where  the  hidden  layer  size  and  the  number  of  attention  heads  are  512  and  16,  respectively.  The  position  information  of  the nucleotides in each sequence are encoded using the one-hot positional embedding method. SpliceBERT has a total of about 19.4 million parameters.

Tokenization Previous pre-trained models for DNA sequences mainly adopted the k-mer tokenization (19, 20, 23) or the Byte Pair Encoding (BPE) tokenization (24) strategy. However, in this study, we simply used each nucleotide type (A, G, C, T/U) as a single token for the ease of getting embeddings for individual nucleotides. The [CLS] token (classification) and [SEP] token (separator) were added to the beginning and the end of each sequence input to SpliceBERT, which is a routine operation in most BERT models. The [MASK] token was used to represent the masked tokens in MLM task.

Pre-training by masked language modelling task We pre-trained SpliceBERT by the masked language modelling (MLM) task, which is a self-supervised  learning  strategy  to  pre-train  language  models.  Specifically,  for  each  input  sequence,  we  randomly  picked  15%  of  the nucleotides and 80%, 10%, 10% of which were replaced with the special token '[MASK]', random nucleotides, and original nucleotides (they  were  kept  unchanged),  respectively.  During  pre-training,  the  model  was  optimized  to  predict  the  correct  base  type  of  the  original nucleotides.  Intuitively, the  model  was  required  to  learn the  dependencies between  the  nucleotides in the input sequences  to recover the corrupted tokens.  In this way, we expected the model could capture the complex logic that may be encoded in the DNA/RNA sequences. Model implementation and pre-training We implemented SpliceBERT in PyTorch with the Huggingface transformer library (v4.24.0). The maximum length of input sequences was set to 1024. Since most pre-mRNA sequences are longer than 1024nt, fragments no longer than 1024nt  were  randomly  sampled  for  pre-training.  We  used  the  cross-entropy  loss  as  the  objective  to  optimize  the  model.  The  AdamW optimizer  was  used  and  the  initial  learning  rate  was  set  to  0.0001.  The  learning  rate  will  be  reduced  by  a  factor  of  2  when  model's performance on the validation data stops improving for 3 epochs. The model was trained in the automatic mixed precision (AMP) mode to decrease the consumption of memory and speed up model training. In practice, we pre-trained SpliceBERT in two stages. In the first stage, we used fixed-length sequences of 510nt to train SpliceBERT on 8 V100 GPUs for a week and the accuracy of MLM task finally reached about 0.56. Then, we continued to pre-train SpliceBERT with sequences ranging from 64nt to 1024nt until the MLM accuracy reached 0.56 again.

### Finetuning SpliceBERT for downstream tasks

In  our  study,  SpliceBERT  can  be  applied  to  two  types  of  downstream  tasks:  sequence-level  prediction  and  token-level  prediction.  For sequence-level prediction, we took the embedding of the [CLS] token as the sequence-level representation and fed it into a two-layer fully connected neural network for prediction. For token-level prediction, we simply fed the hidden states of the last Transformer layers as tokenlevel representations and fed them to a two-layer neural network to make prediction. During finetuning, we used an initial learning rate of 0.00005 and reduced the learning rate using the 'get\_polynomial\_decay\_schedule\_with\_warmup' scheduler provided in the Huggingface transformer package.

We adopted the 5-fold nested cross validation scheme to finetune and evaluate SpliceBERT on the cross-species splice site prediction task and the human branchpoint prediction task. The samples were first split in to  5  folds.  In  each  epoch, each  fold  was  held  out  as  the test dataset in turn and the other 4 folds were split into a training (3 folds) and a validation dataset (1 fold). The average precision score of the 5 validation datasets were used to select the optimal hyperparameters and the number of training epochs. In this way, all the samples could be used  for  independent  test.  Notably,  we  split  the  samples  into  different  folds  by  chromosomes  to  keep  the  samples  from  the  same chromosome will also be assigned into the same fold. This can help alleviate the problem of overfitting (30, 31).

### In silico mutagenesis analysis

In silico mutagenesis was employed to investigate the effects of genetic variants on their neighboring nucleotides or splice site/branchpoint predictions. Specifically, we exhaustively enumerated all single nucleotide variants (SNVs) in pre-defined regions of sequences and only one SNV was introduced to a sequence each time.

### Visualization of consensus motifs around splice sites and branchpoints using sequence logo

We visualized the  consensus motifs  around  splice  sites  and  branchpoints  using  the  Logomaker  package  (32).  The  logarithm  of  the  ratio between observed and expected nucleotide frequency was used for visualization. The observed frequencies of nucleotide were obtained by taking the average occurrence of nucleotide types across different samples for each position, respectively. The expected frequency of each nucleotide type (background frequency) was obtained by taking its average occurrence among all the samples at all the positions.

SpliceBERT is a language model pre-trained on more than 2 million pre-mRNA sequences from 72 vertebrates with the masked language modelling (MLM) task. In the pre-training stage, we randomly masked about 15% of the nucleotides in the sequences and trained the model to fill out the masked tokens (nucleotides) based on sequence context. In this way, we expected the model to learn high-level representations of  nucleotides  that  can  capture  the  logic  of  pre-mRNA  sequences.  Subsequently,  we  applied  SpliceBERT  a  series  of  downstream  tasks associated the RNA splicing (Figure 1A).

The genes of eukaryotic genes consist of exons and introns and the exons of protein-coding genes can further be partitioned to 5 prime untranslated regions (5'UTR), coding sequences (CDS) and 3 prime untranslated regions (3'UTR). Only the CDS regions encode proteins while they make up only a small fraction of the pre-mRNA sequences. Since the coding regions and non-coding regions may exhibit quite distinct characteristics, we compared the performance of MLM task in different genomic function regions (GFRs) using the MLM accuracy (MLM ACC, the fraction of masked tokens correctly filled out by the model) as a metric. The MLM ACC in introns is about 0.57 in both protein-coding  genes  and  long  non-coding  RNAs  (lncRNAs),  which  is  higher  than any  other  regions  by  at  least  0.05  (Figure  1B).  This suggested that  it  may be  easier  for  MLM  in  non-coding  regions  compared  to  coding  regions.  We  suspected  that  this  was  caused  by  the distinct  level  of  repeats  in  different  GFRs  and  therefore  calculated  the  fraction  of  repeated  regions  according  to  the  RepeatMasker annotation. As shown Figure 1B, the CDS regions have the lowest level of repeats and the introns have the highest level of repeats. This indicated that the fraction of repeated regions have a large impact on the MLM task.

It  is  well  known  that  evolutionary  information  provides  rich  information  of  the  function  of  biological  sequences,  which  plays  an important role  in  many  tasks  including  protein/RNA  structure  prediction  and  genomic  variant  analysis.  The  evolutionary  information  of genome sequences  can  be acquired by  multiple sequence alignment  across  many species.  Given the  pre-training  of  SpliceBERT  is  also involved with a variety of species, we wondered whether the evolutionary information could be captured by the MLM task. To this end, we first  randomly  selected  1000  510nt  fragments  from  the  human  transcriptome  and  extracted  the  PhastCons100way  conservation  score  for each  nucleotide.  The  nucleotides  with  a  PhastCons(100way)  score  of  'NaN'  were  discarded  and  the  distribution  of  PhastCons(100way) scores is shown in Figure 1C. We labeled the nucleotides with PhastCons score greater than 0.8 as 'conservative' and the other nucleotides as 'non-conservative'. Then, we fitted a linear regression (LR) model using the hidden states in SpliceBERT's last Transformer layer to classify conservative and non-conservative nucleotides. For comparison, we also fitted two models using the embeddings from SpliceBERThuman and one-hot encoded sequences (200nt up-/downstream of the sites), respectively. The  LR model using SpliceBERT embeddings achieved  the  highest  average  precision  (AP)  score,  which  is  0.34,  and  the  other  two  models  achieved  the  AP  scores  of  0.27  and  0.10, respectively  (Figure  1D).  The  result  demonstrated  the  language  model  could  implicitly  capture  evolutionary  information  through  MLM training and pre-training on multiple species outperformed single species.

### Language models enable unsupervised prediction of variant effect on splicing

Several studies  have  employed  pre-trained  protein/DNA  models  for zero-shot  mutation  effect prediction.  These  methods  usually  utilized only  the  difference between the predicted  MLM logits  of  mutant (MT)  allele  and  wild-type (WT)  allele to  estimate  the  mutation effect. However, the impact of genetic variations on the MLM logits of their contextual nucleotides was never explored. Here, we first performed in silico mutagenesis to obtain 10000 single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and applied SpliceBERT on the MT and WT sequences to acquire predicted  MLM logits and  measured the impact  of the  SNVs  using the  KL-divergence between  WT logits and  MT logits.  As shown  in Figure 2A, we grouped the SNVs into a conservative group and a non-conservative group according to the PhastCons(100way) score. We found that the SNVs occurring at conservative sites tend to induce a larger impact of predicted MLM logits on their neighboring nucleotides. Similarly, when we performed the same analysis on two datasets of splice-disrupting (SDV) and non-splice-disrupting (non-SDV) variants, VexSeq (33) and MFASS (34), a consistent trend can be observed in Figure 2B and Figure 2C that SDVs tend to have a larger impact on the MLM logits of their contexts.

We next compared the sum of KL-divergence in the 100nt neighboring regions for each SNV (denoted by KL-div(context)) to the logit change of masked mutation position (denoted by Δ logit) adopted by previous studies. The difference of KL-div(context) scores between the SDV and the non-SDV groups is more significant (P-value = 1.6 /g3400 10 /g2879/g2869/g2868 / 9.7 /g3400 10 /g2879/g2874/g2870 in  VexSeq/MFASS) than that of Δ logit  scores (Pvalue=0.06 /3.9 /g3400 10 /g2879/g2872/g2876 in VexSeq/MFASS) (Figure 3D,3E), indicating that taking the context information into consideration may achieve better performance than just using the mutation site. Moreover, we particularly extracted only the KL-divergence at the splice donor and acceptor sites (KL-div(SS)) to take the distance between mutations and splice donors/acceptors into account.  A significant difference could also be observed  in the KL-div(SS)  scores  between the  SDV  and  the  non-SDV  group  (P-value= 2.0 /g3400 10 /g2879/g2869/g2872 / 5.2 /g3400 10 /g2879/g2874/g2875 in VexSeq/MFASS).

Finally,  we  compared  SpliceBERT  with  SpliceBERT-human,  DNABERT  and  several  other  unsupervised  metrics  (phastCons/phyloP conservation  score, distance from  mutation to splice donors/acceptors) on the VexSeq and the MFASS dataset. We noticed that there are 68.2% and 80.7% of the SNVs in splice donors and acceptors are SDVs in VexSeq and MFASS, which are much higher than other SNVS (4% and 3% in both  datasets, respectively),  which  may  dominate  the  results  (Figure  S1).  We  thus  excluded  the  variants  in  donors  and acceptors  and  plotted  the precision-recall  curves  in  Figure  2F  and  2G.  SpliceBERT  consistently  outperformed  the  language  models  pretrained  with  human  data  only (SpliceBERT-human and DNABERT) and conservation scores, demonstrating the superior performance of language models pre-trained on multiple closely related species.

### SpliceBERT captures the association between splice donors and acceptors

We next investigated whether the attention weights in SpliceBERT could capture the association between donor and acceptor sites. To this end, we constructed 6 groups of donor/acceptor/random site pairs and compared the attention weights in these groups. The groups are (1) intronic  donor-acceptor  pair  (D-A(intron)):  the  donor-acceptor  pairs  from  the  same  introns,  (2)  exonic  donor-acceptor  pair:  the  donoracceptor pairs from the same exons, (3) unpaired donor-acceptor combinations: donor-acceptor pairs from different intron/exon, (4) donordonor pairs, (5) acceptor-acceptor pairs and (6) a control group of randomly selected sites in transcripts. Limited by the maximum sequence that SpliceBERT can process, we retained only the pairs within 800nt. To eliminate the potential bias caused by distance (Figure S5), we guaranteed that  the  distance  distribution  of  randomly-selected  samples is  approximately  uniformly  distributed  between  100nt  and  800nt, where samples of too short distance (&lt;100nt) were also excluded.

We first showed the distribution of attention weights (averaged across all layers) between the nucleotide pairs in 6 groups (Figure 4A). Donor-acceptor pairs from the same introns tend to have higher attention weights than the other groups. Although the attention weights between  other  donor-acceptor,  donor-donor  or  acceptor-acceptor  pairs  are  higher  than  the  randomly  paired  control  group,  they  are  still significantly lower than the first group's (P-value /g3407 1 /g3400 10 /g2879/g2874 by Mann-Whitney test). This is likely because the donors and acceptors from the same introns are connected in mature mRNAs. As exonic regions tend to have higher conservation scores than intronic regions (Figure 4B),  the  mature  mRNAs  may  be  more  conserved  across  species  so  that  SpliceBERT  could  identify  the  association  between  donors  and acceptors separated by intronic regions. This also implied that SpliceBERT is able to detect the long-range dependencies in pre-mRNAs.

Moreover, we also examined the patterns of the attention weights in different Transformer layers. We focused on the attention weights between donors and acceptors from the same introns and analyzed the distribution of donor sites' attention weights around acceptors and vice versa. Specifically, we extracted the attention weights of the donors or acceptors and centered them on the corresponding acceptors or donors, respectively. The median attention weights across samples within 100nt flanking regions of splice sites are shown in Figure 4C. We found that the attention weights in the 3 rd ,  the 4 th and the 5 th layer  were enriched around acceptors  and donors. For instance, the attention weights in acceptor sites in layers 3-5 are 0.0052, 0.0997 and 0.0046, respectively, which are 2.0, 24.7 and 1.8 times that of the background attention weights (average attention weights within the 201nt window. In contrast, in layers 1,2 and 6 the attention weights in acceptors are only 0.8, 0.5 and 0.7 times the background weights. A similar pattern could be observed in the distribution of acceptors' attention weights around donors (Figure 4C and Table S3). To pursue the reason why the attention weights in layers 3-5 could reflect the association between donors and accepters is beyond this study. However, the result suggested that the hidden states in layers 3-5, especially layer 4, are likely to have a closer relationship to RNA splicing, which is also consistent with our findings in the previous section (Figure S4).

### SpliceBERT enables robust splice site prediction across species

Given that SpliceBERT is able to generate representations related to RNA splicing by unsupervised learning, we next applied it to predict splice  sites  in  different  species  by  supervised  learning  to  see  if  it  could  achieve  state-of-the-art  performance.  Specifically,  we  finetuned SpliceBERT  on  the  training  data  taken  from  Scalzitti  et  al's  study  (35),  which  consisted  of  high-quality  splice  sites  in  114  eukaryotic organisms,  and  then  evaluated  it  on  independent  datasets  from  5  organisms.  We  followed  the  same  training/validating/testing  scheme described  in  the  Spliceator  study  to  make  the  results  comparable  to  those  reported  in  the  paper.  We  compared  SpliceBERT  with SpliceBERT-human,  SpliceBERT(w/o  PT),  DNABERT  and  4  existing  methods  (Spliceator  (35),  SpliceFinder  (36),  DSSP  (37)  and MaxEntScan (1) ) on the test datasets. The test datasets were taken from 5 species: H. sapiens (human), D. rerio(fish), D. melanogaster (fly), C. elegans (worm) and A. thaliana (plant). The F1 scores of these models are shown in Figure 5A and Table S4. SpliceBERT significantly outperformed the other methods with the P-values being less than 0.05 by the Wilcoxon test (Table S4). Notably, SpliceBERT consistently exhibited superior performance to the other methods on 4 non-human datasets, reflecting that the model pre-trained on multiple species can be better generalized to different species. The best-performing model on the human data is SpliceBERT-human. This is likely because the determinants of splice sites vary across species to some extent and thus the model pre-trained on only human data may better capture the logic of human splice site determinants. We also performed in silico mutagenesis analysis to compare the putative variant effect to known consensus sequence of splice sites. ### SpliceBERT improves branchpoint sites prediction

In addition to splice sites, branchpoint is another important group of RNA splicing regulator as they play a critical role in the identification of acceptors.  Here, we applied SpliceBERT for predicting branchpoints collected from Mercer et al's study (38). Since most (~92%) of the BPs locate within the 18-44nt upstream of splice acceptors (Figure 5A), we recruited only the BPs within this region as previous studies did (39, 40).

Before finetuning, we visualized the SpliceBERT, DNABERT and one-hot encoding embeddings of BPs and randomly selected non-BPs using  the  UMAP  algorithm  for  A,  C,  G  and  T,  respectively.  Unlike  splice  sites,  we  found  that  BPs  and  non-BPs  could  not  be  clearly separated  in  the UMAP of SpliceBERT embeddings (Figure 5B) and the results were even worse for DNABERT and one-hot encoding embeddings (Figure  S5). This is likely  because  human  branchpoints  are  usually  degenerative  (13)  and  cannot  be  well  characterized  like splice sites.

To predict  BPs,  we  finetuned  SpliceBERT  to  predict  the  high-confidence  BPs  in  the  18-44nt  upstream  of  splice  acceptors.  The  lowconfidence BPs were excluded from the dataset and other sites within the regions were used as negative samples. The 5-fold nested CV strategy  was  used  to  train  and  evaluate  the  model.  We  compared  SpliceBERT  against  5  other  models,  including  SpliceBERT-human, SpliceBERT(w/o  PT),  DNABERT,  LaBranchoR  (41)  and  Branchpointer  (39).  For  a  fair  comparison,  these  models  (except for Branchpointer)  were  trained  and  evaluated  under  the  same  scheme  as  SpliceBERT.  The  predictions  from  Branchpointer  were  directly generated using its scripts. The performance was evaluated using AP scores, and the PR-curves were plotted in Figure 5C. SpliceBERT outperformed all the baseline models, and the AP improvement was between 0.013 and 0.084. Notably, the AP score of SpliceBERT(w/o PT) was only 0.661. Although it has the same architecture as SpliceBERT/SpliceBERT-human, it was trained from scratch for BP prediction without pre-training.  The  gap  between  the  performance  of  SpliceBERT(w/o  PT)  and  SpliceBERT/SpliceBERT-human  demonstrated the importance of pre-training.

Next, we performed in silico mutagenesis on the regions around true BPs to visualize the effect of genetic variants on BP recognition. The variants  between  the  BP  -  3  (3nt  upstream  of  BP)  and  BP  +  1  (1nt  downstream  of  BP)  of  BPs  have  the  largest  putative  impact  on  BP prediction (Figure 5D), exactly matching the sequence consensus of known BPs (Figure 5E). Moreover, we also visualized the change of predicted  BP  scores  induced  by  specific  variant  types  in  Figure  5F.  For  example,  the  loss  of  adenine  at  the  BP  sites  will  significantly decrease  the  predicted  BP  scores,  and  variant  T&gt;A  and  T&gt;G  at  BP-2  can  also  largely  decrease  the  BP  scores.  These  observations  are consistent with the known pattern of pathogenic variants around BP sites as reported in Zhang et al's study (42).

### DISCUSSION

Genetic  information,  encoded  in  the  genomes,  provided  a  unique  challenge  for  language  models.  We  are  only  at  the  very  beginning  of unveiling the great learning potential of language models in solving computational genomic problems. Unlike human languages, genomic sequences are extremely long and the nucleotide composition in different functional loci may have completely different characteristics (e.g., different types of repetitive regions), which may bring critical challenges to directly apply the models for human languages successfully. Therefore, in this study, we focused pre-mRNAs and developed SpliceBERT pre-trained on vertebrate pre-mRNA sequences to facilitate the studies of RNA splicing. We were particular interest in how to connect pre-trained models and biological knowledge to give explainable evidence why pre-trained LLMs could improve sequence-based RNA prediction.

Multiple sequence alignment of genome/protein sequences across multiple species has shown a substantial capacity to improve sequencebased predictions because it could capture evolutionary information hidden in the sequences. In our study, we found that SpliceBERT pretrained  on  multiple  species  exhibited  superior  power  to  single-species  DNA/RNA  language  models  in  preserving  the  evolutionary information  in  nucleotide  embeddings.  It  suggested  that  although  SpliceBERT  did  not  explicitly  perform  MSA  during  pre-training,  the MLM task may also play a role similar to MSA to obtain evolutionary information. In addition, we found that the nucleotide embeddings generated by SpliceBERT, pre-trained by unsupervised learning only, not only provide information for identifying splice sites, but also have the potential  to  distinguish  strong  and  weak splice sites.  More  importantly,  the  donor-acceptor pairs  from  the  same introns tend to  have higher attention  weights  than  other types  of  donor  and  acceptor pairs. This  indicated that  nucleotides'  distinct  properties associated  with RNA splicing can be captured by unsupervised learning on the primary sequences of transcripts. Interestingly, we also noticed that splicedisrupting variants tend to induce a larger impact on the embeddings of their flanking nucleotides than benign ones, which was also useful to identify  pathogenic  mutations.  When  we  finetuned  SpliceBERT  to  predict  splice  sites  in  multiple  species  and  human  branchpoints,  it outperformed conventional  baseline  models  as  well  as  language  models  pre-trained  only  on  human  data.  Our  analysis  and  experiments indicated the effectiveness of multi-species unsupervised learning for sequence-based modelling of RNA splicing.

Currently, we only applied SpliceBERT to predict splice sites and branchpoints independent of tissue or cell types, while tissue/cell typespecific  alternative  splicing  cannot  be  ignored  because  they  contributed  a  lot  to  transcriptomic  and  proteomic  diversity  (43).  In  fact, predicting tissue/cell type-specific splicing is still challenging solely from sequences (10, 44). We may combine additional tasks like tissuespecific splice site strength (10) and RNA binding protein binding sites (45) to improve the model's ability for tissue-specific prediction.

Another potential limitation of SpliceBERT is that it could only process sequences no longer than 1024nt because the memory complexity of  vanilla Transformer layers  is quadratic to the sequence  length.  However, in  many  tasks in  genomics, including splice site prediction, integrating large genomic context from thousands to millions of nucleotides is crucial to ensure the accuracy of predictions (9, 46-48). One straightforward solution is to adopt Transformers with linear memory complexity with respect to sequence length. Besides, we may employ distill  learning  techniques  to  transfer  the  knowledge  in  LLMs  to  lightweight  architectures  like  CNN  to  handle  long  sequences.  Besides, given recent studies have shown that some improved versions of CNNs can achieve superior performance to Transformer-based models (49, 50), it is also promising to develop pre-trained genomic sequence language models without Transformer (51).

### Conclusion

We developed  SpliceBERT, a pre-trained language  model  for pre-mRNA sequences to study  and  improve sequence-based  prediction  of RNA  splicing.  SpliceBERT  successfully  captured  evolutionary  information  and  RNA  splicing  signals  from  primary  sequences  by unsupervised learning. Downstream tasks further demonstrated that our model outperformed the other language models pre-trained on only human data. Our study provided a better understanding of the possible mechanisms by which pre-trained models could improve sequencebased predictions of RNA splicing.
