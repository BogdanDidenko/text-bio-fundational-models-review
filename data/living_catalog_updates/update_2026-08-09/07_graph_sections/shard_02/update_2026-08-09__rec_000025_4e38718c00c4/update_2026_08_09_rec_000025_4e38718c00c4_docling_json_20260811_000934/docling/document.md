## Abstract

Virtual cells represent a promising paradigm to understand cellular mechanisms, behavior, and dynamics. The realization of virtual cells relies on the accurate modeling of cellular dynamics from large-scale, multi-modal single-cell data. However, experiment-specific technical noise and intrinsic biological heterogeneity pose major challenges for virtual cell modeling. To address this gap, we present scDifformer, a context-aware transformer model augmented with a denoising diffusion module and a dedicated post-training phase. This three-phase design, comprising masked language model pre-training, diffusion-driven post-training, and downstream fine-tuning, directly enhances scDifformer's ability to denoise sparse, noisy data and generalize across studies. Benchmarking across seven tissues and multiple independent studies shows that the diffusion module consistently improves cross-dataset performance, particularly in settings with strong batch effects. By combining the strengths of transformer and diffusion models, scDifformer achieves state-of-the-art performance in cell type annotation across diverse datasets. It further demonstrates robust capability in resolving immune cell identities across multiple tissues, accurately recovering key marker genes, functional pathways, and cross-tissue differentiation trajectories. Finally, by integrating scDifformer with a graph neural network, we extend its utility to spatial transcriptomics, significantly enhancing spot-level deconvolution accuracy. Altogether, scDifformer provides a scalable and biologically grounded framework for modeling heterogeneous single-cell data, offering a powerful foundation for the development of high-fidelity, multi-modal virtual cell models.

## Introduction

The concept of a virtual cell was proposed decades ago as a framework to understand cell behavior and transitions over time [ [1](#B1) ]. It is a computational representation that captures the dynamic interactions and regulatory processes of all DNA, RNA, proteins, and metabolites within a cell. Early efforts toward building virtual cells relied on systems of ordinary differential equations to model cellular networks [ [2](#B2) ]. However, the inherent complexity of intracellular processes and the lack of high-resolution molecular data limited the feasibility of these approaches. The advent of next-generation sequencing technologies, such as single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics (ST), has revolutionized our ability to profile cells across tissues, conditions, and developmental stages. These technologies have generated massive, high-dimensional datasets that capture cellular heterogeneity and dynamics with unprecedented resolution [ [3](#B3) ]. Concurrently, advances in deep learning, particularly natural language models [ [4](#B4) ], have enabled the development of scalable, data-driven models that can extract meaningful biological representations from these complex datasets. Together, these innovations have laid the foundation for constructing virtual cell models that are both comprehensive and generalizable.

A major recent direction in this effort involves the development of cell foundation models, which are large-scale pretrained models designed to learn universal representations of single-cell transcriptomes. Most existing models adopt transformer-based architectures, inspired by advances in natural language processing (NLP) and biological sequence modeling, whereas Saturn [ [5](#B5) ] and SCimilarity [ [6](#B6) ] employ alternative architecture. Geneformer [ [7](#B7) ], GeneCompass [ [8](#B8) ], and scBERT [ [9](#B9) ] leverage masked language modeling (MLM) on rank- or token-based gene expression encodings to learn contextual embeddings. These models show strong performance on tasks such as cell type classification, though they often rely heavily on input normalization schemes to control for batch effects and sparsity. Other approaches emphasize architectural advances. Models such as scGPT [ [10](#B10) ], CellFM [ [11](#B11) ], scFoundation [ [12](#B12) ], and xTrimoGene [ [13](#B13) ] treat full expression profiles as sparse sequences, using attention mechanisms or state-space models to capture complex gene-cell dependencies. These architectures support a wide range of downstream applications, from transfer learning to perturbation response prediction, and can be trained at a massive scale. A third emerging direction focuses on cross-species generalization, with models like Universal Cell Embedding [ [14](#B14) ] and TranscriptFormer [ [15](#B15) ] aiming to learn foundational representations that transfer across species boundaries, an essential step toward virtual cell modeling in evolutionary and comparative biology.

Despite these advances, several challenges remain. First, current models often struggle to fully denoise technical noise, such as batch effects introduced by different platforms, protocols, or labs. Most efforts to address this rely on input-level preprocessing rather than integrating noise-aware mechanisms within model architectures. Second, while diffusion models have shown impressive denoising capabilities in protein design [ [16](#B16) ], they are rarely adopted in single-cell transcriptomics, despite their natural advantage in mitigating noise and heterogeneity that are pervasive in single-cell data. Third, relatively few foundation models are equipped to integrate multi-modal data, such as jointly modeling scRNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics, limiting their utility in reconstructing tissue-level organization and dynamics.

To address these challenges, we present scDifformer, a diffusion-augmented transformer model enhanced with a diffusion-driven post-training phase designed to improve the fidelity and generalizability of virtual cell representations. Trained on a corpus of 34.2 million human single-cell transcriptomes spanning diverse tissues and biological conditions, scDifformer captures rich context-aware gene expression patterns while actively denoising technical variation through a discrete diffusion module. A second training stage, absent in most existing frameworks, enables the model to recover clean, biologically meaningful signals from sparse and heterogeneous data. In addition to accurate cell type annotation, scDifformer also enables the robust integration of immune cells from multiple organs and accurately captures gene- and pathway-level differences. Notably, applied to real-world data, it reconstructs canonical hematopoietic trajectories from hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) to terminal immune lineages across diverse tissues, including bone marrow, spleen, thymus, and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). We further demonstrate its potential usage in spatial transcriptomic data analysis, by integrating it with a graph neural network, GraphSAGE, to perform spot-level deconvolution. This approach offers a promising direction for unifying single-cell and spatial information within a shared modeling framework. These results highlight scDifformer as a scalable, biologically grounded framework capable of unifying single-cell information, and advancing the construction of high-fidelity, multi-modal virtual cell models.

## Materials and methods

### Standardizing pre-training corpus for accurate transcriptomic profiling

To create a comprehensive pre-trained corpus, we included 36.77 million human single-cell transcriptomes from various publicly available datasets. We focused on droplet-based sequencing techniques to ensure consistency in expression value metrics. We filtered potential doublets and retained cells with both total reads and mitochondrial reads laying within a 3-standard-deviation margin of the average values for each measurement within that particular dataset. Cells with elevated mutational loads were carefully excluded to maintain data integrity [ [17](#B17) ] and prevent potential misinterpretations arising from substantial network rewiring that can not be detected by genome sequencing [ [18](#B18) ]. Only Ensembl-annotated protein-coding/microRNA genes were kept for downstream processing. Cells expressing fewer than seven detected genes were excluded from the analysis since the 15% masking strategy used during pre-training was unable to effectively obscure individual genes in cells with reduced transcriptional detection [ [19](#B19) ]. Ultimately, a total of 34.2 million cells from 7225 samples met the defined filtration criteria and were included for further analysis. These cells were compiled and stored in .loom HDF5 format, accompanied by metadata extracted from the original studies.

To represent the transcriptome of each single cell non-parametrically, we followed instructions of the rank value encoding method [ [7](#B7) ]. Briefly, this method prioritized genes distinguishing cellular states via ratings normalized throughout the corpus, thereby highlighting transcription factors expressed at lower levels while attenuating genes that are universally expressed. The specific steps involved calculating the nonzero median expression for each detected gene across all cells and then normalizing the gene expression against total read counts of the sample and its corresponding nonzero median. This yielded the normalized expression values. Finally, we ranked the normalized expression values of each cell to obtain rank values. Tokenization of encodings made use of a 23 698-gene vocabulary. The Huggingface Datasets, which offers computational efficiency and facilitates archiving, was utilized to store our data.

### Three-stage training paradigm of scDifformer

Unlike conventional pre-training approaches founded upon a two-phase architecture consisting of "pre-training" and "fine-tuning" stages, the proposed scDifformer subjects the pre-trained weights to continued post-training using diffusion probabilistic models, rather than directly fine-tuning the pre-trained model after the initial pre-training phase. This additional post-training serves to further reduce noise and refine the learned representations by allowing for incremental parameter updates within the scDifformer framework. ### scDifformer architecture and pre-training

In the pre-training stage, we constructed a transformer encoder to leverage the multi-head self-attention mechanism to learn relationships between genes within single-cell transcriptomic data. Specifically, it consists of the following key components: gene embedding layer that maps each gene to a low-dimensional vector as the input, and a transformer encoder containing multiple self-attention and feedforward blocks to capture contextual information. During the pre-training process, 15% of genes within each transcriptome were randomly masked. The objective of pre-training was to train the model to infer the identity of genes occupying the masked positions by utilizing the contextual information provided by the unmasked genes, thereby enabling it to accurately represent the gene expression patterns of the respective cell states.

We trained our own model from scratch based on partially the code of Geneformer [ [7](#B7) ] using over 34 million single-cell transcriptomic profiles collected in-house. Our model architecture utilized 24 layers of transformer encoder units, representing, to our knowledge, the deepest pre-trained single-cell model to date, termed transformer-24L. Other parameters of the model include an input size of 2048, an embedding dimension of 512, and eight attention heads per layer. This model was pre-trained for 10 epochs to ensure thorough optimization of parameters. Subsequent empirical analysis validated that this configuration of model depth and pre-training duration achieved superior performance. The pre-training procedure aimed to identify an optimal hyperparameter configuration, including a maximum learning rate of 2 × 10 −4 , linear learning rate scheduling with warmup, the Adam optimizer with weight decay regularization [ [20](#B20) ], warmup steps of 20 000, L2 weight decay of 0.001, and a batch size of 6. This pre-training setup allowed for effective representation learning from our considerable single-cell transcriptomics dataset [ [19](#B19) ].

To pre-train efficiently on sizable datasets, we operationalized the most recent innovations in distributed graphics processing unit (GPU) training using Deepspeed [ [21](#B21) - [23](#B23) ]. This encompassed parcelling model parameters, gradients, and optimizer states crosswise GPUs to maximize computing power utilization and refine effectiveness. It also involved redistributing as much handling and memory as feasible to central processing units to adapt to GPUs, decreasing GPU memory usage and advancing scale and proficiency. Memory fragmentation was decreased by discriminating long-term and short-term allocations, boosting utilization and performance [ [19](#B19) ].

Through massive self-supervised pre-training on single-cell datasets, transformer-24L learned the complex interaction patterns between genes. The pre-trained transformer-24L can be directly used for downstream single-cell analysis or applied to other omics data like proteomics and multicellular contexts. The pre-training stage was conducted on 8 computing nodes using a total of 64 NVIDIA A100 GPUs for 10 epochs, requiring ~119 h.

### Post-training of scDifformer

We proposed scDifformer with a novel "post-training" phase for post-training transformer-24L with transcriptomic data, inspired by DiffusionBERT from NLP field [ [24](#B24) ], aiming to improve masked language models using diffusion models in the single-cell area. We chose diffusion models because they have been proven to achieve a superior trade-off between generation quality and diversity with its training strategy [ [24](#B24) ]. The high quality of generation corresponds to the high accuracy of data prediction, and its diversity advantage aligns with the inherent diversity of cell types in transcriptomic data.

Different from the existing single-cell large models' paradigm that mainly depends only on language understanding models, the generative language models we used are complementary to language understanding models. Diffusion models include two processes: a forward process that gradually adds noise to the data, and a reverse process that denoises the data. During training, both diffusion models and transformer-based pre-trained language models share a learning objective, which involves the task of noise reduction [ [24](#B24) ]. This allows scDifformer to leverage the advantages of both models. Pre-trained denoising language models like transformer-24L provide a good initialization that accelerates the convergence of the training process in the diffusion model. On the other side, scDifformer post-trains transformer-24L to learn the reverse process of a discrete diffusion process with an absorbing state to help improve the quality of data generation [ [24](#B24) ].

To facilitate the forward diffusion process, scDifformer utilizes the spindle noise schedule, which was originally derived from text generation tasks [ [24](#B24) ]. This schedule regulates the amount of noise introduced at each step, taking into account the information contained in each token. Specifically, its purpose is to ensure that the most informative token is given priority for masking in the initial stages, allowing the model to learn to recover it in the final position. This approach aligns with the easy-first generation hypothesis prevalent in denoising language models [ [24](#B24) , [25](#B25) ].

As to the matter of temporal encoding schemes, a typical diffusion model's reverse process predicts the denoised sample by taking a corrupted input sample and time step as inputs, represented as , where the time step *t* is an exogenous variable not observed during the pre-training of language models. In our approach, we do not explicitly include the time step *t* but infer it from the noised sample , motivated by time-agnostic decoding (TAD) within the scope of NLP [ [24](#B24) ]. Unlike images, due to the discrete nature of rank-encoded transcriptomic tokens, the diffusion time step can be implicitly deduced in sequences by counting masked tokens. This allows the pre-trained model to perform iterative decoding without representing the current time step, i.e. . Through time step inference rather than explicit modeling, this method facilitates the direct adaptation of diffusion models to the parameters of the base pre-trained model, i.e. transformer-24L, specifically in terms of time step incorporation, without modifications to the embedding structure or model architecture, accordingly improving the training efficiency.

In summary, the diffusion post-training phase is theoretically a process that leverages the pre-trained transformer as a denoising function and continuously optimizes its weights by minimizing the variational lower bound of a discrete diffusion process. It does not alter the model architecture but refines and enhances the model's already learned representations through a new generative training objective (from noise to data) that is compatible with the MLM objective, making it more adept at recovering clear, generalizable biological signals from complex, heterogeneous real-world single-cell data. The diffusion-based post-training stage was performed on 9 computing nodes with 72 NVIDIA A100 GPUs for 3 epochs, requiring ~65.6 h.

Experimental results on downstream tasks demonstrate that scDifformer not only achieved significant improvement over transformer-24L but also had superior performance compared to existing single-cell pre-training models in downstream tasks.

### scDifformer gene embeddings, cell embeddings, and attention weights

There are mainly two strategies to apply pre-trained language representations to downstream tasks: feature-based and fine-tuning. The feature-based method establishes an architecture specific to the task to incorporate pre-trained representations as additional features [ [26](#B26) ]. Extractable features in scDifformer include gene embeddings, cell embeddings, and attention weights. When each single-cell transcriptomic data are input into the scDifformer model, the model projects each gene into a 512-dimensional vectorized semantic space. Thus, the gene embedding can be extracted from the hidden state weights of the model. In particular, it can be extracted from the penultimate layer, because the output layer is more closely related to the predictive task, while the penultimate layer provides a more generalizable representation [ [19](#B19) ]. scDifformer's cellular embeddings can be generated by averaging the embeddings of all detected genes within that cell, likewise yielding a 512-dimensional vector.

Each scDifformer layer comprises eight attention heads, intending to learn different classes of genes and their relationships. The attention weights represent the contextual dependency or association strength from one gene to another, as learned by the model, and are interpreted as indicative of gene-gene interactions. We referred to the scBERT method for extracting the attention weights, which are computed during the self-attention operation and can be obtained for each attention head. There are 24 layers with eight attention heads in each layer of scDifformer. We chose to use the last layer to extract attention weights of our model. For integration, we take an element-wise average across all attention metrics of the eight attention heads in the last layer. In this average attention matrix, each value indicates the amount of attention paid from gene to gene . To emphasize the significance of genes to each cell, we summed the attention matrix column-wise to obtain an attention-sum vector, which has a length equal to the number of input genes.

### scDifformer fine-tuning

Fine-tuning was implemented by initializing the model with post-trained weights and adding a final task-specific layer. In this study, we focused on the downstream task of cell-type annotation, which is a classification task. Specifically, a classification head consisting of a linear layer on top of the pooled output was added on top of the scDifformer model. The purpose of this head is to map the final output of the scDifformer model to the appropriate label i.e. cell-type for the classification task. The parameters of the model were updated during training on this specific task in the fine-tuning stage. Fine-tuning results for cell-type categorization were reported as accuracy and F1 score utilizing five-fold cross-validation, training on 80% labels and testing the remaining 20% held-out labels across five iterations. Furthermore, cell type fine-tuning was additionally assessed in an inter-dataset setting to evaluate the robustness and cross-dataset generalization performance of the model.

#### Simulating "multi-cell-spot" datasets

In order to benchmark the deconvolution softwares, we simulated spots containing multiple cells with known cell composition within each spot as introduced in [ [42](#B42) ]. Mouse visual cortex data [ [43](#B43) ] was gridded into 253 squares (600 × 600 pixels each) according to the coordinates of each cell. Only squares with more than two cells were kept in the following benchmarking. The center of each square was considered as the coordinate of the "spot." The sum of read count of each cell was considered as the total read count of the spot. We take the percentage of each cell type in each spot as the ground truth. Single-cell RNA-seq data of mouse visual cortex served as reference for deconvolution was acquired from [ [44](#B44) ].

### Application of model in spatial transcriptome deconvolution

scDifformer cell embeddings can encode characteristics of the state of that single cell. We tried to utilize the diffusion embeddings to enhance the cell information so as to improve deconvolution performance. For each cell, the embeddings generated at first would be the shape of [2048, 512], which represents the dimension of tokens and the dimension of gene embeddings. Since the size for the embedding is too large, we first straighten the embedding matrix and then used Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to reduce the dimension from 2048*512 to 1024. We used the second-to-last layer of the models to extract our embeddings, as the final layer is known to capture features more directly related to the learning objective prediction, while the second-to-last layer provides a more generalizable representation.

The embeddings generated by the diffusion model were utilized in deconvolution of ST data. We first embedded the raw scRNA-seq data and the ST data separately using scDifformer, following the procedure above. For embeddings of the reference scRNA-data, pseudo-spots were generated by randomly selecting a mixture of cells. Here, we used a sampling-by-cell-type method [ [45](#B45) ] to obtain a more balanced pseudo-spot generation ( [Supplementary Tables S7](#sup1) and [S8](#sup1) ). The pseudo-spots generated by scRNA data and ST embedding were combined. Meanwhile, we identified cell-type marker genes from the raw expression matrix using logistic regression. The expression matrix of these marker genes was finally concatenated with the combined embeddings.

We constructed the *k* -nearest neighbouring graph based on the above pseudo- and real spots and finally fed it into an inductive graph neural network, GraphSAGE [ [46](#B46) ], to learn the information from the neighbouring spots. ## Results

### scDifformer model architecture and pre- and post-training strategies

Understanding cellular behavior through virtual cell models requires learning from increasingly large and complex single-cell transcriptomic data. However, technical noise and biological heterogeneity pose major challenges to building accurate and generalizable representations. Existing foundation models often rely on transformer architectures and two-stage training (pre-training and fine-tuning), which can struggle with noisy or sparse signals. To address this, we introduce scDifformer, a three-stage framework that incorporates a diffusion-based post-training module to denoise embeddings and improve robustness across diverse biological contexts (Fig. [1A](#F1) ).

#### Construction of the training corpus

To construct a comprehensive and high-quality corpus for pre-training, we compiled a total of 34.2 million single cells derived from over 7000 publicly available studies, integrating data from large-scale repositories such as DISCO [ [53](#B53) ] and CZ CELLxGENE [ [54](#B54) ], representing cells from diversity tissues (Fig. [1B](#F1) ). To ensure consistency and reliability across datasets, we implemented a standardized filtering pipeline (see "Materials and methods" section). To represent the transcriptome of each cell in a way that is compatible with transformer architectures, we adopted the rank value encoding strategy, which encodes the relative expression ranks of genes rather than their absolute values (Fig. [1A](#F1) ). As previously demonstrated in Geneformer [ [7](#B7) ], rank-based encoding effectively transforms transcriptomic profiles into a language-like input sequence, enabling the model to capture contextual dependencies among genes while preserving biologically meaningful expression pattern. This encoding is well-suited to transformer architectures, as it preserves co-expression pattern while reducing sensitivity to technical variation.

#### Self-supervised transformer pre-training

During the pre-training phase, we adopted a transformer encoder architecture [ [55](#B55) ] to process the rank-encoded transcriptomic inputs. Several prior implementations have successfully combined transformer-based models with single-cell transcriptomics or gene expression data [ [7](#B7) , [14](#B14) ]. Rather than reinventing the architecture, we built upon existing advances, particularly Geneformer, a single-cell foundation model that incorporates attention mechanisms and contextual modeling. Scdifformers also leverages self-attention to identify salient genes from high-dimensional transcriptomic inputs, optimizing predictive performance for cell-level inference tasks. The pre-training objective follows a MLM paradigm, where 15% of genes are randomly masked, and the model is trained to predict their identities based on surrounding gene context. This approach encourages the model to learn latent expression relationships and functional gene modules, ultimately improving its capacity to distinguish cell types and phenotypes. To enhance model capacity, we developed a 24-layer variant, referred to as transformer-24L, enabling deeper representation learning and improved scalability. Moreover, rather than directly utilizing off-the-shelf pre-trained model parameters from public sources, we trained transformer-24L from scratch using our in-house compiled dataset of over 34 million single-cell profiles, offering broader diversity and improved coverage of biological states (Fig. [1B](#F1) ).

#### Diffusion-based post-training: a denoising innovation

Following the pre-training, we introduced a diffusion-based post-training stage to further refine the representations learned by transformer-24L. Specifically, we applied a discrete diffusion process to the rank-encoded transcriptomic data, aiming to enhance the expressiveness of MLM by incorporating the denoising capabilities of diffusion principles into the single-cell context. This strategy combines the strengths of both architectures: the contextual learning capacity of transformers and the distributional robustness of diffusion models. The post-training stage allows for incremental parameter updates that reduce noise and improve the fidelity of virtual cell representations within the scDifformer framework. The diffusion model leverages its inherent ability to model diversity, aligning naturally with the biological heterogeneity present in single-cell transcriptomic data. During the forward diffusion process, structured noise, representing both technical variation and biological heterogeneity, is added to the input tokens following an information-aware noise schedule. Specifically, this schedule prioritizes masking the most informative tokens early in the diffusion process, compelling the model to learn their recovery later. This mechanism aligns with the easy-first generation hypothesis common in denoising language models. The model then learned the reverse (denoising) process through a discrete diffusion framework with an absorbing state. To maintain compatibility with the pre-trained transformer model, we adopted a TAD strategy, allowing for temporal conditioning without modifying the architecture of transformer-24L. By refining the model's internal representations through post-training, this stage significantly improves the ability of scDifformer to recover biologically meaningful signals from noisy single-cell profiles (Table [1](#tbl1) ).

### Diffusion post-training enhances model generalizability across studies

To test whether the diffusion module enhances the model's ability to reduce technical noise and better represent cellular heterogeneity, we conducted a systematic evaluation across seven tissues, bone marrow, PBMC, lung, pancreas, liver, heart, and kidney, each represented by two independent single-cell RNA-seq studies (see "Materials and methods" section). This design enabled both intra-study (within-study) and inter-study (cross-study) evaluations. While intra-study comparisons reflect the model's basic representational ability in consistent technical settings, inter-study evaluations introduce more realistic noise from batch effects, platform differences, and donor variability.

We found that the 24-layer transformer architecture consistently outperformed its 12-layer counterpart in capturing features across datasets ( [Supplementary Table S1](#sup1) and Table [1](#tbl1) ). Furthermore, it surpassed the performance of the original Geneformer model, indicating that increasing model depth improves the fidelity and generalizability of learned cell representations. We additionally evaluated the computational impact of model depth using the Kidney\_Hochane/Kidney inter-dataset annotation task. Under identical hardware and training settings (4 NVIDIA A40 GPUs), the 24-layer transformer required 382 s per epoch during training on Kidney\_Hochane (11 318 cells), compared to 202 s for the 12-layer model. During inference on Kidney (13 057 cells), the 24-layer model required 259.4 s, whereas the 12-layer model required 171 s. Despite the increased computational cost, the deeper architecture consistently yielded improved downstream performance and representation quality. Based on these findings, we selected the 24L transformer as the backbone for scDifformer and combined it with the diffusion module to maximize representational capacity and robustness across studies.

To evaluate the specific contribution of the diffusion module, we compared models trained with and without diffusion using the same 24-layer transformer backbone. In intra-study settings, where technical variation is minimal, the diffusion module yielded limited improvements, with gains in the lung, heart, bone marrow, and kidney datasets. However, the overall improvement trend remained consistent across tissues ( [Supplementary Table S1](#sup1) ). In contrast, under inter-study conditions with greater technical and biological variability, the diffusion-enhanced model achieved an overall improvement in annotation accuracy across all tissues (Table [1](#tbl1) ), underscoring its ability to generalize across heterogeneous datasets. Although the absolute gains were moderate, the consistent trends across independent datasets are in line with the hypothesis that the diffusion module may help improve the stability of latent cell representations, potentially through noise-aware refinement. However, given the current experimental design, we cannot fully disentangle the contribution of the diffusion module from other factors, and the observed improvements should not be interpreted as solely attributable to diffusion. Further evaluation across a broader range of tissues and cell types, together with additional ablation studies, would be helpful to draw more definitive conclusions.

Taken together, these results suggest that incorporating the diffusion module is associated with improved robustness and generalization across diverse datasets. This provides preliminary evidence supporting the potential value of diffusion-based refinement in single-cell modeling pipelines, particularly for settings involving heterogeneous tissues, platforms, and experimental conditions.

### scDifformer achieves state-of-the-art performance in cell-type annotation across diverse datasets

Cell-type annotation is a fundamental and widely used downstream task in single-cell RNA-seq data analysis. To rigorously assess performance, we constructed a standardized benchmarking framework comparing scDifformer with 10 representative annotation methods across both intra-dataset and inter-dataset settings. The compared methods include supervised classification-based approaches (scPred [ [56](#B56) ], scBERT [ [9](#B9) ], scClassify [ [57](#B57) ], Geneformer [ [7](#B7) ]), correlation-based methods (CHETAH [ [58](#B58) ], scmap-cell [ [59](#B59) ], scmap-cluster [ [59](#B59) ]), a marker gene-based method (Garnett [ [60](#B60) ]), and two foundation model-based approaches (ScFoundation and CellFM).

We first evaluated performance on six human scRNA-seq datasets (liver, kidney, lung, pancreas, bone marrow, and PMBCs). For intra-dataset benchmarking, five-fold cross-validation was performed to reduce the influence of random splits. The number of cell types per dataset ranged from 11 to 20, and total cell counts ranged from ∼8000 (typical-sized datasets) to 68 000 (large datasets). Performance was measured using accuracy and macro *F* 1 -score (see "Materials and methods" section). Across all datasets, scDifformer consistently outperformed all other methods on both metrics (Fig. [2A](#F2) , [Supplementary Fig. S1](#sup1) , and [Supplementary Table S2](#sup1) ).

In real-world scenarios, users often rely on annotated reference datasets to label unannotated data [ [61](#B61) ]. To reflect this use case, we conducted inter-dataset benchmarking using five pairs of datasets derived from the same tissue but from different studies. Each dataset in a pair was used alternately as training and test data. scDifformer again achieved the best performance in kidney datasets and yielded comparable results to the best-performing methods in most other tissues, with the exception of pancreas (Fig. [2B](#F2) and [Supplementary Table S3](#sup1) ). Notably, model-based cell-type classification consistently outperforms marker gene-based approaches, highlighting the advantage of learning-based methods in handling large and complex datasets. However, a key limitation is that such models often require retraining for each tissue or cell type, reducing scalability and limiting their general applicability across diverse biological contexts.

To further investigate prediction quality, we visualized confusion matrices of the kidney dataset (Fig. [2C](#F2) and [Supplementary Fig. S2](#sup1) ). scDifformer accurately predicted all cell types, including challenging ones with subtle differences. In contrast, scBERT failed to distinguish intercalated cell types A and B, and Geneformer showed poor performance on type B. These two cell types, both located in the kidney's collecting ducts, are involved in acid-base homeostasis [ [62](#B62) ]. Under physiological conditions, type B cells can transdifferentiate into type A [ [63](#B63) ], a process essential for maintaining pH balance. Disruption of this mechanism is implicated in distal renal tubular acidosis [ [64](#B64) ]. The ability of scDifformer to discriminate between such functionally and transcriptionally similar cell types demonstrates its robustness and biological interpretability in real-world annotation tasks.

Together, these results demonstrate that scDifformer not only achieves state-of-the-art accuracy in cell-type annotation across a wide range of datasets but also excels in distinguishing transcriptionally similar and functionally related cell populations. Its ability to generalize across studies and tissues, combined with its strong biological interpretability, highlights its potential as a robust and reliable annotation framework for large-scale single-cell analysis.

We focused on four cell types, B cells, ciliated cells, fibroblasts, and NK cells, from the Travaglini lung dataset [ [29](#B29) ], and extracted the top 10 genes with the highest attention scores in each. In B cells, top-ranked genes included *MS4A1* (encoding the *CD20* protein) [ [65](#B65) ], a canonical B-cell marker, as well as *CD79A* and *CD79B* , which form part of the B cell receptor signaling complex [ [66](#B66) ] (light blue columns in Fig. [3A](#F3) ). For ciliated cells, high-attention genes such as *CIBAR2* and *MAPK15* , while not traditionally considered cell-type markers, are known to be involved in ciliogenesis [ [67](#B67) , [68](#B68) ] (ochre columns in Fig. [3A](#F3) ), suggesting the model captures relevant biological context beyond known markers. Fibroblast attention genes were strongly enriched for known markers including *DCN, LUM, FMO2* , and *COL1A2* [ [69](#B69) , [70](#B70) ] (dark blue columns in Fig. [3A](#F3) ). In NK cells, the model assigned high attention to both *IL2RB* [ [71](#B71) ], a surface marker, and effector molecules such as *KLRF1* and *FCGR3A* , involved in NK-cell-mediated cytotoxicity (red columns in Fig. [3A](#F3) ).

To validate the biological relevance of these high-attention genes, we performed enrichment analysis using the top 100 attention-weighted genes for each cell type ( [Supplementary Table S4](#sup1) ). When queried against a reference collection of human cell-type expression signatures [ [51](#B51) ], the most significantly enriched term in each case corresponded to the true annotated analysis further revealed strong associations with biological processes specific to each cell type (Fig. [3C](#F3) - [F](#F3) and [Supplementary Table S5](#sup1) ). For example, B cell attention genes were enriched for "B cell activation," ciliated cell genes for "primary cilium development," fibroblast genes for "regulation of fibroblast proliferation," and NK cell genes for "natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity" and "granzyme-mediated programmed cell death signaling."

To further assess whether these attention-derived genes provide more reliable biological interpretation than other single-cell foundation models, we compared scDifformer with scFoundation and CellFM using the same gene-ranking and enrichment-analysis workflow. In the lung dataset, scDifformer showed a clear advantage in cell-type enrichment. As previously described, the top genes identified by scDifformer were consistently enriched for the expected cell-type signatures (Fig. [3A](#F3) ). In contrast, scFoundation and CellFM produced less specific top-attention-weighted genes and enrichment profiles ( [Supplementary Fig. S3](#sup1) ). For example, cell-cycle related gene *BLCAP* [ [72](#B72) ] was identified by scFoundation for B cells and several house-keeping genes such as *QARS1* and *XPOT* were identified by CellFM ( [Supplementary Fig. S3](#sup1) ). They produced unrelated epithelial, macrophage, thymocyte, neuronal, smooth muscle, pericyte, or club-cell-associated signatures among the top enriched terms ( [Supplementary Fig. S4A](#sup1) ). These results suggest that scDifformer-derived genes better preserve the annotated cell identities in the lung dataset.

Consistently, GO enrichment analysis of the lung dataset further supported the superior functional interpretability of scDifformer. The genes prioritized by scDifformer were enriched for canonical biological programs corresponding to each cell type, including B cell activation and adaptive immune response for B cells, cilium organization and cilium movement for ciliated cells, extracellular matrix organization for fibroblasts, and immune effector/cytotoxicity-related processes for NK cells (Fig. [3C](#F3) - [F](#F3) ). By contrast, scFoundation was more frequently enriched for broad metabolic or housekeeping processes, such as cellular respiration, oxidative phosphorylation, translation, and ATP biosynthetic process, whereas CellFM often yielded weakly related or mismatched pathways, including neuronal signaling, neurotransmitter transport, endoplasmic-reticulum-stress-related terms, and generic transport processes ( [Supplementary Fig S4B](#sup1) and [Supplementary Table S6](#sup1) ). Thus, in the lung dataset, scDifformer outperformed the comparison models in both cell-identity preservation and cell-type-specific functional enrichment.

To assess whether the attention patterns generalize beyond the lung dataset, we further analyzed an independent kidney sample [ [36](#B36) ] containing four major cell types: endothelial cells, principal cells, proximal tubule cells, and thick ascending limb cells. We extracted the top attention-weighted genes for each kidney cell type and visualized their expression patterns across cells ( [Supplementary Fig. S5](#sup1) ). The attention heatmap showed clear cell-type-specific gene modules. Endothelial cells were characterized by high attention on vascular and endothelial-associated genes such as *FLT1* and *ADGRL4* [ [73](#B73) ]. Principal cells showed high attention on renal epithelial and collecting-duct-associated genes, including *HSD11B2* [ [74](#B74) ], consistent with epithelial identity, sodium transport, and mineralocorticoid-responsive renal physiology. Proximal tubule cells were marked by reabsorption and transporter-associated genes such as *SLC5A12* and *ACSM2A/ACSM2B* [ [75](#B75) ]. Thick ascending limb cells showed high attention on canonical nephron-segment and ion-transport genes, including *SLC12A1* [ [76](#B76) ] ( [Supplementary Fig S5](#sup1) ). These gene-level patterns indicate that scDifformer attention weights capture biologically meaningful and cell-type-relevant programs in the kidney dataset rather than merely reflecting generic transcriptional activity.

We next performed enrichment analysis on the kidney attention-weighted genes using the same workflow. GO enrichment analysis showed that the scDifformer-derived genes were strongly associated with the expected functions of the corresponding renal cell types ( [Supplementary Fig. S6A and B](#sup1) ; [Supplementary Table S6](#sup1) ). Endothelial cells were enriched for vascular programs such as angiogenesis, blood vessel morphogenesis, vasculature development, and tube morphogenesis. Principal cells were enriched for renal system development, kidney development, intracellular chemical homeostasis, and ion homeostasis. Proximal tubule cells showed enrichment for membrane localization and ion transport, including protein localization to membrane, metal ion transport, and monoatomic cation transmembrane transport. Thick ascending limb cells were enriched for metal ion transport and ion-homeostasis-related pathways, consistent with the known role of this nephron segment in electrolyte transport. These results demonstrate that scDifformer attention-derived genes remain functionally interpretable in an independent tissue context.

We also compared the kidney enrichment results with scFoundation and CellFM ( [Supplementary Fig. S6A and B](#sup1) ; [Supplementary Table S6](#sup1) ). Unlike the lung dataset, cell-type enrichment results in the kidney sample were less discriminative among the three models, likely because multiple renal tubular cell types share epithelial lineage and overlapping kidney epithelial signatures. As expected, GO enrichment revealed a clear advantage of scDifformer. While scDifformer recovered renal cell-type-relevant functional programs, scFoundation and CellFM were more frequently associated with broader or less cell-type-specific processes, such as RNA splicing, protein folding, nucleotide metabolism, cellular respiration, chromatin remodeling, and generic metabolic programs. These findings indicate that, even when broad cell-type signature enrichment is comparable, scDifformer identifies genes that more accurately reflect the functional programs of kidney endothelial and tubular epithelial cells.

These findings demonstrate that scDifformer assigns biologically meaningful attention to cell-type-relevant genes, underscoring its potential not only as a high-performing classifier but also as a tool for interpretable discovery of molecular programs that define cellular identity.

### Tracing immune cell development across tissues through embedding-guided trajectory inference

Building on the gene marker and pathway-level interpretability demonstrated in heterogeneous immune cell populations, we next investigated whether the cell embeddings learned by scDifformer could capture developmental trajectories across distinct immune tissues. To this end, we curated scRNA-seq datasets from bone marrow [ [77](#B77) ], spleen [ [77](#B77) ], thymus [ [78](#B78) ], and PBMCs [ [79](#B79) ], encompassing immune cells spanning hematopoietic stem and HSPCs to terminally differentiated lineages. We first compared the performance of scDifformer embeddings against a widely used reference model, scVI [ [80](#B80) ], scFoundation [ [12](#B12) ], and scGPT [ [10](#B10) ], to assess whether our embeddings could preserve both cell type identity and tissue-specific information. We then employed Slingshot [ [81](#B81) ] for pseudotime inference, using cluster-level connectivity derived from Partition-based Graph Abstraction [ [82](#B82) ] to reconstruct cross-tissue lineage maps.

Visualization of the cell embeddings projected with UMAP showed that raw expression data (Fig. [4A](#F4) ) suffered from strong batch effects and poor cell type separation. In contrast, embeddings learned from scVI (Fig. [4B](#F4) ), scDifformer (Fig. [4C](#F4) ), scFoundation (Fig. [4D](#F4) ), and scGPT (Fig. [4E](#F4) ) significantly improved cellular resolution. Notably, scDifformer generated more compact and continuous clusters, better reflecting underlying developmental transitions. To quantitatively evaluate the inferred trajectories, we adopted five criteria: biological plausibility, directional coherence, confidence distribution, lineage continuity, and topological connectivity. Canonical hematopoietic hierarchies served as references for biological validity, where HSPCs differentiate into three major lineages [ [83](#B83) , [84](#B84) ]: (i) lymphoid (e.g. B cells, T cells, NK cells), (ii) myeloid (e.g. granulocytes, monocytes, dendritic cells), and (iii) erythroid/megakaryocyte lineages. Trajectory inference using scDifformer embeddings (Fig. [4C](#F4) ) recovered multiple well-supported developmental paths, including HSPC → megakaryocyte and monocyte → dendritic cell differentiation, with clear directional transitions from progenitors to mature immune cell types. In contrast, the scVI-based trajectories (Fig. [4B](#F4) ) mainly captured lateral transitions between immune subtypes (e.g. CD4 to CD8 T cells), and even biologically implausible paths such as megakaryocyte → HSPC. Though both models exhibited relatively low global confidence scores (mean &lt; 0.05), key transitions in the scDifformer map, such as monocyte → dendritic cell (confidence = 0.09), received stronger support. Notably, scDifformer also identified a transition from CD4 to CD8 T cells. While this conversion is not part of the canonical T cell differentiation hierarchy, it has been reported under specific pathological contexts such as chronic inflammation or viral infection [ [85](#B85) ], where T cells may exhibit phenotypic plasticity or reprogramming. Although our benchmarking datasets were not derived from these disease states, it is possible that the pretraining corpus included related immune profiles, enabling the model to learn such rare transitions. For scFoundation, the inferred trajectories show limited biological consistency. Several reversed lineage relationships were observed (e.g. B cell → HSPC, dendritic cell → HSPC), which contradict established hematopoietic hierarchies. In addition, T, NK, and ILC populations are poorly resolved, suggesting the embeddings capture transcriptional similarity rather than developmental structure. Overall, these embeddings are less suitable for trajectory inference under the same framework. For scGPT, the inferred structure resembles a transcriptional similarity graph rather than a lineage trajectory. We observed multiple cross-lineage and reversed connections (e.g. B cell → macrophage, mast cell → HSPC), along with extensive mixing among T, NK, and ILC populations. This suggests the embeddings prioritize expression similarity over developmental directionality. Overall, considering both biological plausibility and global coherence of the inferred trajectories, scDifformer performs best in trajectory inference. The model demonstrates strong sensitivity in capturing subtle or underrepresented cell state transitions. While most inferred trajectories are consistent with known biology, a small number of implausible connections (e.g. macrophage → megakaryocyte) are observed, likely reflecting residual technical noise or integration artifacts.

These results demonstrate that scDifformer's context-aware embeddings support more accurate and biologically coherent pseudotime inference than traditional approaches. The ability to reconstruct lineage hierarchies across diverse tissues highlights its potential for modeling dynamic cell state transitions, reinforcing its role as a core component in building virtual immune cell representations.

### Cross-modal application of scDifformer to spatial spot deconvolution

To extend the application of scDifformer embeddings beyond conventional transcriptomic analyses, we investigated their utility in spatial transcriptomics deconvolution, a task that aims to infer the underlying cell-type composition of spatial spots. Spatial deconvolution offers a potential avenue for bridging single-cell and spatial transcriptomic modalities by leveraging the cell-type information learned from scRNA-seq to interpret spatial data. Given the performance of scDifformer embeddings in cell annotation, we hypothesized that they could also enhance spatial deconvolution accuracy.

We integrated scDifformer-derived embeddings with a graph neural network, GraphSAGE [ [46](#B46) ], creating a hybrid GraphSAGE-scDifformer model (Fig. [5A](#F5) ; see "Materials and methods" section). To benchmark its performance, we generated simulated spatial transcriptomics (ST) data by gridding cell coordinates from a spatially resolved single-cell dataset [ [43](#B43) ], resulting in 253 artificial spots with known cell type compositions (Fig. [5B](#F5) ). We compared our model against seven established deconvolution methods spanning multiple algorithmic categories: probabilistic (RCTD [ [86](#B86) ], STRIDE [ [87](#B87) ], Cell2location [ [88](#B88) ]), deep learning (Tangram [ [89](#B89) ]), non-negative matrix factorization (SpatialDWLS [ [90](#B90) ]), graph-based approaches (DSTG [ [91](#B91) ], stdGCN [ [45](#B45) ]), and a spatial transcriptomics foundation model-based approach (scGPT-spatial [ [92](#B92) ]).

scDifformer in spatial transcriptome deconvolution. ( A ) Schematic overview of scDifformer-GraphSAGE for deconvolution of spatial transcriptomics data. We first randomly generated the pseudo-ST data with cell mixtures from scRNA-seq data. Based on the pseudo- and real-spots, we find the k -nearest neighbors for each spot using the expression matrix and construct a link graph containing all the spots. Based on the link graph, the graph convolutional network is utilized to propagate both pseudo- and real-spots data into the latent layer and discern the compositions of distinct cell types in each spot. The cell embeddings extracted from scDiffomer will be concatenated with the spot expression matrix as the input of GraphSAGE module. Finally, we get the spot-cell-type matrix containing the proportion of each cell type in each spot. ( B ) A spatially resolved single-cell transcriptome dataset from mouse cortex. Each dot represents a single cell, and colors represent different cell types. We grided these cells based on their coordinates to generate spots with known cell type composition. ( C, D ) Radar charts showing the ranking of deconvolution performance of different methods measured by PCC, SSIM, RMSE, and JSD. The outermost layer of a radar chart represents the best performance, including the highest PCC/SSIM and the lowest JSD/RMSE. Gridded spots of single-cell level mouse cortex sample (C) and simulated spatial transcriptome data from scRNA-seq of Human pancreas data (D) are used as test samples.

<!-- image -->

Performance was assessed using four complementary metrics: JSD, PCC, RMSE, and SSIM. GraphSAGE-scDifformer achieved the best scores in JSD (0.44), RMSE (0.15), and SSIM (0.57) (Fig. [5C](#F5) and [Supplementary Fig. S7](#sup1) ), indicating accurate reconstruction of spatial compositions. Notably, GraphSAGE-scDifformer also outperformed the specialized spatial transcriptomics foundation model scGPT-spatial in both spatial pattern identification and deconvolution accuracy ( [Supplementary Table S9](#sup1) ), further demonstrating the robustness and generalization capability of our framework for spatial transcriptomics analysis. To further validate generalizability, we conducted an additional benchmark on pancreas data using simulated spots generated via random sampling of cells. Our model outperformed the top three deconvolution tools from a prior benchmark (Fig. [5D](#F5) ) [ [42](#B42) ]. To isolate the contribution of the scDifformer embeddings, we compared the original GraphSAGE with the GraphSAGE-scDifformer variant. Across all four metrics, the integration of diffusion-derived embeddings led to significant improvements (Wilcoxon test, *P* &lt; .05; [Supplementary Fig. S8](#sup1) ), highlighting the utility of pretrained single-cell embeddings in spatial modeling tasks.

In summary, our results demonstrate that scDifformer embeddings, when integrated with a graph neural network framework (GraphSAGE), provide a powerful tool for improving spatial transcriptomics deconvolution. By leveraging the rich, context-aware representations learned from large-scale single-cell data, GraphSAGE-scDifformer significantly enhances the accuracy of cell type composition inference in spatial spots. Our model not only outperforms existing deconvolution methods across multiple performance metrics but also offers a novel approach for bridging the gap between single-cell and spatial transcriptomics, enabling more precise spatial resolution of cellular identities. These findings underscore the potential of scDifformer as a versatile model for both cell identity annotation and dynamic cellular modeling in spatially resolved data.

## Discussion

In this study, we developed scDifformer, a large-scale single-cell foundation model that integrates the transformer-based architecture of Geneformer with a diffusion probabilistic module via a novel post-training step. Leveraging an extensive pre-training corpus of 34.2 million human single-cell transcriptomes from publicly available databases including DISCO and CZ CELLxGENE, scDifformer surpasses existing models such as scBERT and Geneformer, both in scale and downstream annotation performance. The diffusion-enhanced architecture effectively reduces data-driven noise, refines learned representations, and accelerates convergence. Moreover, our attention-based interpretation revealed biologically meaningful gene signatures, linking top-ranked attention genes to enriched pathways consistent with known cell identities. Additionally, extending scDifformer embeddings to spatial transcriptomics through integration with the GraphSAGE graph neural network demonstrated superior performance in simulated spot-level deconvolution tasks. This innovative step represents the first successful application of diffusion modeling in spatial transcriptomics, suggesting a promising strategy for future integration of spatial and single-cell data.

Despite these advances, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, our current dataset primarily includes normal or steady-state cellular profiles, with limited representation of abnormal, perturbed, or disease-associated cells. This restricts the model's predictive capacity in pathological conditions, limiting its immediate applicability in areas such as disease modeling or therapeutic perturbation studies. Second, while our cross-tissue analysis validated performance using immune cells, a relatively well-defined cellular population, future studies should investigate more complex developmental processes, such as embryonic development or stem-cell differentiation pathways. Modeling these processes could substantially impact developmental biology and regenerative medicine, including advancements in artificial organ technologies. Third, although we demonstrated potential for integrating single-cell and spatial transcriptomics data, scDifformer is not inherently a multi-modal model. Comprehensive multi-modal frameworks should integrate additional data types, such as proteomics, epigenomics, and metabolomics, to fully capture cellular complexity and interactions at multiple molecular layers.

Addressing these limitations will guide our future research efforts. One critical direction is to expand training datasets to encompass diverse abnormal or perturbed cellular states, enhancing the model's ability to capture disease progression, cellular response to treatments, and dynamic reprogramming events. Additionally, the development of fully multi-modal foundation models, capable of jointly embedding transcriptomic, proteomic, and other molecular data, represents an exciting avenue to build next-generation virtual cells. Such models would offer deeper insights into cellular function and regulation, significantly advancing our capability to reconstruct accurate, context-aware biological simulations.
