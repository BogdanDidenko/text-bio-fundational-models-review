###### Abstract

Generative artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a powerful framework for drug discovery, yet most current approaches follow one-drug-one-gene targetbased paradigms that struggle to capture the complexity and heterogeneity of chronic and systemic diseases. Omics-driven systems pharmacology provides a promising strategy to overcome these limitations, but generative AI tools specifically designed for systems pharmacology-oriented drug design remain scarce. To address this gap, we introduce GEM-GPT, a transcriptomics-based molecule generation framework that designs personalized therapeutic compounds capable of reverting cell type-specific disease states back to a healthy phenotype. GEMGPTemploys a biology-inspired deep fusion architecture that couples a single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) foundation model with a molecular GPT model, enabling the modeling of cell type-specific chemical-gene interactions during molecule generation. This integration allows GEM-GPT to outperform stateof-the-art baselines, generate distinct molecules for different cell types, and generalize robustly to previously unseen cell types. We further demonstrate the utility of GEM-GPT through a case study in personalized drug discovery for opioid use disorder (OUD). In this application, GEM-GPT successfully identifies both therapeutic compounds possessing distinct chemotypes and existing FDAapproved drugs predicted to modulate cell type-specific OUD disease phenotypes in individual patients. Together, these results establish GEM-GPT as an advance in AI-driven systems pharmacology by bridging single-cell omics and molecular generation to support personalized, systems-aware therapeutic design.

Keywords: target-based drug discovery, phenotype drug discovery, precision medicine, generative AI, deep learning, machine learning, foundation model, single-cell RNA-seq

## 1 Introduction

Drug discovery and development are among the most resource-intensive and high-risk endeavors in biomedical science. A new drug requires on average 10-15 years and over $ 1-2 billion to progress from initial concept to regulatory approval [1]. Despite substantial investment in target identification, lead optimization, and preclinical validation, approximately 90% of drug candidates that enter clinical trials ultimately fail [1, 2]. A major contributor to this high failure rate is the continued reliance on the onedrug-one-target paradigm in modern drug discovery [3, 4]. Although this approach has yielded important breakthroughs, particularly in oncology where actionable mutations drive targeted therapies [5], it fundamentally oversimplifies the complexity of most human diseases [6]. Chronic and systemic conditions such as neurological disorders, autoimmune diseases, metabolic syndromes, and substance use disorders are inherently polygenic, multifactorial, and dependent on cellular and physiological context [3, 4]. In such diseases, modulation of a single molecular node rarely restores homeostasis across the broader biological network, which may contribute to the particularly high attrition rates observed in these therapeutic areas [7]. Systems pharmacology and network-based drug discovery have emerged as alternative frameworks that emphasize the coordinated modulation of disease-relevant biological networks rather than isolated molecular targets [3, 6]. Yet despite their conceptual and therapeutic potential, relatively few AI-driven computational tools have been developed to implement these principles systematically and at scale, particularly for the de novo design of novel therapeutic compounds.

Acomplementary and increasingly important goal in drug discovery is the design of therapies tailored to the individual patient. Inter-individual variability in disease biology, which arises from genetic background, epigenetic state, environmental exposures, and tissue-specific gene expression, means that population-level measures of drug response may obscure substantial differences in treatment efficacy and safety [8, 9]. The past decade has witnessed the rise of precision medicine, wherein patient-specific molecular profiles guide therapeutic decision-making [9]. However, precision drug design, defined here as the de novo generation of molecules tailored to a patient's specific molecular disease state, remains an unmet challenge. This limitation arises partly because conventional target-based approaches generally do not incorporate the cellular and transcriptomic context that distinguishes individual patients, and partly because computational frameworks capable of integrating patient-specific omics data with generative molecular design in a scalable manner remain limited.

The advent of transcriptomics and, more recently, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has substantially expanded our ability to characterize disease processes at cellular resolution. By profiling the gene expression of thousands of individual cells simultaneously, scRNA-seq reveals cell type-specific transcriptional programs that are invisible to bulk sequencing approaches, enabling identification of novel disease-relevant cell subpopulations, drug targets, and biomarkers [10]. This cellular resolution is particularly critical for studying heterogeneous diseases, in which distinct cell populations may exhibit different pathological states and responses to therapeutic perturbations [10]. The use of scRNA-seq for personalized and cell type-specific drug discovery, in which therapeutic molecules are designed to shift disease-associated cellular states toward healthy states, provides an alternative to conventional target-centric drug discovery [11, 12]. Because this approach does not require prior identification of a specific molecular target, it is particularly applicable to diseases with complex or incompletely understood molecular mechanisms, including many chronic and systemic conditions [11, 12]. Nevertheless, compared with target-based drug discovery, relatively few computational methods have been developed for systems pharmacology approaches centered on the modulation of cellular states [13-16], and the integration of single-cell transcriptomics with de novo molecular generation remains a largely open problem [16].

The emergence of foundation models pretrained on large-scale biological data has provided new computational tools for addressing this challenge. scRNA-seq foundation models [17, 18] have demonstrated the capacity to learn rich, generalizable representations of gene regulatory networks and cellular states through self-supervised learning, enabling strong transfer performance across a range of downstream biological tasks. In parallel, generative models for molecular design have advanced rapidly. Variational autoencoders [19], generative adversarial networks [20], transformer-based chemical language models [21, 22], and equivariant diffusion models [23] have each demonstrated the capacity to generate novel, drug-like molecules with desired physicochemical and biological properties, greatly accelerating the exploration of chemical space relative to traditional high-throughput screening [24]. Several recent works have begun to bridge transcriptomics and molecular generation. MolGAN [13] pioneered this direction by conditioning a generative adversarial network on bulk transcriptomic signatures to generate hit-like molecules with desired gene expression effects, demonstrating the feasibility of phenotype-guided molecular design. GxVAEs [14] extended this concept by employing two jointly trained variational autoencoders, one to extract latent features from bulk gene expression profiles and another to generate drug-like molecules conditioned on those features. GexMolGen [15] further advanced the field by adopting a cross-modal 'first-align-then-generate' strategy that leverages a scRNA-seq foundation model (specifically scGPT) to encode bulk gene expression signatures and align them with a scaffold-based molecular decoder, yielding hit-like molecules with improved structural similarity to known active compounds. Most recently, MolGeneE [16] made a critical step toward single-cell resolution by combining a cross-modal domain adaptation model to harmonize bulk and single-cell chemical transcriptomics data with a contrastive learning-based generative model, demonstrating that single-cell omics data can, in principle, guide molecular generation.

Despite these advances, existing transcriptomics-guided molecular generation methods retain several important limitations. First, MolGAN and GxVAEs rely exclusively on bulk transcriptomics data, primarily the L1000 database, which lacks the cellular resolution needed to distinguish disease-associated states across individual cell types and therefore limits the generation of molecules tailored to specific cell populations within a patient. Second, GexMolGen and MolGene-E employ two-stage frameworks in which transcriptomic and molecular representations are learned separately and subsequently aligned through contrastive learning. Because the integration of the two modalities is primarily achieved through representation alignment rather than continuous interaction throughout molecular generation, these approaches may be limited in their ability to capture cell-state-dependent chemical-gene relationships. Third, existing methods are not explicitly designed to preserve cell type-specific transcriptomic differences during molecular generation and have not systematically demonstrated that distinct cellular states lead to correspondingly distinct molecular outputs. Finally, none of these approaches has been evaluated for personalized drug discovery in patient-derived cohorts, nor have they demonstrated robust generalization to previously unseen cell types. These capabilities are important for extending transcriptomics-guided molecular generation to heterogeneous diseases and clinically relevant settings.

Given these limitations, none of these methods have been applied to single cellbased drug discovery. Opioid use disorder (OUD) provides a clinically important example of the need for personalized and cell type-specific therapeutic design. The ongoing opioid epidemic remains a major public health crisis, yet pharmacological treatment options for OUD remain limited, and relapse and inadequate treatment response are common [25]. Emerging single-cell transcriptomic studies have revealed that opioid exposure induces heterogeneous transcriptional changes across distinct neuronal and glial cell populations within brain reward circuits, and that these responses vary substantially across patients [26, 27]. This heterogeneity at both the patient and cell type levels suggests that uniform pharmacological strategies may not adequately address the diverse molecular states associated with OUD. Personalized therapeutic design based on individual, cell type-specific transcriptomic profiles may therefore provide a more direct approach for identifying compounds capable of modulating disease-associated cellular states.

Here we introduce GEM-GPT ( G ene E xpression-guided M olecular G enerative P re-trained T ransformer), a single cell transcriptomics-guided molecular generation framework designed to address these limitations through three main contributions. First, GEM-GPT employs a biology-inspired deep fusion strategy that integrates scRNA-seq foundation model representations with molecular token representations at each hidden layer of a unified generative architecture. This design enables continuous interaction between cellular-state and molecular representations during generation, supporting more detailed modeling of cell-state-dependent chemical-gene relationships than alignment-based approaches. Second, by conditioning molecular generation directly on cell type-aware disease-state representations derived from a pretrained single cell foundation model, GEM-GPT generates personalized and cell type-specific candidate molecules predicted to shift disease-associated transcriptional states toward healthy phenotypes. It produces distinct molecular outputs across cell types, generalizes to previously unseen cell types, and consistently outperforms existing state-of-the-art baselines across multiple evaluation settings. Third, through its application to OUD, GEM-GPT identifies diverse candidate molecules targeting cell type-specific disease states in individual patients, including FDA-approved drugs relevant to OUD, establishing a new paradigm for AI-driven, omics-based, personalized drug discovery.

## 2 Results

### 2.1 Overview of GEM-GPT

The overall architecture of GEM-GPT is shown in Fig. 1. GEM-GPT uses a GPTbased autoregressive architecture to generate SMILES strings conditioned on a desired transcriptional transition from a diseased cell state toward its healthy counterpart (Fig. 1a). During generation, a pretrained and frozen scRNA-seq foundation model encodes the paired diseased and healthy gene expression profiles into layer-specific transcriptomic representations. In this study, we use scGPT [18] because its celllevel embeddings capture cell type information directly from transcriptomic profiles and generalize across diverse single-cell datasets, enabling cell type-specific molecular generation without requiring explicit cell type annotations as input. The cell-state embedding derived from scGPT serves as the initial token embedding for molecular generation, providing explicit cell type-specific conditioning from the beginning of the autoregressive process.

Fig. 1b presents the detailed architecture of each GEM-GPT block. To effectively integrate transcriptomic and molecular information, we introduce a deep fused attention mechanism that couples the molecular hidden state with the corresponding scGPT hidden representations at every layer of GEM-GPT. Specifically, the molecular representation is separately fused with the scGPT representations of the diseased and healthy cell states. The difference between the resulting conditioned molecular representations is then propagated to the next GEM-GPT layer, allowing the desired disease-to-healthy transcriptional shift to guide each stage of molecular generation. This layer-by-layer integration incorporates transcriptomic information throughout the entire generation process, rather than only at the input level, and enables direct and repeated interactions between cellular-state and molecular representations.

GEM-GPT was compared with state-of-the-art transcriptomics-guided molecular generation baselines, including MolGAN [13], GxVAEs [14], GexMolGen [15], and MolGene-E [16]. We first evaluated whether GEM-GPT could generate hit-like molecules from compound-induced gene expression profiles in both seen and previously unseen cell lines. We then examined whether GEM-GPT could generalize across perturbation modalities by generating pharmacologically relevant molecules from CRISPR knock-out transcriptomic signatures. Finally, we applied GEM-GPT to single-cell transcriptomic profiles from patients with Opioid Use Disorder to identify personalized and cell type-specific therapeutic candidates, including molecules with distinct chemotypes and repurposable FDA-approved drugs. Together, these experiments evaluate the ability of GEM-GPT to learn generalizable transcriptometo-molecule relationships and support personalized, cell type-specific therapeutic design.

### 2.2 GEM-GPT enables cell type-specific hit-like molecule generation from gene expression profiles across seen and unseen cell lines

A central objective of GEM-GPT is to move beyond the conventional one-drugone-target paradigm by learning the relationship between chemical structures and cellular transcriptional phenotypes. Rather than conditioning molecular generation on an isolated molecular target, GEM-GPT uses gene expression profiles to represent systems-level cellular responses to chemical perturbations. We therefore first evaluated whether GEM-GPT could recover chemically meaningful molecules from compound-induced gene expression profiles while retaining information associated with different cell lines. This task provides a foundational benchmark for systems pharmacology-oriented molecular generation because successful performance requires the model to connect a transcriptional phenotype with the chemical structure responsible for inducing it, while generating distinct molecular patterns for different cellular populations.

We evaluated GEM-GPT under two complementary settings that separately assess generalization to unseen compounds and unseen cell lines. In the in-distribution (ID) cell-line setting, L1000 profiles from 3 cell lines were divided using an InChIKey-based compound split following the setting in [15] (Fig. 2a). The training, validation, and test sets contained the same cell lines but non-overlapping compounds, allowing us to evaluate whether GEM-GPT could generate molecules corresponding to previously unseen chemical perturbations in cell lines represented during training. In the outof-distribution (OOD) cell-line setting, profiles from 20 L1000 cell lines were divided using a cell-line-level split, with the test set consisting of cell lines that were not represented during training (Fig. 2c). This setting directly evaluates whether GEM-GPT can transfer its learned transcriptome-to-molecule relationship to previously unseen cell lines, which is an important requirement for cell type-specific and personalized drug design.

In the ID cell-line setting, GEM-GPT generated molecules with consistently greater similarity to their corresponding reference compounds than the baseline methods (Fig. 2b). Kernel density estimates of Morgan fingerprint-based Tanimoto similarity, Fraggle similarity, and MACCS fingerprint-based Tanimoto similarity were shifted toward higher values for GEM-GPT, indicating improved recovery of complementary structural characteristics of the reference molecules. The consistent improvement across fingerprint-based, fragment-based, and structural-key-based measures suggests that GEM-GPT captures a broader relationship between transcriptional phenotypes and chemical structures rather than learning similarities specific to a single molecular representation.

In the OOD cell-line setting, the UMAP visualization of control gene expression profiles revealed a distribution shift between the training and validation cell lines and the held-out test cell lines (Fig. 2d). The dashed contours delineating the training and validation cell lines illustrate how the held-out test cell lines occupy distinct regions of the transcriptional space. This distribution indicates that the OOD benchmark evaluates generalization to cellular populations excluded from model training rather than only interpolation among previously observed cell lines. Despite this distribution shift, GEM-GPT achieved the highest average similarities overall across the OODtest cell lines when evaluated using Morgan fingerprint-based Tanimoto, Fraggle, and MACCS fingerprint-based Tanimoto similarities (Fig. 2e). The heatmaps show that GEM-GPT consistently recovers complementary structural characteristics of the corresponding reference compounds across multiple similarity definitions and previously unseen cell lines. This performance demonstrates that GEM-GPT can translate transcriptional phenotypes from previously unseen cell lines into molecules that more closely resemble their corresponding reference compounds. This generalization is particularly important for systems pharmacology and personalized drug design, where disease-relevant cell types and cellular states may be absent or sparsely represented in available perturbational training datasets.

To assess cell type-specific molecular generation, we examined whether GEM-GPT produced molecular sets with greater shared molecular patterns within the same cell line than across different cell lines. We compared the mean Jaccard overlaps of molecular sets generated within the same cell line and between different cell lines in both the ID and OOD settings (Fig. 2f). Here, ∆ denotes the difference between withincell-line and between-cell-line Jaccard overlaps. High overlap in both conditions would indicate that a model collapses toward a common set of generic molecules regardless of cell-line context, as observed for GxVAEs in the OOD setting. Conversely, low overlap in both conditions would indicate that a model generates highly diverse molecular sets without capturing molecular patterns associated with individual cell lines, as observed for MolGAN and MolGene-E in both settings. GEM-GPT instead exhibited substantially higher within-cell-line than between-cell-line overlap, producing the largest separation between the two conditions in both settings, with ∆ = 0 . 051 in the ID setting and ∆ = 0 . 070 in the OOD setting. This pattern indicates that GEM-GPT preserves molecular consistency within each cell line while generating distinct molecular sets across different cell lines, providing cell-line-level evidence for cell type-specific molecular generation in both seen and previously unseen cell lines.

Together, these findings demonstrate that GEM-GPT learns a generalizable mapping between transcriptional phenotypes and chemical structures while preserving cell type-specific molecular patterns. The model not only generates molecules that more closely resemble the compounds associated with the input gene expression profiles, but also differentiates its generated chemical space across cell lines, including previously unseen cell lines.

### 2.3 GEM-GPT generates pharmacologically relevant molecules from CRISPR knock-out transcriptomic signatures

Since GEM-GPT is designed to support systems pharmacology by translating transcriptomic states into candidate molecules, we next investigated whether it could generate pharmacologically relevant molecules from genetically perturbed cellular states. CRISPR knock-out signatures capture the systems-level transcriptional consequences of gene loss of function and therefore provide a complementary benchmark to the compound-induced profiles evaluated above. This setting tests whether the relationship learned by GEM-GPT between transcriptomic states and chemical structures can generalize across chemical and genetic perturbation modalities. Known geneinhibitor relationships were used as controlled references to assess whether molecules generated from each gene knock-out signature resembled known inhibitors and were predicted to interact with the corresponding protein target.

We used the L1000-based dataset curated by [15] for model training and evaluated the models on 10 human genes with known inhibitors from the ExCAPE dataset: AKT1, AKT2, AURKB, CTSK, EGFR, HDAC1, MTOR, PIK3CA, SMAD3, and TP53. For each gene, 100 transcriptomic profiles from the CRISPR knock-out and corresponding control conditions were used to define the genetic perturbation signature and guide molecular generation. The generated molecules were subsequently compared with known inhibitors of the corresponding gene target.

We first evaluated the structural similarity between the generated molecules and known inhibitors using Morgan fingerprint-based Tanimoto similarity, Fraggle similarity, and MACCS key-based Tanimoto similarity. Across the 10 genes, GEMGPT generally generated molecules that were more similar to the corresponding known inhibitors than those produced by baseline models, MolGAN, MolGene-E, GexMolGen, and GxVAEs (Fig. 3a). This trend was consistently observed across fingerprint-based, fragment-based, and structural-key-based similarity measures, suggesting that GEM-GPT recovered complementary molecular characteristics associated with known inhibitors rather than features specific to a single molecular representation.

Since structural similarity to known inhibitors does not directly establish interaction with the intended protein target, we further evaluated the generated molecules using predicted binding probabilities from Boltz-2 [28]. To ensure that the binding predictions were performed in structurally well-defined and pharmacologically relevant settings, this analysis was restricted to targets classified as druggable enzymes with a single binding pocket, including AKT1, AKT2, AURKB, CTSK, EGFR, and HDAC1.

GEM-GPT generally generated molecules with higher predicted binding probabilities for these protein targets than the baseline methods (Fig. 3b). Together with the compound-based similarity results, these predictions suggest that GEM-GPTgenerated molecules contain inhibitor-associated structural features and molecular characteristics compatible with binding to structurally well-defined druggable targets.

Together, these results demonstrate that GEM-GPT can generate pharmacologically relevant molecules from genetically induced transcriptomic phenotypes. The consistent findings from ligand-based structural similarity and predicted protein binding support the ability of GEM-GPT to capture chemical-gene relationships encoded in transcriptional states. This experiment further extends GEM-GPT beyond compound-induced expression profiles, showing that its learned transcriptome-tomolecule mapping can transfer from chemical perturbations to genetic perturbations.

### 2.4 GEM-GPT generates personalized and cell type-specific therapeutic candidates for OUD using patient and healthy single-cell transcriptomic profiles

Having established that GEM-GPT can generalize across compounds, cell lines, and perturbation modalities, we next investigated whether it could support personalized and cell type-specific therapeutic design for a complex disease characterized by substantial inter-individual and cellular heterogeneity. We applied GEM-GPT to single-cell RNA-sequencing profiles obtained from individuals with opioid use disorder (OUD) and healthy individuals [29], focusing on four brain cell types: astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes (ODC), and oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPC). None of these cell types was represented in the L1000 training set, making this application a stringent evaluation of molecular generation from previously unseen cellular populations.

For each cell type, a healthy reference gene expression profile was constructed by averaging the corresponding single-cell profiles from healthy individuals, whereas a patient-specific OUD expression profile was constructed separately for each OUD patient using cells of the same annotated cell type. Each model input therefore consisted of a paired patient-specific OUD profile and a cell type-matched healthy reference profile. GEM-GPT conditioned molecular generation on the transcriptional transition from the patient-specific OUD state toward the corresponding healthy state, thereby producing candidate molecules specific to both the individual patient and cell type. The generated molecules were subsequently analyzed using the Similarity Ensemble Approach (SEA) [30] to search their potential protein targets. The resulting targets associated with OUD were then used to prioritize candidate molecules, connecting patient- and cell type-specific molecular generation with disease-relevant target networks (Fig. 4a).

To determine whether the generated chemical space reflected inter-patient heterogeneity, valid molecules were represented using Morgan fingerprints and grouped into 30 molecular clusters. For each patient and cell type, we calculated the proportion of generated molecules assigned to each cluster. Because each molecular set was generated from a paired patient-specific OUD profile and cell type-matched healthy reference profile, differences in the resulting chemical space reflect differences in the disease-to-healthy transcriptional transitions across patients. The resulting heatmaps showed distinct molecular cluster distributions among patients within the same cell type (Fig. 4b). Different patients exhibited enrichment in different clusters and distinct combinations of molecular patterns, indicating that GEM-GPT did not generate a uniform candidate distribution across the patient cohort. These patient-dependent molecular distributions demonstrate that GEM-GPT translates inter-patient heterogeneity in OUD-associated transcriptional states into personalized candidate chemical spaces.

We next evaluated whether GEM-GPT generated distinct molecular candidates for different cell types within the same patient. For each patient, we compared the Jaccard overlap between molecular sets generated from independent subsets of the same cell type with the overlap between molecular sets generated from different cell types. GxVAEs showed similar within-cell-type and cross-cell-type overlaps, with mean values of 0.055 and 0.054, respectively, resulting in only a minimal separation of ∆ = 0 . 001 (Fig. 4c). This pattern suggests that GxVAEs generated largely shared molecular sets across different cell types. In contrast, GEM-GPT showed a mean within-cell-type overlap of 0.044 and a substantially lower cross-cell-type overlap of 0.014, resulting in a markedly larger separation of ∆ = 0 . 030. The lower molecular overlap across cell types relative to that within the same cell type demonstrates that GEM-GPT responds to cell type-specific transcriptional states and generates distinct therapeutic candidate spaces for different cell types within individual OUD patients.

Finally, SEA-based target analysis identified generated candidates predicted to interact with OUD-related targets. Among these candidates, GEM-GPT identified 3 FDA-approved compounds related to OUD that were absent from the training set: naltrexone, naloxone, and clonidine (Fig. 4d). In contrast, GxVAE did not recover any FDA-approved drugs relevant to OUD under the same analysis. The generation of clinically relevant compounds without prior exposure during training suggests that GEM-GPT learned transferable relationships between transcriptional disease states and pharmacologically relevant chemical structures rather than simply memorizing known drugs. Together, these results demonstrate that GEM-GPT can translate patient- and cell type-level transcriptional heterogeneity into differentiated molecular candidate spaces while identifying clinically relevant FDA-approved drugs, providing a proof of concept for personalized systems pharmacology in OUD.

## 3 Discussion

In this study, we developed GEM-GPT, a transcriptomics-guided cell type-resolved molecular generation framework that integrates a pretrained single-cell foundation model with an autoregressive molecular language model through deep fused attention. GEM-GPT was designed to support systems pharmacology by generating cell typespecific candidate molecules from transitions between disease-associated and healthy cellular states, rather than conditioning generation on a predefined molecular target. Across compound-induced transcriptomic profiles, CRISPR knock-out signatures, and single-cell profiles from patients with opioid use disorder, GEM-GPT consistently generated molecules that retained information associated with the input transcriptional state while remaining applicable to previously unseen cellular populations. Together, these results demonstrate the potential of deeply integrating single-cell foundation representations with molecular generation for personalized and cell type-specific therapeutic design.

A central contribution of GEM-GPT is its unified modeling of transcriptomic and molecular representations during autoregressive generation. Existing transcriptomicsguided approaches [13-16] either condition generation on bulk expression representations or align transcriptomic and molecular latent spaces through separate training objectives. In contrast, GEM-GPT introduces transcriptomic information into every hidden layer of the molecular generator. At each autoregressive step, the molecular representation is independently fused with the healthy and diseased cellular-state representations, and the difference between the resulting representations is propagated to the next hidden layer. This architecture allows the desired disease-to-healthy transcriptional transition to guide the generation of every molecular token, rather than being compressed into a single fixed conditioning vector at the input level. The use of a frozen scGPT encoder further enables GEM-GPT to incorporate biological representations learned from large-scale single-cell data while limiting the number of parameters that must be optimized on comparatively smaller chemical perturbation datasets.

The compound-induced transcriptomic experiments demonstrate that GEM-GPT learns a transferable relationship between cellular transcriptional responses and molecular structures. The model generated molecules with greater similarity to their corresponding reference compounds in both seen and previously unseen cell lines, indicating that its performance was not limited to interpolation within the training cellular contexts. In addition, GEM-GPT showed a larger separation between within-cell-line and between-cell-line molecular overlaps than the baseline models. This pattern suggests that the model neither collapses toward a common set of generic molecules nor generates highly diverse molecules independently of cellular state, but instead preserves molecular patterns associated with individual cellular populations.

The CRISPR knock-out experiment further showed that the learned transcriptometo-molecule relationship generalized across perturbation modalities. Although GEMGPT was trained using compound-induced profiles, molecules generated from knockout signatures generally showed greater similarity to known inhibitors and higher Boltz-2 predicted binding probabilities for the corresponding druggable enzyme targets. These complementary results suggest that GEM-GPT captures pharmacologically relevant chemical information encoded in systems-level transcriptional responses.

The OUD case study extends GEM-GPT from controlled perturbation benchmarks to patient-derived single-cell transcriptomic data. By conditioning generation on patient-specific disease profiles and cell type-matched healthy references, GEM-GPT produced distinct molecular sets across both patients and OUD-related cell types. The identification of novel chemotypes and FDA-approved OUD-related drugs that are unseen in the training set illustrates how the framework could support de novo personalized cell type-resolved molecular design.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the quality of the generated molecules depends on the quality, coverage, and experimental consistency of the transcriptomic data. L1000 measures a restricted set of landmark genes, and its compound perturbation profiles are dominated by immortalized cell lines. These profiles may not fully represent transcriptional responses in primary tissues or disease-associated cell populations. Although the pretrained single-cell foundation model provides transferable cellular representations, it cannot completely eliminate the domain shift between L1000 cell lines and patient-derived single-cell data.

Second, the current evaluations primarily assess molecular validity, structural similarity to reference compounds or inhibitors, molecular-set differentiation, and predicted binding probability. These measurements provide useful evidence of chemical and pharmacological relevance, but they do not directly establish that the generated molecules reverse the intended transcriptional phenotype. Experimental perturbation assays will be required to determine whether the candidates induce the predicted disease-to-healthy transition. Integrating experimentally measured transcriptional reversal, target engagement, toxicity, and phenotypic activity into iterative model training would provide a stronger basis for therapeutic prioritization.

Third, GEM-GPT currently focuses on transcriptomic conditioning and molecular structure generation without explicitly optimizing multiple downstream drugdevelopment objectives. Drug-likeness, synthetic accessibility, selectivity, safety, metabolic stability, and tissue exposure are not directly incorporated into the generation objective. This limitation is particularly important for central nervous system disorders, where blood-brain barrier penetration and neurological safety strongly influence therapeutic feasibility. Future extensions could combine transcriptomic conditioning with multi-objective optimization, reinforcement learning, uncertainty estimation, and structure-based evaluation to prioritize candidates with more favorable development profiles.

Future work should also investigate the integration of additional biological modalities. Genetic, epigenomic, proteomic, and spatial information could provide complementary descriptions of disease-associated cellular states and improve the biological specificity of molecular generation. Larger single-cell perturbation datasets would allow GEM-GPT to be trained directly on cell type-resolved chemical responses rather than transferring primarily from bulk L1000 data. In addition, systematic experimental validation across patient-derived cells, organoids, and animal models will be necessary to establish whether transcriptomics-guided molecular generation can produce compounds with reproducible therapeutic effects.

Overall, GEM-GPT provides a step toward connecting virtual representations of cellular states with generative molecular design. By conditioning each stage of molecular generation on the difference between healthy and diseased transcriptomic states, the framework moves beyond target-centric molecule generation and instead focuses on systems-level phenotype modulation. The results across seen and unseen cell lines, genetic perturbations, and patient-derived OUD profiles suggest that this strategy can support both generalizable and personalized molecular generation. Further development and experimental validation may enable GEM-GPT to serve as a computational platform for cell type-specific therapeutic discovery in complex diseases.

Overall, GEM-GPT provides a unified framework that connects transcriptomic representations of healthy and diseased cellular states with generative molecular design for systems pharmacology. By conditioning every hidden layer of the molecular generator on the transition from a disease-associated state toward a healthy state, GEM-GPT moves beyond predefined target-based design and instead focuses on systems-level transcriptional phenotype modulation. The results across compound-induced profiles, genetic perturbations, previously unseen cell types, and patient-derived OUD data demonstrate that this strategy can support generalizable, personalized, and cell typespecific molecular generation. Particularly in the OUD case study, the generation of distinct candidate spaces across patients and cell types, together with the identification of FDA-approved OUD-related drugs absent from the training set, highlights the potential of GEM-GPT as a computational platform for personalized and cell type-specific therapeutic discovery in complex and heterogeneous diseases.

### 4.1 GEM-GPT

#### 4.1.1 Overall architecture

GEM-GPT is a transcriptomics-conditioned autoregressive molecular generation framework designed to generate candidate compounds that shift a disease-associated cellular state toward a corresponding healthy state. Given a healthy or perturbed profile G H and diseased or control gene expression profile G D , GEM-GPT generates a molecular SMILES sequence s = ( s 1 , s 2 , . . . , s T ). As shown in Fig. 1, the architecture of GEM-GPT consists of a pretrained scRNAseq foundation model, scGPT, and a GPT-based molecular generator. The healthy and diseased gene expression profiles are independently encoded by scGPT to obtain transcriptomic representations. The molecular generator then autoregressively predicts SMILES tokens while being conditioned on the desired transition between the two cellular states. The cell state embedding derived from scGPT is used as the initial embedding for molecular generation, allowing the generated molecular sequence to be conditioned on cellular state from the beginning of the autoregressive process.

The parameters of scGPT are frozen throughout model training to preserve the biological representations learned from large-scale single-cell transcriptomic data. The parameters of the molecular generator and the newly introduced fusion modules are trainable. Unlike two-stage approaches [15, 16] that independently encode transcriptomic and molecular modalities before aligning their latent representations, GEM-GPT directly integrates transcriptomic information into each layer of the molecular generator. This unified architecture allows transcriptomic and molecular representations to interact repeatedly throughout SMILES generation.

#### 4.1.2 Autoregressive molecular generation and model training

Molecules are represented as SMILES strings and tokenized using the character-level vocabulary. Each sequence is surrounded by beginning-of-sequence and end-ofsequence tokens and is padded to a maximum sequence length of 80 tokens.

During training, GEM-GPT receives paired control and compound-perturbed expression profiles together with the SMILES representation of the perturbing compound. The perturbed profile is treated as the state induced by the reference molecule, while the corresponding control profile represents the cellular state before perturbation. The model is trained to predict each SMILES token conditioned on the preceding molecular tokens and the paired transcriptional states.

During inference, molecules are generated autoregressively using beam search. Generation terminates when the end-of-sequence token is produced or when the maximum sequence length is reached. The generated sequences are decoded into SMILES strings and processed using RDKit. SMILES strings that cannot be parsed or sanitized are treated as invalid. Valid molecules are converted to canonical SMILES for downstream analyses.

### 4.2 Experiments

#### 4.2.1 Hit-like molecule generation from compound-induced transcriptomic profiles

For the in-distribution cell-line setting, we used the dataset curated by [15]. The dataset contained compound-induced profiles from VCAP, PC3, A375 cell lines. Samples were divided into training, validation, and test sets using a compound InChIKey-based split. Compounds in the test set were excluded from the training and validation sets, whereas the same cell lines were represented across the three splits. GEM-GPT was compared with MolGAN, GxVAEs, GexMolGen, and MolGeneE under the same data split. Each model generated the same number of candidate molecules for each test expression profile.

For the out-of-distribution cell-line setting, compound-induced profiles from 20 L1000 cell lines were collected. The 5 cell lines with the largest numbers of available profiles, A375, A549, HT29, MCF7, and PC3, were used for model development. Training and validation samples were constructed exclusively from these 5 cell lines. The remaining 15 cell lines were excluded from model training and used as the held-out test set. GEM-GPT was compared with MolGAN, GxVAEs, and MolGene-E. Each method received expression profiles processed using the same gene set and generated the same number of molecular candidates per input profile.

#### 4.2.2 Molecule generation from CRISPR knock-out transcriptomic signatures

GEM-GPT and the baseline models (MolGAN, GxVAEs, GexMolGen, and MolGeneE) were trained on compound-induced L1000 profiles curated by [15] and were evaluated on CRISPR knock-out signatures without additional training on gene knockout data. CRISPR knock-out and corresponding control profiles were curated by [15] from the L1000 dataset. 10 human genes with known inhibitors in the ExCAPE database were selected: AKT1, AKT2, AURKB, CTSK, EGFR, HDAC1, MTOR, PIK3CA, SMAD3, and TP53. For each gene, 100 CRISPR knock-out profiles and 100 corresponding control profiles were used.

Protein-ligand binding probability was further investigated using Boltz-2. This analysis was restricted to knocked-out genes whose corresponding protein products were classified as druggable enzyme targets with a single well-defined binding pocket. The included targets were AKT1, AKT2, AURKB, CTSK, EGFR, and HDAC1. Each pair of protein sequence and the corresponding generated molecule was fed into Boltz-2 for binding probability prediction.

#### 4.2.3 Personalized and cell type-specific therapeutic design for OUD

We applied GEM-GPT to single-cell transcriptomic profiles from patients with opioid use disorder (OUD) to evaluate personalized and cell type-specific therapeutic generation. The single-cell RNA-seq dataset was obtained by [29] and included patientderived and healthy samples. The analysis focused on the 4 brain cell types covered in the dataset: astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes (ODC), and oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPC).

For each patient and cell type, gene expression profiles were aggregated across cells using pseudobulk averaging. A healthy reference profile was constructed from all healthy individuals' cells of the same cell type. GEM-GPT generated up to 1,000 molecular candidates for each pair of profiles. GxVAEs [14], which is the bestperforming baseline in our other experiments, was evaluated using the same inputs and generation scale. All generated SMILES strings were processed using the same RDKit-based validity, sanitization, and canonicalization procedure.

Cell type-specific molecular generation was examined by comparing molecular sets generated for different cell types or the same cell type within the same patient. Random splitting was repeated five times to reduce variation caused by a single partition.
