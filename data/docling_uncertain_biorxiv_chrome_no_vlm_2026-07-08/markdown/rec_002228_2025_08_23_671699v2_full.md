###### Abstract

Pre-trained language models promise to enrich analyses of single-cell data with additional layers of information leveraging large text corpora. Yet, it is still unclear how to achieve optimal alignment with the primary quantitative single-cell data. To address this, we construct text-based training datasets from both scRNA-seq data and biomedical literature targeted to the experimental setting at hand. We then jointly train language models on both information sources to learn a common, literature-enriched representation. Our examples on functionality, disease associations, and temporal trajectories show the potential of knowledge-augmented embeddings as a generalizable and interpretable strategy for enriching single-cell analysis pipelines.

Keywords: Single-cell RNA sequencing, Encoder-only language models, Text-based embeddings, Biological knowledge integration

## 1 Introduction

In single-cell data analysis, an increasing number of foundation models has recently become available [1], i.e., models that have been pre-trained on a huge amount of data. This includes models trained directly on quantitative single-cell profiles (e.g.,[2, 3]), and language models trained on biomedical literature, (e.g.,[4, 5, 6]), which contains rich contextual knowledge about cellular programs and gene function.

While both types of foundation models have been predominantly investigated as primary end-toend alternatives to classical quantitative single-cell analysis workflows [7, 8, 9, 10, 11], they also offer an opportunity to complement the original data and augment standard analyses. In particular, language models trained on scientific literature could add layers of qualitative biological information to the original single-cell data by embedding qualitative descriptors, such as functional programs, disease associations, or developmental stage information, in a joint lower-dimensional representation. Yet, it remains unclear how to best leverage such textual knowledge to enrich analyses of a specific dataset of interest [11].

A natural approach for using language models with single-cell data is to convert single-cell omics data to 'cell sentences' [8, 12], which represent each cell as a ranked list of its most highly expressed genes. We hypothesize that this approach also enables the integration of dataset-specific metadata available from increasingly complex and multi-layered datasets [13, 14, 15] and related contextual information retrieved from scientific literature. Yet, to achieve this, text- and expression-derived information needs to be combined. Here, we explore a contrastive alignment strategy to learn a joint embedding space in which expression profiles and literature-encoded knowledge are directly comparable.

This alignment is achieved through the training of relatively small encoder-only language models [16, 17], which combine a number of potential advantages for the task at hand. They are flexible in terms of task-specific fine-tuning, allow for straightforward integration of all text-based data sources and can be trained to generate semantically meaningful representations that are inherently compatible with well established downstream analysis of single-cell data.

Current research for the application of pre-trained language models for single-cell data focuses mainly on large language models (LLMs) that can be employed for diverse primary analysis tasks such as cell-type annotation, batch integration, and perturbation prediction. For example, C2S-Scale [8] uses LLMs like Pythia [18], which are additionally trained on cell sentences to support both traditional single-cell tasks and novel applications like dataset interpretation via natural language queries. Other approaches leverage general-purpose LLMs (e.g., GPT-3.5) to create cell embeddings based on expression-weighted gene vectors [9, 19], or for cluster-based annotation [7]. These approaches

Approaches that combine the strengths of both quantitative and text-based data representation strategies leverage language models for text-based interaction with the quantitative data. For example, joint embedding spaces shared between transcriptomic models such as Geneformer [3] or scGPT [2] and literature-trained models like BioBERT [20] integrate raw expression data with textual knowledge by allowing user queries to be matched directly to cells via semantic similarity [4, 21, 22]. However, scientific literature is typically only incorporated implicitly during general pre-training, and models do not yet leverage literature-based knowledge specific to the dataset of interest for downstream analysis and interpretation [11].

Therefore, we propose a more targeted approach for taking information from biomedical literature into account, specifically focusing on smaller task-specific language models for adding layers of information to the original single-cell data. In particular, we train small encoder-only language models [16, 17] to align gene expression profiles with biomedical literature, learning a shared semantic embedding space that integrates both data-derived and text-derived information. To build text-based datasets with a consistent structural configuration from both scRNA-seq data- and literature-derived information, we construct cell sentences and collect biomedical literature retrieved from PubMed [23] based on available metadata. Each cell sentence is paired with a similar and a dissimilar sentence to form a triplet [24, 25], and corresponding triplets are constructed from PubMed titles and abstracts. This triplet structure allows for efficient alignment of literature-derived and scRNA-seq-based cell sentences using contrastive loss functions to capture semantic relationships in the embedding [26, 27].

We demonstrate how this unified representation, where single-cell profiles, annotations, and textual descriptions are embedded within a shared coordinate system, provides a context-aware and interpretable representation. By projecting biological concepts expressed in natural language into this representation, we demonstrate how the trained models can transfer contextual knowledge from literature to datasets, and enable flexible annotation, e.g. for exploring cellular states across functional, disease-related, and developmental contexts.

## 2 Results

### 2.1 A unified workflow to embed single-cell profiles in the context of biomedical knowledge

To integrate single-cell gene expression profiles and complementary biomedical knowledge, we developed a novel approach that embeds single-cell profiles and literature-derived information into a shared semantic space (Fig. 1). This enables direct comparison between individual cells and biomedical concepts expressed in textual form, thus adding layers of contextual information such as function, disease association, or developmental state to the data.

Our approach builds on scRNA-seq datasets that have been processed by a conventional analysis workflow (Fig. 1 left) with annotated cell types, e.g., based on cluster-specific differential gene expression analysis [28, 29]. Starting from the gene expression count matrix(Fig. 1, Step 1), we transformed the top expressed highly-variable genes of each cell into a list of gene symbols and concatenate them to cell sentences. In addition to the gene list, metadata can be added to the cell sentence to allow for incorporating additional, dataset-specific contextual information (e.g., on donor or cell types).

In parallel, we built a literature-derived training dataset by retrieving titles and abstracts of research articles stored in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) PubMed database [23] that are relevant to the organism, cell types, or developmental stages represented in the scRNAseq data. This allows for capturing complementary biological knowledge not explicitly encoded in the gene expression profiles, such as cell-type functionality, disease relationships, or developmental trajectories. We thus obtained a gene expression-derived and a literature-derived dataset in textual format, facilitating direct embedding by a language model into a shared space.

To align embeddings of both datasets based on their semantic relationship, we used a labelaware triplet strategy for fine-tuning a language model. Specifically, we constructed triplets within the expression-derived and the literature-derived datasets, consisting of an anchor, a positive and a negative sample (Fig. 1, Step 1, bottom). Cell-type annotations were used as labels for triplet generation, i.e., positive samples were chosen randomly from all data samples with the same label, while data samples with a different label but high cosine similarity to the anchor were chosen as negatives (Methods Section 5.6). On these triplets, we fine-tuned a small encoder-only language model with a Siamese-BERT architecture [17] (Methods Section 5.5), which is specifically designed to efficiently compute similarity of multiple inputs (Fig. 1, Step 2). ### 2.2 Designing the joint training on literature and gene expression data to be consistent with cell-type labels

With our label-aware training strategy, we aim to preserve consistency and ensure label alignment across gene-expression- and literature-derived training data. To evaluate the effectiveness of this approach, we investigated the alignment of cell sentences with their cell-type labels on a T cell-specific subset of a primary blood mononuclear cell atlas, specifically the Human Immune Health atlas from the Allen Institute (HIAI dataset) [13]. The embedding of the base language model before fine-tuning showed poor separation of cell subtypes in a UMAP representation, where embeddings of cell-type labels formed a separate cluster distant from the corresponding single-cell embeddings (Fig. 2 A 1)). Next, we trained a model on a literature-based dataset only, using the cell-type annotation in the HIAI dataset as queries. We observed improved alignment between cell-type labels and the corresponding single-cell profiles but no clear clustering by subtype (Fig. 2 A 2)). Finally, fine-tuning the model on both literature- and expression-based datasets resulted in an embedding with distinct cell clusters by subtype, with their respective cell-type label embedded within those clusters (Fig. 2 A 3)). This indicates that fine-tuning was effective for differentiating cells of different types based on their top expressed genes. Additionally, we observed meta-clusters of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and a tendency of cells with similar functionality to also cluster together in the embedding, such as memory CD8+ T cells and γδ T cells, both characterized through strong cytotoxic capabilities [31, 32]. This is supported by assessment of marker gene expression in the model embeddings (see Additional file 1, Fig. S2), confirming that the model embeddings capture biologically meaningful marker gene expression patterns while preserving within-cell-type heterogeneity.

### 2.3 Capturing cell-type functionality programs unsupervised and at singlecell level

Training our approach jointly on literature- and cell-derived datasets enables analyses with respect to broader biological knowledge captured in the scientific literature. On the HIAI T cell dataset, we investigated whether the model captures cell-type functionalities reflected in literature-derived descriptions.

We used expert-curated functionality descriptions (Fig. 3 A ) for each cell type and computed cosine similarity between these descriptions and the different cell-type labels. We refer to this as cell type-level comparison. The similarity scores shown in Fig. 3 B indicate that our approach accurately captured different cell-type functionalities. We also observed higher similarity between functionality descriptions that share biological context. For example, MAIT cells can be described to 'bridge innate and adaptive immunity with semi-invariant TCR and rapid cytokine output.' [33], a function also characteristic of the V δ 2 subset of γδ T cells [31]. This is reflected by higher similarity scores between functionality descriptions of MAITs for γδ T cells compared to all other unrelated T cell subtypes, indicating the model's ability to capture shared functional programs beyond cell-type labels.

The alignment of cell-type labels and single cells in the embedding space also allows for transferring these functionality descriptions to individual cells: We used cosine similarity between embeddings of individual cells and functionality descriptions to assess how well the similarity scores can separate cells of a given type from all others. We refer to this as cell-level comparison. For example, similarity scores matching the description of γδ T cells separated this subset from all other cells with a ROCAUC score of 0.984, while MAIT cells with a ROC-AUC of 0.522 could not be distinguished. To compare our approach to existing single-cell foundation models and general-purpose LLMs, we utilized both comparison strategies (cell-based and cell-type-based). For the cell-level comparison, we compared our approach to CellWhisperer [21], an omics foundation model approach that allows for computing the similarity of cell embeddings to text. For the cell type-level comparison, we compare our approach to general-purpose LLMs, including Llama3.3 70B [34] and Qwen3 235B [35], where we used a pairwise decision strategy with a rank-based evaluation (described in Methods Section 5.10.3) to obtain similarity relations between functionality descriptions and cell types. Both comparison strategies yield association scores (similarity-based for the cell-type-level comparison and ROC-AUC-based for the cell-level comparison). These scores were employed to compare the different approaches based on the assigned ranks of the ground-truth cell-type label associated with each functionality description shown in Fig. 3 C . We found that our small encoder-only models achieved comparable performance to LLMs in the cell type-level comparison, and outperformed CellWhisperer in the cell-level comparison strategy.

Next, we employed the contextualized embeddings of the scRNA-seq data to identify overarching functional programs that span multiple cell types, such as cytotoxicity. We calculated similarity scores between each cell embedding and the textual description 'cytotoxic' and classify cells with a similarity &gt; 0 . 75 as cytotoxic cells (Fig. 3 D ). Differential gene expression analysis comparing these cells with all others (Fig. 3 E ) revealed that the top five differentiable expressed genes are well-established cytotoxicity markers [36]. This illustrates that our approach supports functional annotation directly from text, thus enriching single-cell datasets with interpretable biological context.

We have shown that this embedding provides a semantically meaningful, unified representation of information from both data sources that captures not only cell identities but also functional programs, disease associations, and developmental trajectories.

In particular, we have applied our approach to single-cell RNA-seq datasets including human immune and developing mouse brain data to evaluate its ability to integrate literature-derived context across biological systems. The results demonstrate that the framework can meaningfully connect gene expression profiles of individual cells with textual descriptors of their biological roles and function, producing a semantically coherent space that links data-driven and literature-based representations.

In a T cell dataset, the model accurately aligned cell-type labels with their corresponding gene expression profiles and captured shared functional programs, such as cytotoxicity, consistent with known immune phenotypes. When incorporating metadata describing disease status, the embeddings reflected phenotypic alterations in CMV-positive individuals, recovering known cytotoxic shifts in CD4+ T cells. In developing brain data, the inclusion of temporal metadata enabled the model to reconstruct developmental progressions consistent with established lineage trajectories, demonstrating its capacity to encode continuous biological transitions for studying dynamic processes such as differentiation.

Integrating gene expression data and literature through a shared text modality addresses a limitation in current single-cell foundation models. Previous approaches that use LLMs to embed gene identities or metadata [2, 19] often do not incorporate the rich contextual knowledge in the biomedical literature. By leveraging this information, our approach enables interpretability and hypothesis generation across biological layers, supporting downstream tasks such as context-aware cell-type annotation and the transfer of functional and disease-related knowledge into single-cell analyses. Our current implementation relies on relatively lightweight models (110M parameters), which can be efficiently run and fine-tuned even with limited hardware and are adaptable to new datasets with known cell types. Although our framework is based on compact encoder-only models, the results illustrate more broadly that adding contextual annotations represents a promising application for language models, including also large-scale language models. Our results indicate that existing generalist LLMs already capture aspects of biological metadata, such as relationships between cell types and their functionality, suggesting that further exposure to gene expression data could be a promising strategy to improve their applicability to genomic data analysis.

Nonetheless, the current implementation has important limitations. First, our literature-derived data relies solely on PubMed titles and abstracts, which, while information-dense, omit details found only in full-text articles and may under-represent newly described or rare cell types. ## 4 Conclusion

Our approach demonstrates the utility of encoder-only language models as lightweight tools for enriching single-cell RNA-seq data with information from biomedical literature. Showcasing how expression profiles and literature-derived knowledge can be aligned in a joint embedding space, our approach enables interpretable analyses across multiple biological layers such as cell identity, function, disease association, and developmental stage across biomedical contexts. This strategy offers a scalable and efficient framework for enriching single-cell analyses employing language models as complementary analysis tool, in contrast to other approaches that place language models at the core of single-cell data analysis. The work thus contributes to ongoing efforts to tailor LLMs to complex biological datasets to enable interpretable and context-aware single-cell-based analyses.

### 5.1 Preprocessing of single-cell RNA seq data

#### 5.1.1 Human Immune Health Atlas (Allen Institute)

Weused a subset of the Human Immune Health Atlas from the Allen Institute (HIAI) containing around 230,000 T cells [13]. Cell-type annotations are provided at three levels of resolution; we used the intermediate annotation, resulting in 10 distinct T cell subtypes. We preprocessed the data using scanpy , according to the workflows described in Heumos, Schaar, et al. [28]. Cells were retained if they expressed more than 500 and less than 5000 genes, and genes were retained if detected in at least 50 cells. After normalization and log transformation of the counts, we selected the top 2000 highly variable genes ('seurat\_v3' method), excluding ribosomal and mitochondrial genes. Data was split into training (50%) and test (50%) sets resulting in around 115,000 cells in both training and test dataset, with the training set further divided into a training (90%) and validation (10%) set. We refer to this dataset as the HIAI dataset .

#### 5.1.2 Developing Mouse Brain dataset (LaManno et al.)

As a second dataset, we used the developing mouse brain atlas containing around 270,000 cells at 20 embryonic time points E7-E18 La Manno, Gyllborg, et al. [37]. We only included cell types with more than 3 cells in the dataset, resulting in 130 of the 133 original cell types being included in the dataset. We removed cells with less than 100 expressed genes and genes expressed in less than five cells. We performed normalization and log transformation and selected the top 2000 highly variable genes ('seurat\_v3'), excluding ribosomal and mitochondrial genes. We split the data into a training and test (10%) set, with the training set subdivided into a training (90%) and validation (10%) set. We refer to this dataset as the LaManno dataset .

### 5.4 Base model

All sentence embedding and fine-tuning tasks of our approach were initialized from PubMedBERT ( neuml/pubmedbert-base-embeddings , available via Hugging Face) as base model, a domainspecific Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) model trained on Pubmed titles and abstracts [16, 41]. The base PubMedBERT model is a 12-layer bidirectional Transformer encoder with 110M parameters, and outputs 768-dimensional embeddings.

The original PubMedBERT model has been trained on a large-scale corpus of parsed and indexed PudMed titles and abstracts (via the paperetl pipeline), with training pairs created from combinations of titles and corresponding abstracts or semantically similar titles. Training used the Multiple Negatives Ranking Loss (for details, see below in Section 5.7) with mean pooling for token aggregation, which encourages semantically similar pairs to be closer in embedding space. PubMedBERT outperforms general-purpose embedding models on biomedical semantic similarity tasks (e. g., PubMedQA, PubMed Subset, PubMed Summary).

This pre-trained encoder served as the backbone for all model fine-tuning experiments in this study.

### 5.5 Siamese Sentence BERT architecture

To enable efficient similarity computations, we used the pre-trained PubMedBERT model within a Sentence-BERT (SBERT) framework [17], a modification of BERT designed for efficient semantic similarity in sentence-pair tasks, such as aligning cell sentences of similar cells. In contrast to standard BERT, SBERT encodes each sentence independently using a siamese or triplet architecture [42]. A mean pooling layer produces a fixed-size (768-dimensional) token embedding, which enables fast pairwise similarity computation using metrics such as cosine similarity. This architecture reduces the number of model passes required for pairwise comparisons [17], making it well suited for large-scale comparison of cell sentences and literature-derived information.

SBERT supports training with contrastive objectives such as triplet loss functions, contrastive loss functions, and multiple negatives ranking (MNR) loss functions, which we employed in our training procedure (see Section 5.7 for details). Because the model is designed to cluster semantically similar sentences in embedding space, it is well suited for tasks like semantic alignment and clustering.

### 5.8 Fine-tuning of models using the sentence-transformer package

We fine-tuned the sentence embedding models using the sentence-transformers Python package, which provides efficient training routines and tools for contrastive learning with sentence embeddings. Fine-tuning was performed either on literature-derived text alone or jointly on both literature-derived text and cell-derived cell sentences. For joint optimization, we alternated epoch-wise between the two datasets to avoid gradient interference during training.

Training was performed with batch size 128 for both literature- and scRNA-seq derived data. We trained for 5 epochs, with 1000 warm-up steps, a weight decay of 0.01, mixed precision ( fp16=True ) to reduce memory usage, and gradient clipping with max\_grad\_norm=1.0 to avoid instability.

### 5.10 Evaluation of contextualization of scRNA-seq data with different layers of information

#### 5.10.1 Contextualization with functionality descriptions

To evaluate whether model embeddings capture functionality- and disease-related context, we employed expert-curated statements describing the general functionality of the cell type. We computed cosine similarities between single cell embeddings or cell-type label embeddings and the functionality and disease-related descriptions, and compared the resulting scores to the expert-derived matching of the descriptions to the cell types. We refer to this cell-type label-based assessment as cell type-based comparison level.

Additionally, we computed similarity scores between the embeddings of all cells' ranked gene lists and each functionality description. For a given cell type, we compared the distributions of the similarity scores for cells of the respective cell type with the scores of all other cells. By varying the decision threshold over the similarity scores, we generated ROC curves that quantify how well cells of the target type can be discriminated from the rest of the dataset based on their similarity scores to the given functionality description. The resulting area under the ROC curve thus provides a measure of the association between a functionality description and each cell type, reflecting how distinctively the description matches the cells' embeddings. Ranking the AUC values across cell types for a given description identifies the cell type most strongly associated to that functionality.

We refer to this strategy as the cell-based comparison level.

#### 5.10.3 Cell-type-based comparison using generative language models

For comparing our approach to general-purpose generative language models, we used the cell-typebased comparison strategy. To obtain ranked associations of cell types to functionality descriptions from generative LLMs, we employed a pair-wise comparison strategy, where LLMs were tasked with systematically matching cell-type labels to the functionality descriptions. Specifically, we used the following prompt for querying Llama3.3 70B [34] and Qwen3 235B [35] via the OpenRouter interface [46]:

' You are a biomedical expert specializing in T cell immunology.

Functional description: {description}

Option A: {celltype\_a}

Option B: {celltype\_b}

Question: Which of these two T cell types most likely performs the described function? Please respond ONLY with one of the following exactly:

A

B

Do not add any other text. '

For each functionality description, all possible ordered pairs of cell-type labels were presented to the model, i.e., each pair was presented in both possible orders to eliminate potential position bias in the model's responses. Each model choice was recorded as a 'win' for the selected cell type. Across all pairs, the total number of wins per cell type was normalized by the maximum attainable number of wins and converted into a rank score. These ranks represent the LLM-derived association between functionality descriptions and cell-type labels.

For each functionality description, we recorded the rank position assigned by the model to the expert-assigned 'true' cell type for that description (14 in total). Lower ranks indicate better alignment (1 = perfect). We then visualized the distribution of these ranks across descriptions to evaluate performance and compared these rank positions to those obtained with our encoder-based approach.
