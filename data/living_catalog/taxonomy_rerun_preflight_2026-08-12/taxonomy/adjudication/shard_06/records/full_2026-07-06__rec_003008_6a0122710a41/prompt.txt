## Aligning LLMs with Biomedical Knowledge using Balanced Fine-Tuning

Zhenchao Tang 1† , Fang Wang 1†* , Haohuai He 1 , Jiale Zhou 1 , Tianxu Lv 1 , Jun Zhu 1 , Shouzhi Chen 1 , Minghao Yang 1 , Yu Wang 1 , Jiayang Wu 1 , Yidong Song 1 , Jianhua Yao 1 ∗

1. Tencent AI for Life Sciences Lab, Shenzhen, China.

† Equal contribution * Corresponding author

### Abstract

Effective post-training is essential to align Large Language Models (LLMs) with specialized biomedical knowledge to accelerate life science research. However, current approaches face significant limitations. First, biomedical reasoning involves intricate mechanisms often represented by sparse textual data. Standard Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) tends to overfit to surface-level instruction patterns without effectively internalizing this fragmented scientific knowledge. Second, Reinforcement Learning (RL) is impractical for this domain, as defining meaningful rewards often necessitates prohibitive experimental validation (e.g., wet-lab verification of drug responses), rendering real-time feedback unfeasible. We propose Balanced Fine-Tuning (BFT), an efficient post-training method designed to learn complex reasoning from sparse data without external reward signals. BFT operates through a two-layer weighting mechanism: 1. At the token level, it scales loss via prediction probabilities to stabilize gradients and prevent overfitting; 2. At the sample level, it uses 'minimum group confidence' to adaptively enhance the learning of hard samples. Experiments demonstrate that BFT significantly outperforms SFT. In medical tasks, it enables LLMs to acquire knowledge that SFT misses. In biological tasks, BFT-based LLMs surpass GeneAgent (an accurate agent for biology analysis) in biological process reasoning. Moreover, the text embeddings generated by BFT can be directly applied to downstream tasks, such as gene interaction and single-cell perturbation response prediction. These results indicate that BFT facilitates broad applications of LLMs in biomedical research.

### 1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have achieved remarkable success across general domains, with supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL) becoming standard components of the post-training pipeline [1]. However, biomedical science involves complex reasoning over sparse and fragmented knowledge. Developing effective post-training methods for aligning LLMs with biomedical knowledge is therefore crucial-not only because LLMs can directly accelerate biomedical discovery, but also because they can inherit and extend the most recent advances in Artificial General Intelligence (AGI), paving the way for future intelligent agents in life sciences. Yet, the scarcity of high-quality biomedical data, the complexity of scientific reasoning, and the high cost of current post-training approaches have made it difficult for LLMs to generalize effectively to this domain [2].

Although SFT is a simple and effective post-training method that performs well in general tasks, it often faces the risk of overfitting in complex biomedical reasoning. The gradients of SFT may become unstable, leading to degraded training performance [3, 4]. Biomedical reasoning involves highly intricate causal and regulatory mechanisms, yet the available textual data are extremely sparse-for example, only a fraction of gene regulatory relationships are currently known. When SFT is applied to such sparse textual data, LLMs tend to overfit, losing their ability to generalize to unseen biomedical contexts. In contrast, RL can explore multiple strategies through explicit reward signals, potentially improving generalization. However, applying RL in the biomedical field is difficult because it requires precisely defined reward functions [5, 6]. Designing such rewards incurs substantial experimental and computational cost-for instance, validating a drug's response in a specific cell line demands extensive laboratory testing, making real-time or low-cost feedback nearly impossible to obtain. Therefore, a simple, generalizable post-training method capable of learning complex reasoning from sparse data is crucial for advancing LLMs in the biomedical domain.

In this paper, we propose a method called Balanced Fine-Tuning (BFT), which aims to align LLMs with biomedical science. First, BFT limits the gradients on each token, ensuring stable parameter updates and preventing overfitting. Second, BFT adjusts the model's learning intensity for different samples based on sample-level confidence, allowing the model to self-adaptively focus on difficult samples during training. BFT is a simple improvement built upon SFT, with RL-like generalization capabilities but without the significant training cost of RL.

We comprehensively evaluate the benefit of applying BFT on well-known LLMs such as DeepSeekR1-Distill series [1]. In the medical domain, BFT enables LLMs to acquire knowledge that SFT cannot learn. More importantly, after BFT, LLMs exhibit less forgetting in general domains and thereby achieve overall improvement. In the biological domain, the BFT-based DeepSeek-R1-Distill (70B) outperforms GeneAgent (a self-verification language agent for biology analysis using domain databases) [7]. The BFT-based LLM does not require the complex scheduling process and external database used in GeneAgent, indicating that BFT has injected biological knowledge into LLM. Furthermore, we demonstrate the advantages of BFT from the embedding perspective, where the text responses generated by the BFT-based LLM can be converted into embeddings and widely applied in downstream tasks. Specifically, we obtain semantically rich gene entity embeddings from the BFT-based LLM, which can be directly used in biological research for gene interaction prediction, single-cell multimodal integration, single-cell perturbation response prediction. These results show that BFT can effectively align LLMs with biomedical science.

### 2 Results

## 2.1 BFT overview

Balanced Fine-Tuning (BFT) introduces adaptive weighting at both the token and sample levels to enhance training stability and learning efficiency. At the token level, BFT records the prediction probability of each token-the likelihood assigned to it during generation. The loss for each token is rescaled according to its probability: tokens predicted with lower confidence receive smaller gradient contributions, preventing overfitting. This mechanism stabilizes optimization while maintaining fine-grained control over token-level learning. At the sample level, BFT evaluates the overall difficulty of each training sample based on its group confidence. Specifically, it computes the average prediction probability of tokens within a sliding window, representing the model's local confidence across short text spans. Among all such windows, the one with the minimum group confidence is selected to characterize the sample's overall difficulty. Samples with lower minimum group confidence are assigned higher weights, allowing the model to adaptively focus on challenging examples. By integrating weighting mechanisms at both the token and sample levels, BFT achieves a balance between gradient stability and adaptive difficulty learning, enabling large language models to generalize more effectively in complex biomedical reasoning tasks. For a detailed description of the method, please refer to the Methods section.

## 2.2 Ablation study

We conducted ablation study in the mathematical reasoning domain, as it involves complex multi-step inference processes and produces concise and deterministic results at each step. Moreover, this domain provides several public datasets for testing the generalization capability of post-training methods.

To conveniently verify the effectiveness of BFT's components, we fine-tuned a relatively small-scale model, DeepSeek-R1-Distill (1.5B), on the NuminaMath dataset [8]. The fine-tuned model was then evaluated on multiple widely used mathematical reasoning benchmarks, including math\_oai [9], minerva\_math [10], and olympiadbench [11].

Extended Data Figure 1a shows the test accuracy across different datasets. Two baselines are included: the red dashed line represents SFT, and the blue dashed line represents reinforcement learning (represented by GRPO [1]). BFT is evaluated under three window length settings (BFT128/256/512). In addition, two ablation variants are included: BFT w/o sample, which removes sample-level weighting, and BFT w/o token, which disables token-level weighting. Across all datasets, BFT consistently outperforms SFT and achieves performance comparable to GRPO. When token-level weighting is applied (comparing BFT w/o sample with SFT), BFT exhibits significantly better generalization, indicating that stabilizing token-level gradients effectively enhances reasoning performance. Adding sample-level weighting (comparing BFT-128/256/512 with BFT w/o sample) further improves performance by encouraging LLMs to focus on more challenging samples. However, removing token-level weighting (BFT w/o token) leads to less improvement compared to SFT, suggesting that gradient stabilization is a prerequisite for effective difficult-sample learning.

Extended Data Figure 1b tracks the reasoning performance of BFT under different window lengths within a single training epoch. All configurations (BFT-128/256/512) exhibit stable optimization trajectories and similar performance trends, demonstrating robustness to window size. Among them, BFT-256 achieves the highest overall accuracy, likely because shorter windows capture overly local confidence, while longer windows may be influenced by unrelated noisy tokens within the window. Therefore, we adopt BFT-256 as the default configuration in subsequent experiments.

## 2.3 BFT enhances the outputs of LLMs

### 2.3.1 Medicine: BFT performs well on the OpenAI Health Bench

We evaluated our method in the medical domain using the OpenAI Health Bench [12], a comprehensive benchmark for assessing LLMs on real-world clinical and biomedical reasoning tasks. The benchmark provides two key subsets: the Consensus subset, which contains tasks where experts consistently agree on correct answers, and the Hard subset, which consists of more complex, ambiguous, or high-stakes questions designed to test the limits of LLM reasoning and factual grounding.

We employed the DeepSeek-R1-Distill series, specifically the 14B, 32B, and 70B versions. These models are distilled from the DeepSeek-R1, allowing them to inherit strong reasoning capabilities while maintaining computational efficiency. We fine-tuned these models on the Consensus subset and evaluated them on the Hard subset to assess generalization to challenging medical scenarios. Health Bench adopts two complementary evaluation views: theme-wise evaluation , which categorizes results by medical themes, including complex\_response, health\_data\_task, communication, and clinical knowledge; and axis-wise evaluation , which measures model performance across key skill dimensions such as instruction following, factual accuracy, completeness, and reasoning depth. Each score is computed based on OpenAI ChatGPT evaluations that assess the quality of model outputs along multiple axes, normalized to a 0-1 range and averaged across all samples within each subset or theme.

BFT significantly improves the performance of LLMs on OpenAI Health Bench. In the theme-wise evaluation presented in Figure 1a, for certain themes like complex\_response, health\_data\_task, and communication, BFT enables LLMs to master knowledge that is difficult for SFT to learn. As shown in Figure 1b, in the axis-wise evaluation, BFT greatly enhances metrics such as instruction following, accuracy, and completeness. Additionally, we compared the computational efficiency of BFT and SFT in Extended Data Figure 2. BFT achieves stable overall scores under different sliding window settings (128, 256, 512), and its training runtime is close to that of SFT.

### 2.3.2 General area: reducing forgetfulness

SFT often causes LLMs to experience catastrophic forgetting, where knowledge acquired from general domains is partially lost after domain-specific fine-tuning. To assess whether BFT mitigates this issue, we evaluated models fine-tuned on the OpenAI Health Bench Consensus subset using two widely recognized general-domain benchmarks: MMLU [13] and CMMLU [14].

MMLU (Massive Multitask Language Understanding) evaluates broad knowledge and reasoning ability across 57 academic subjects, including STEM (Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics), social sciences, humanities, and other professional domains. It is one of the most widely used indicators of general reasoning capability in LLMs. CMMLU (Chinese Massive Multitask Language Understanding) is its Chinese counterpart, covering 67 subjects across similar categories, designed to measure both general knowledge and domain transfer capability in Chinese-language reasoning. Evaluating on both MMLU and CMMLU enables a comprehensive understanding of how post-training affects multilingual generalization and forgetting.

As shown in Figures 1c and 1d, BFT consistently mitigates general-domain forgetting compared to SFT across all three models (14B, 32B and 70B). For example, on MMLU, BFT achieves higher or comparable scores to the base model, outperforming SFT across most subject categories, particularly in the social sciences and humanities. On CMMLU, BFT also maintains or improves accuracy relative to the base model, demonstrating its robustness in multilingual reasoning. These results indicate that BFT effectively stabilizes gradients and preserves general knowledge during domain-specific alignment, achieving the trade-off between specialization and generalization.

### 2.3.3 Biology: BFT improves reasoning about biological processes

In the biology domain, we fine-tuned LLMs using both SFT and BFT. The training data construction process is as follows: NCBI text provided by GenePT [15] is used as the knowledge base, and GPT-OSS-120B [16] is employed to generate a dataset in the share-gpt format. Extended Data Figure 3 illustrates examples of the constructed samples.

We then evaluated the fine-tuned models on biological process reasoning tasks. As shown in Figure 1e, across all models and test datasets, LLMs fine-tuned with BFT demonstrate more accurate inference of biological processes, indicating that BFT enhances the biological knowledge of LLMs.

Among the three models, we chose DeepSeek-R1-Distill 70B, which has the most accurate reasoning results. We compared the BFT-based 70B LLM with two latest baselines. As shown in Figure 1f, the BFT-based LLM outperforms GeneAgent in biological process reasoning tasks, demonstrating stronger reasoning ability in gene interactions and related processes. Unlike GeneAgent, the BFTbased LLM does not rely on external API calls and database access (such as OpenAI and NCBI), nor does it require the design of an agent scheduling process. This indicates that BFT has enabled LLM to learn biological knowledge.

In Extended Data Figure 4 and Extended Data Figure 5, we present examples of the ability of SFT-based and BFT-based LLMs to master gene knowledge and infer biological processes. In the first example, while SFT provides a generic overview, BFT demonstrates superior biological granularity by identifying specific mechanisms like MHC II presentation and SARS-CoV-2 spike activation. BFT also captures critical clinical insights missed by SFT, such as CTSL's regulation by cystatin and its status as a potential therapeutic target. For a more detailed comparison, please refer to Extended Data Table 1. In the second example, the answer of BFT is more accurate, as it considers the biological functions of the gene more comprehensively, especially in terms of genome stability and nuclear lamina organization. However, the answer of SFT is overly simplified in terms of tumor growth inhibition and ignores the key role of the gene in cell structure and genome stability. For a more detailed comparison, please refer to Extended Data Table 2.

## 2.4 BFT learns representations with biological meaning

### 2.4.1 Gene-level task evaluation

Considering that many downstream biological tasks can be accomplished through representation learning, we further examined whether the embeddings generated by BFT-based LLM capture biological knowledge. As shown in Extended Data Figure 6a, we obtained response texts from BFTbased LLM and generated their embeddings using Youtu-Embedding [17]. The UMAP visualization in Figure 2a shows that genes with similar biological functions form distinct clusters, indicating that BFT-based representations reflect biological heterogeneity at the gene level. Compared with scGPT [18] and GenePT [15], BFT-based embeddings exhibit more compact and biologically meaningful clustering. While scGPT relies on large-scale biological pretraining and GenePT adapts ChatGPT with high computational cost, BFT-based embeddings achieve strong biological meaning without requiring any domain-specific data management.

### 2.4.2 Cell-level task evaluation

We further evaluated the potential of BFT in cell-level embedding representation. As shown in Extended Data Figure 6b, we directly obtained cell embeddings by weighted aggregation of singlecell expression data and gene embeddings. In Figure 2d, we evaluated the heterogeneity of cell embeddings using phenotypic labels and cell type labels respectively, and found that BFT-based embeddings perform the best. This result also reveals an interesting phenomenon: BFT-based embeddings outperform scGPT, a single-cell foundation model pre-trained with large-scale singlecell datasets. This indicates that BFT indeed has the ability to align LLMs with biological knowledge. In Extended Data Figure 8, we used UMAP to compare the PCA embeddings of raw data and BFT-based embeddings. We found that BFT-based embeddings eliminate batch effects across patients and distinguishes clusters of different cell types.

We also evaluated the single-cell multi-modal integration task [19]. We used BFT-based LLM to obtain protein texts, then used Youtu-Embedding to generate protein embeddings, and obtained cell embeddings under the protein modality in the same way. After mixing with cell embeddings under the RNA modality, we found that BFT-based embeddings have multi-modal integration ability. We compared this method with mainstream integration methods such as BBKNN [20], Harmony [21], and scMODAL [22]. The integration performance of BFT is second only to scMODAL and far exceeds that of Harmony and BBKNN (Figure 2e). This indicates that BFT-based embeddings not only preserve biological heterogeneity but also accurately align the two modalities. For the UMAP visualization of the integration results, please refer to Extended Data Figure 9.

BFT-based embeddings demonstrate excellent biological knowledge in cell atlases and have the potential to serve as a unified representation in virtual cells [23]. We applied BFT-based embeddings to virtual cells, where its main task is perturbation response prediction. We obtained cell embeddings by weighting the gene embeddings of BFT-based LLM, and then used these cell embeddings as the input for the STATE [24] decoder. The experiment involved zero-shot prediction of perturbation responses on four perturbation datasets [25]. Assuming HepG2 as the test set, we used RPE1, Jurkat, and K562 to train the decoder of STATE. The comparison methods included STATE-SE (standard STATE: SE + ST) and STATE-small (a lightweight version of STATE). We observed that BFT-based embeddings achieve the same performance as the current state-of-the-art model STATE (Figure 2f). Notably, BFT-based embeddings can achieve performance consistent with that of standard STATE without incurring biological data management costs, and are expected to serve as the cornerstone for future virtual cell research. ### 3 Discussion

Balanced Fine-Tuning (BFT) is a general yet effective post-training method that enhances the learning stability and generalizability of LLMs, particularly in domains characterized by sparse and complex reasoning such as biomedical science. Unlike reinforcement learning (RL), BFT does not rely on explicit reward functions or costly feedback loops; instead, it dynamically reweights gradients at both the token and sample levels. This design allows BFT to stabilize optimization while adaptively emphasizing challenging examples, enabling efficient learning from limited biomedical supervision.

Although BFT itself is a domain-agnostic method, biomedical knowledge becomes integrated into the LLMs through the training data and the adaptive learning process it induces. Specifically, when BFT is applied to biomedical data, the model's parameters are gradually reshaped to capture the causal and functional regularities embedded in biomedical language. The token-level stabilization prevents overfitting to fragmented biomedical facts, while the sample-level weighting encourages the model to focus on more uncertain or complex biomedical scenarios. This mechanism implicitly transfers biomedical knowledge into the model's internal representation space.

Our experiments demonstrate that BFT improves reasoning and factual grounding in biomedical tasks while preserving general-domain competence. For example, models fine-tuned with BFT on the OpenAI Health Bench not only achieve higher accuracy in medical reasoning but also exhibit reduced forgetting on general benchmarks such as MMLU and CMMLU. In the biological domain, post-training with BFT can encode biologically meaningful representations-gene embeddings derived from BFT-based LLM naturally cluster by functional categories and align across modalities, outperforming specialized biological foundation models. These results suggest that BFT facilitates the internal organization of biomedical knowledge within the LLM's parameter space.

In conclusion, BFT provides a lightweight, stable, and domain-adaptive framework for post-training LLMs on scientific data. It offers a practical route to integrate complex biomedical knowledge without specialized architecture or costly reinforcement signals. More broadly, BFT bridges general-domain intelligence with domain-specific reasoning, advancing the development of intelligent agents capable of scientific understanding and discovery. However, we recognize that BFT serves to maximize the utilization of available data, distinct from pre-training which injects massive new knowledge. Consequently, if the base LLM is entirely void of relevant biological knowledge, or if the quality of the fine-tuning data is compromised, BFT cannot compensate for this absence of foundational information.
