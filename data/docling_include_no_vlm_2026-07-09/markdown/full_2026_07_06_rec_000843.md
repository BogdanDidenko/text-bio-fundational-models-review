#### Abstract

The success of SARS-CoV-2 (severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2) messenger RNA (mRNA) vaccine has led to increased interest in the design and use of mRNA for vaccines and therapeutics. Still, selecting the most appropriate mRNA sequence for a protein remains a challenge. Se v eral recent studies ha v e sho wn that the specific mRNA sequence can ha v e a significant impact on the translation efficiency, halflife, degradation rates, and other issues that play a major role in determining vaccine efficiency. To enable the selection of the most appropriate sequence, w e de v eloped mRNA-LM, an integrated small language model f or modeling the entire mRNA sequence. mRNA-LM uses the contrastive language-image pretraining integration technology to combine three separate language models for the diff erent mRNA segments. W e trained mRNA-LM on millions of diverse mRNA sequences from se v eral different species. The unsupervised model was able to learn meaningful biology related to e v olution and host-pathogen interactions. Fine-tuning of mRNA-LM allo w ed us to use it in se v eral mRNA property prediction tasks. As we show, using the full-length integrated model led to accurate predictions, improving on prior methods proposed for this task.

Several additional efforts are focused on using mRNA for other vaccines, including Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV), flu, and more [ 6-8 ]. Beyond vaccines, mRNA is also important for industrial biotechnology applications, enabling the production of therapeutic proteins and enzymes in a controlled and scalable manner [ 9 ].

The mRNA molecule is single-stranded and contains three reading frames; the 5 ′ untranslated region (UTR), the coding sequence (CDS), and the 3 ′ UTR. Of these three, only the CDS is translated into proteins and codon usage was shown to have a significant impact on the efficacy and stability of mRNA in host cells [ 10-12 ]. However, even though they are not translated, the 5 ′ and 3 ′ UTRs also play crucial role in mRNA stability and translation [ 13-17 ]. The 5 ′ UTR plays an important role in translation initialization. For eukaryotic mRNAs, the 5 ′ cap recruits the ribosome, which then scans along the 5 ′ UTR to identify the start codon (ATG) and initiate translation. Specific motifs in the 5 ′ UTR may block, or delay, the entry or scanning of ribosome, causing a lower rate of initiation [ 14 ]. For viral genomes, although the internal ribosome entry sites directly recruit ribosome to the 5 ′ UTR in a cap-independent way, their structures and motifs can also impact translation initiation [ 13 ]. The 3 ′ UTR regulates mRNA stability, translation, and localization by RNA binding proteins. These bind to the 3 ′ UTR and recruit different functional proteins [ 16 ]. Finally, there is also evidence that the interaction between 5 ′ and 3 ′ UTRs, e.g. via RNA circularization, increases translation efficiency [ 15 ]. Taken together, it is clear that these regions are important for optimizing mRNA vaccine efficacy.

A number of recent methods, including from our group, have focused on optimizing coding region (CDS) composition for mRNA vaccine efficacy and safety [ 18-23 ]. Relatively little work to date has attempted to similarly optimize the 5 ′ or 3 ′ UTRs [ 24-27 ]. Still, to our knowledge, no work has attempted to optimize all three regions together.

Prior methods focused on coding regions have often utilized language models pretrained on large-scale mRNA data. These methods were applied, separately, to both CDS and 5 ′ and 3 ′ UTRs (cdsBERT [ 28 ], UTR-LM [ 29 ], and 3UTRBERT [ 30 ]). However, these models utilized relatively small number of sequences (around 0.7 million and 20 000 sequences for UTRLM and 3UTRBERT, respectively). In addition, none of these methods is able to analyze and predict for a full-length mRNA sequence including all three regions.

To address this challenge, we developed and implemented software that constructs a joint language model for all three regions. For this, we extended our previous CDS-only model, CodonBERT [ 22 ], which is a pretrained small language model (SLM) utilizing 10 million CDSs. As illustrated in Fig. 1 , we pretrained two additional BERT models [ 31 ], 5UTRBERT and 3UTRBERT, on the 5 ′ UTR and 3 ′ UTR, respectively. We next constructed a joint model, mRNA-LM, which combines these three models to create a full-length mRNA sequence model. We utilize the contrastive language-image pretraining (CLIP) approach to align the embeddings of these different regions. By treating the 5 ′ UTR, CDS, and 3 ′ UTR embeddings as separate modalities, we apply contrastive losses to encourage closer alignment of embeddings from the same mRNA sequence while distinguishing between different sequences. The joint modeling approach allows us to capture the interactions and dependences between the coding and noncoding regions, providing a comprehensive representation of the full-length mRNA.

To assess our models' performance, we collected four datasets [ 32-34 ] spanning various properties: transcript stability, transcript expression, translation rate, and protein expression. As we show, the joint architecture and model capture relevant aspects of biology and are able to accurately reconstruct mRNA sequences. We then compared the performance of our mRNA-LM model against two existing state-of-the-art approaches. mRNA-LM improves the performance of the individual region models, leading to more accurate predictions for several different key mRNA properties.

## Materials and methods

#### Datasets for pretraining UTRBERTs

We used several datasets to construct the input for our models. These included Mammalia reference sequences ( https://www. ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/ datasets/ taxonomy/ 40674/ ), human virus with complete nucleotides ( https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/ labs/ virus/ vssi/ #/ ), Esc heric hia coli ( https://www.ncbi.nlm. nih.gov/ datasets/ taxonomy/ 562/ ), and yeast ( https:// www. yeastgenome.org/). Both the NCBI and yeast genome websites only provide the full mRNA sequences and the CDSs. To identify the UTR sequences, we aligned the CDS with the full sequence. The section preceding the coding region was designated as the 5 ′ UTR, while the section following it was labeled as the 3 ′ UTR. Following these steps, we ended up with 10 million coding, 6 million 5 ′ UTR, and 4 million 3 ′ UTR sequences. The number of unique sequences for each family can be found in Supplementary Table S1 .

#### Pretraining our models

See CodonBERT [ 22 ] for detailed information on how we trained the CDS component. In contrast to the coding module, which uses codons as the tokens, both UTRBERTs are nucleotide-based models and treat the nucleotides as tokens directly without any special tokenization. The BERT model we used for these regions follows the model we used for CodonBERT [ 22 ] and has 12 hidden layers, 12 attention heads, a hidden size of 768, and an intermediate size of 3072. The main difference between the two architectures is the maximum sequence length. We set it to 512 for 5UTRBERT since &gt; 80% of the training sequences are shorter than 500 nt. In contrast, we set it to 1024 for 3UTRBERT since 3 ′ UTR sequences are much longer than 5 ′ UTR. The pretraining of two models was performed using masking language model (MLM) optimization [ 31 ]. MLM is a self-supervised learning task that randomly masks 15% of the input tokens with the [MASK] token and predicts the masked tokens based on the remaining context.

#### mRNA-LM: joint language model

Given a full-length mRNA sequence, consisting of three regions (5 ′ UTR, CDS, and 3 ′ UTR), we feed each region into its corresponding pretrained model and obtain the contextual embeddings for all tokens (Fig. 2 A). Next, we apply a mean pooling operation to the output token embeddings from each pretrained model to obtain segment representations of three regions, respectively. Mean pooling is a widely used, simple, yet effective solution for constructing sequence embeddings from a list of token embeddings for certain compute vision and natural language processing tasks [ 35 , 36 ]. #### Learning the joint representation of segment sequence using CLIP

To learn the joint representation of the three segment embeddings of 5 ′ UTR, CDS, and 3 ′ UTR obtained from tokenlevel embeddings of the three BERT-based encoders, we use the CLIP approach [ 37 ]. CLIP was originally used to leverage contrastive learning to align image and text embeddings in a shared space. Here, we adapt the CLIP technology to capture relationships between different segments in the mRNA. To use CLIP, we view the three segments' embeddings as three separate modalities of the full-length sequence. We next optimize a loss function that aims to align the embeddings of these different modalities.

Minimizing the contrastive loss encourages the model to bring the embeddings of different parts of the same sequence closer together in the latent space, while pushing apart embeddings from different sequences. Specifically, for each example in a batch, the contrastive loss aims to maximize the similarity between the 5 ′ UTR and CDS embeddings, as well as the CDS and 3 ′ UTR embeddings, if they originate from the same mRNA sequence. It also increases the distance between embeddings from different mRNA sequences within the batch. In a batch, if u i and v i represent the embeddings of two modalities (e.g. 5 ′ UTR and CDS) from the same mRNA sequence, the contrastive loss function ensures that sim( u i , v i ) is maximized. Simultaneously, for different sequences i and j , the contrastive loss function reduces sim( u i , v j ). This dual mechanism of attraction and repulsion in the embedding space helps the model learn discriminative features that are more representative of the biological context of the sequences.

We further tested various values of α : {0, 0.2, 0.5, 0.7, 1.0} and observed no significant impact on the overall results. By treating the embeddings of 5 ′ UTR, CDS, and 3 ′ UTR as distinct but related modalities, the model can more effectively capture the contextual information and dependences across these segments. The contrastive loss operates by dynamically adjusting the embeddings during training to reflect these relationships, which in turn enhances the model's ability to generalize and perform well on downstream tasks.

#### Fine-tuning using LoRA

The resulting integrated model contains &gt; 260 million parameters. To enable fine-tuning of the model for specific downstream prediction tasks, we utilized LoRA [ 38 ]. LoRA inserts trainable rank decomposition matrices into each transformer layer [ 39 ], while keeping the weights of the pretrained models frozen. Compared to fine-tuning the whole model, LoRA only trains a small number of parameters making it more efficient and less prune to overfitting. Specifically, LoRA injects two fully connected layers with size d in × r and d out × r , respectively, where d in and d out are the hidden and intermediate sizes of the pretrained model, respectively, and r is the LoRA rank, which is a hyperparameter affecting the number of weights. In our model, the d in and d out are 764 and 3072, respectively, for all 12 layers. We used a rank of 32 for LoRA in our application resulting in a total of (768 + 3072) × 12 × 32 = 1 474 560 tuned parameters, which is &lt; 2% of the total in the pretrained model.

## Results

#### Datasets

To evaluate mRNA-LM, we fine-tune the full model on four labeled human datasets [ 32-34 ]. Dataset [ 32 ] collected and processed transcriptome-wide half-life data for human from 16 publications, resulting in data for over 10 000 human genes from 39 samples. Dataset [ 33 ] contains processed Homo sapiens translation rate data, representing the protein abundance per mRNA per hour in log 10 value, derived from mRNA sequencing and ribosomal profiling experiments. Dataset [ 34 ] provides insights into the relationship between mRNA and protein abundance, covering thousands of transcripts and proteins across 29 human tissues. We focused on the transcript expression in the liver as the target values. In their work, the authors used a classification scheme [ 40 ] to categorize protein expression levels in tissues. This framework divides protein into five classes as follows: 'tissue-enriched' (five-fold above their expression in any other tissue), 'group-enriched' (fivefold above any group of 2-7 tissues), 'enhanced' (five-fold above the average of all other tissues), 'expressed in all' (expressed in all tissues), and 'mixed' (genes that do not belong to any of the previous categories). More information about sequence extraction and data processing is provided in the Supplementary data.

#### Evaluation of UTRBERTs

As mentioned earlier, most prior analysis of SLMs for mRNAs focused on coding regions [ 22 ]. Prior to testing the combined model, we looked at the ability of the UTR parts of the model to accurately capture biological information relevant for mRNA functionality.

To test 5 ′ UTR embeddings, for each organism, 1% of the pretraining dataset has been reserved to serve as an evaluation set. The contextual nucleotide embeddings are first extracted from the 5UTRBERT for the held-out dataset. Full 5 ′ UTR embeddings derived by averaging the contextual nucleotide embeddings across the entire sequence were reduced to twodimensional representations using UMAP [ 41 ]. Figure 3 A illustrates a clear separation between the clusters representing mammals, bacteria, and yeast. This distinct grouping suggests fundamental differences in the genetic makeup of these diverse organisms captured by the model.

The 5 ′ UTR embeddings of viral genomes occupy an intermediate position between the mammalian and bacterial clusters with a substantial overlap. This overlapping cluster is composed of some viruses that infect bacteria or humans. This positioning suggests a potential genetic similarity or adaptation strategy of these viruses to their diverse hosts. This similarity, identified by a completely unsupervised model, provides support to a hypothesis the claims that the genomic resemblance may be a function of co-evolution between viral and host genes [ 42-44 ].

Finally, we assessed the pretrained 5UTRBERT model's generalization capabilities, particularly for predicting 5 ′ UTR sequence properties. For this, we used the 5 ′ UTR embeddings to predict ribosome recruitment, a key function of 5 ′ UTRs in translation. We utilized a dataset from a study that ex- plored the ribosome loading capacity of numerous artificial 5 ′ UTRs [ 45 ]. The pretrained 5UTRBERT was fine-tuned on this dataset, with all 5 ′ UTR sequences left-padded with 'N' to achieve uniform length, as per the original study's methodology Figure 3 B presents the results of this analysis. As can be seen, the fine-tuned model achieved a Pearson correlation of 0.90 on the human test set. This performance is comparable to both the model reported in the original paper and the RNA-FM model, demonstrating the usefulness of 5 ′ UTR embeddings.

#### Evaluation of mRNA-LM and comparison to prior work

We compared our mRNA-LM with two existing models: RNA-FM [ 46 ] and Saluki [ 32 ]. RNA-FM was not able to handle the length of the most of the mRNA sequences in the above datasets since they exceeded the model limitation. We thus extracted nucleotide embeddings by feeding different regions (5 ′ UTR, CDS, and 3 ′ UTR) into RNA-FM separately. The nucleotide embeddings from three regions are concatenated along the sequence to build an n × ( h + 3) matrix, where n is the number of nucleotides in the sequence, h is the hidden size of the embedding from RNA-FM, and the rest three positions indicate the locations. These positions are assigned a value of 1 if the nucleotide belongs to the 5 ′ UTR, coding region, or 3 ′ UTR, respectively, and 0 otherwise. Next, we applied a TextCNN [ 47 ] model to perform the supervised learning on the embedding. Saluki [ 32 ] is a hybrid convolutional and recurrent deep neural network. We retrained the Saluki model from scratch for different datasets using the same model architecture and hyperparameters as proposed in the initial paper.

Comparison results are presented in Table 1 . As can be seen, the mRNA-LM model demonstrates superior performance compared to both RNA-FM and Saluki across all four datasets evaluated. For example, for predicting mRNA halflife, mRNA-LM achieved a Spearman score of 0.696, which is an improvement over the second-best model, Saluki, which had a correlation of 0.675. The average correlation value of the Saluki model is taken from [ 32 ] as part of an ablation study trained without the splice site feature. When determining translation rates, mRNA-LM achieved a Spearman score of 0.610, which again was the highest compared to prior methods.

Transcription of genes is regulated by transcription factors, which bind to enhancer or promoter regions of DNA adjacent to the genes they regulate [ 48 ]. While all models, including mRNA-LM, do not incorporate enhancer and promoter regions as inputs, mRNA-LM outperformed other models for the expression task with a Spearman score of 0.349. Finally, for the protein expression task, which is set as a classification problem, mRNA-LM reached an AUROC of 0.685, again the highest among those evaluated.

For the other models, we found that Saluki [ 32 ] was the second best. Although Saluki is also a nucleotide-based model (similar to RNA-FM), it annotates the first nucleotide of each codon in the coding region, whereas RNA-FM is fully nucleotide-based and does not recognize the coding regions and differentiate between codons. This could indicate that a nucleotide-based model is inadequate for coding regions and actually decreases the performance of the full sequence embedding. On the other hand, as the table shows, a model that only uses the coding regions (even when the representation is codon-based) is also inferior to a joint model that can utilize the UTR information.

To gain insight into the relative impact of different regions (5 ′ UTR, CDS, 3 ′ UTR) of mRNA sequences on target predictions, we conducted an ablation study by fine-tuning three models independently using the corresponding regions. As expected, 5UTRBER T, CodonBER T, and 3UTRBER T all show inferior performance compared to the complete mRNALM model, indicating that using only one of the segments could negatively impact model prediction. However, CodonBERT still shows significantly strong performance, suggesting that an understanding of codon usage is beneficial for all tasks. CodonBERT achieved a Spearman score of 0.564 for mRNA half-life, 0.546 for translation rate, and 0.284 for transcript expression. For the protein expression task, CodonBERT achieves an AUROC value of 0.663, which is even higher than the RNA-FM and Saluki results. UTRBERTs achieve lower performance compared to CodonBERT. This indicates that while the 5 ′ and 3 ′ UTRs of mRNA contribute to the predictions, they are less informative when considered in isolation. Interestingly, for tasks regarding mRNA / transcript levels, the 3 ′ UTR segment was more predictive than the 5 ′ UTR. Conversely, 5 ′ UTR led to better results when predicting protein levels.

We also performed an ablation study of replacing the CLIP module with a simple concatenation of the segment embeddings. As shown in Supplementary Table S2 , our results demonstrate that incorporating the CLIP module, which treats each part of the mRNA sequence as a separate modality, improves the performance of downstream tasks compared to simply concatenating the embeddings. By applying CLIP-style losses, the model learns more robust and meaningful representations, highlighting the effectiveness of treating each sequence part as an independent view and leveraging the contrastive learning paradigm. The inclusion of modality-specific contrastive losses encourages the model to capture complementary information from each sequence part, ultimately leading to enhanced predictive performance that consistently outperforms traditional concatenation approach across all our experiments.

#### Testing mRNA-LM on mRNA vaccine data

Given the interest in using mRNA for vaccines, we tested mRNA-LM using two mRNA vaccine datasets [ 49 , 50 ]. Unlike the other datasets we tested, these datasets are extremely small with &lt; 30 sequences in total. Leppek et al. [ 49 ] con- structed an mRNA library containing 233 unique sequences with varying UTRs and CDSs. Among these, nine sequences encoded a multi-epitope S AR S-CoV-2 (severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2) vaccine CDS (MEV), which were experimentally tested for stability (the MEV dataset). Similarly, Zhang et al. [ 50 ] carried out experimental evaluations of mRNA vaccines designed via LinearDesign, assessing protein expression in HEK293 cells over a 24-h incubation. This study included nine mRNA sequences encoding the S AR S-CoV-2 spike protein and seven sequences encoding the glycoprotein E of the varicella-zoster virus (the LinearDesign dataset).

Given their limited size, we were not able to test them in the same way we used other datasets (fine-tuning mRNA-LM followed by testing). We have thus performed zero-shot learning using models for two of the datasets we previously analyzed: mRNA half-life and translation rate. We assessed the generalization performance of these two models on the MEV and LinearDesign datasets, respectively. Despite operating in a zero-shot setting, both mRNA-LM models demonstrated strong generalization to these vaccine datasets. Specifically, the mRNA-LM model fine-tuned on the mRNA half-life dataset achieved a Spearman correlation of 0.583 when predicting the stability of the MEV dataset, as illustrated in Fig. 4 A. Similarly, the model fine-tuned on the translation rate dataset yielded a Spearman correlation of 0.718 on the LinearDesign dataset, as shown in Fig. 4 B.

For comparison, we also benchmarked two existing models (Saluki and RNA-FM) on these two vaccine datasets. Similarly to how we did for mRNA-LM, both models were initially trained on mRNA half-life and translation rate datasets, and then used to predict target values for the MEV and LinearDesign datasets. The trained RNA-FM model obtained much lower Spearman correlation values of 0.177 and 0.314 for these two datasets, respectively. The trained Saluki model achieved Spearman correlation values of 0.533 and 0.494, both lower than the ones obtained by our model (the result for the second one, the LinearDesign dataset, was significantly lower). Thus, while we do not have a comprehensive benchmark for vaccine design, these results provide anecdotal support for the use of mRNA-LM in vaccine design.

#### Extension and scalability of mRNA-LM to promoter regions

In addition to UTRs and CDSs, there are other regions that regulate transcription and translation, such as promot- ers [ 51 ]. Several deep learning models have been developed to identify promoter regions [ 52 , 53 ]. To evaluate the potential use of incorporating promoter sequences, we utilized the Xpresso model [ 51 ], which was trained to predict gene expression based on promoter sequences and features associated with mRNA stability. The dataset used to train the Xpresso model comprised mRNA expression levels across 56 human cell types [ 54 ]. Using the same dataset, we extended our mRNA-LM model by incorporating the predicted values from Xpresso as an additional feature into the prediction head. As shown in Supplementary Fig. S2 , the Xpresso predictions for each sequence, along with the input 5 ′ UTR, CDS, and 3 ′ UTR sequences, were fed into the extended model. In this setup, only the parameters of mRNA-LM were trainable. Incorporating Xpresso predictions led to an improvement in performance, increasing the Pearson correlation for predicting mRNA expression from 0.675 to 0.771.

To ensure the robustness of these results, we used an additional dataset that includes transcript expression levels evaluated across 29 human cell types [ 34 ]. This dataset was not used in the original training of the Xpresso model, providing an opportunity for a more robust assessment. To obtain the prediction from Xpresso, we extracted the 7-kb promoter sequence upstream of the genomic sequence from Ensembl and combined it with the genomic sequences. These combined sequences were then processed by the Xpresso model to predict gene expression values for each. The integration of Xpresso predictions into our extended mRNA-LM model resulted in an improvement in Pearson correlation from 0.489 to 0.511. We note, however, that while clearly useful, regulatory information is not relevant for mRNA vaccines and so using Xpresso or any other promoter analysis method would not help for vaccine design.

## Conclusion

mRNA-LM integrates three distinct submodules that collectively provide a comprehensive and accurate SLM for fulllength mRNAs. Such SLMs are critical for developing efficient and safe innovative vaccines based on mRNAs. By building a novel architecture that combines the strengths of three language models, mRNA-LM outperforms existing state-of-theart methods. The integration of the CLIP technique enables the mRNA-LM to develop a more robust and meaningful joint representation across the three distinct mRNA segments. This improved representation not only enhances the model's understanding of the entire mRNA molecule but also leads to better performance in various downstream tasks. Our results draw parallels to the success of CLIP in vision-and-language tasks of [ 37 , 55-58 ], where in our framework the inclusion of modality-specific contrastive losses encourages our model to capture complementary information from each sequence segment, ultimately leading to enhanced predictive performance.

Our initial motivation in using CLIP resulted from several findings regarding the relationships between coding and noncoding mRNA regions. Studies looking at the co-evolution of coding and noncoding regions within the same transcript show that these regions contain both overlapping and distinct information, which collectively influences gene expression levels [ 59 , 60 ]. However, as we show in the Supplementary data, CLIP only marginally improved simpler integration approaches. Several factors may explain this. A possible explanation is related to the fact that the ability to predict codon usage from noncoding regions [ 60 ] relies on information from promoter and terminator regions, which are not used in our framework. As we have shown earlier, this information is indeed useful though not always generalizable. Another avenue for improvement lies in the careful curation of sequence pair datasets to enhance the performance of the CLIP algorithm. For example, Orthrus [ 61 ] applied the CLIP algorithm on alternative splicing and orthologous transcripts, leveraging shared biological information and functional similarities that surpass those observed in our approach involving UTRs and CDSs. This work offers valuable insights to inform future developments in our methodology.

The UMAP projection of sequence embeddings shown in Fig. 3 A reveals that yeast is more distantly related to mammals and viruses than to bacteria, which may seem counterin- tuitive. However, additional analysis supports this finding for mRNA. We looked at cosine similarity of sequence embeddings generated by our BERT model. We observed an average similarity of 0.848, 0.813, and 0.885 for yeast with mammals, viruses, and bacteria, respectively. These findings agree with the UMAP projections and point to the fact that yeast mRNA is more similar to bacteria than mammals or viruses. Similar results were obtained when using k -mer sequence similarity ( Supplementary Table S3 ).

The mRNA-LM framework is flexible and any language model can be incorporated to replace the ones used in this study (e.g. UTR-LM [ 29 ] and 3UTRBERT proposed by Yang et al. [ 30 ]). Furthermore, mRNA-LM can be extended to more tasks including RNA-RNA interaction and RNA-protein interaction by replacing the language models with two RNAbased or one RNAand one protein-based model.

There are several promising avenues for expanding the capabilities of the mRNA-LM model. The structures of the UTRs play a crucial role in regulating mRNA stability and translation efficiency [ 13 , 16 ]. These structural elements are key determinants of the UTRs' functional properties. Furthermore, the 5 ′ and 3 ′ UTRs have the ability to interact and form a 'circular' RNA configuration. This circular formation has been observed to enhance translation efficiency [ 15 ]. Interestingly, studies on influenza A virus genomes have revealed evidence of co-evolution between complementary bases in the 5 ′ and 3 ′ UTRs. This observation suggests a coordinated evolutionary process that maintains functional interactions between these regions [ 62 ].

Given the significant impact of UTR structures and their interactions on mRNA function, predicting the structure of full-length mRNA in addition to the sequence based models can lead to further improvements in downstream tasks. A joint sequence-structure model can also provide deeper insights into the complex interplay between different mRNA regions and their collective influence on gene expression and regulation. A straightforward approach to extending the current sequence model into a joint sequence-structure model is to fine-tune it by incorporating structural information through an additional embedding layer that encodes secondary structures. We tested this method on the ribosomal loading dataset, focusing on fine-tuning 5UTRBERT. Initially, secondary structures of the 5 ′ UTR sequences were predicted using ViennaRNA [ 63 ]. The structure embedding layer encoded secondary structures by assigning indices to unpaired bases based on their position and to paired bases based on their complementary positions. For instance, for a toy sequence 'AAA GGGGAAA CCCCAAA'with a secondary structure represented as '...((((...))))...', the input indices for the embedding layer would be [0, 1, 2, 13, 12, 11, 10, 7, 8, 9, 6, 5, 4, 3, 14, 15, 16]. Testing this on the data, we saw that model's performance declined significantly, likely due to the addition of structural embeddings, making the input embedding differ substantially from those used during pretraining. A possible solution is to train a full foundational model with sequence and structure information. While this can indeed improve the method, we believe it is outside the scope of our paper and leave it to future work.

Beyond mRNAs, 3 ′ UTRs may be cleaved off from the coding region and act as long noncoding RNAs [ 64 , 65 ]. 3 ′ UTRs were also found to be able to impact protein localization and protein-protein interactions [ 66 ]. Thus, the 3UTR- BERT model can also be used as a stand-alone model for other biological analysis tasks.

A significant limitation of our current 3UTRBERT model is its inability to process sequences longer than 1024 nt. This limitation becomes particularly problematic when considering that the median length of 3 ′ UTRs in humans is ∼ 1200 nt, with some extending up to 2300 nt [ 67 ]. To address this issue, the model input size can be increased, though this will lead to more parameters and the option for overfitting. Alternatively, to address the challenge of processing longer sequences without increasing the model size, we developed a new tokenizer that learns motifs of varying lengths based on their frequency in the training set. We employed the SentencePiece [ 68 ] method to treat the input sequence as a continuous stream, inserting spaces to tokenize the input into distinct motifs. SentencePiece is an unsupervised text tokenization framework that utilizes two subword algorithms: the Unigram language model [ 69 ] and byte-pair encoding (BPE) [ 70 ]. The vocabulary size is predetermined prior to executing the program. Thus, we trained four tokenizers using two different algorithms (Unigram and BPE) and two vocabulary sizes (500 and 5000).

As we show in Supplementary Fig. S3 A and B, the use of trained tokenizers significantly decreased the input length to the BERT model from a maximum of 5000 nucleotides to 1000 tokens, aligning well with the current model length constraints. Further, we examined the ranking of several significant motifs in the final vocabulary for the two algorithms, using a vocabulary size of 5000, as presented in Supplementary Table S4 . The analysis of significant motifs suggests that the Unigram algorithm with vocabulary size 5000 is more effective in recovering known motifs. Next, we conducted the pretraining of the 3UTRBERT-tok model utilizing the same architecture and identical dataset as 3UTRBERT, but employing the newly developed Unigram tokenizer. The training process and performance metrics of the 3UTRBERT-tok model are visually represented in Supplementary Fig. S4 . More analysis and training details are provided in the Supplementary data.

Even though the language model training did not perform well with this new tokenization approach, we still wanted to see whether it can help with downstream analysis. We thus compared 3UTRBERT-tok with 3UTRBERT on downstream tasks. Since 3 ′ UTRs contain cis -regulatory elements that control mRNA stability and translation, we looked at two of the benchmark datasets: mRNA half-life and protein expression. We fine-tuned both models solely on 3 ′ UTRs. For the protein expression dataset, half of the dataset consists of 3 ′ UTRs longer than 1022 nt. The 3UTRBERT-tok model led to a slightly higher AUROC value of 0.586 compared to 3UTRBERT, which has an AUROC value of 0.57. Similarly, half of the mRNA half-life dataset comprises 3 ′ UTR sequences longer than 1022 nt. Although 3UTRBERT-tok is capable of handling longer 3 ′ UTR sequences, potentially containing more regulatory information, it achieved results comparable to 3UTRBERT with only nuanced differences for this dataset. Thus, while the tokenization idea can indeed help increase the size of the data we use, it did not lead to improvement in overall performance.

Several hypotheses can be proposed to elucidate the lack of improvement observed in 3UTRBERT-tok compared to 3UTRBERT, despite its capacity to process longer sequences. Primarily, although the vocabulary incorporates var- ious meaningful motifs, the tokenizer's ability to accurately segment these motifs from sequences in diverse contexts warrants further investigation. Should the tokenizer fail to precisely identify these motifs at the initial processing stage, it could become a limiting factor in the overall architectural performance. The potential inability of the tokenizer to recognize critical motifs may be attributed to the unsupervised learning approach employed in its training. This methodology relies primarily on the frequency of motif occurrence within the training dataset to determine tokenization patterns. However, it is plausible that these essential motifs do not necessarily represent the most frequently occurring tokens.

To address this limitation, a more guided approach to tokenizer training may be necessary, potentially incorporating supervised learning techniques. This could involve utilizing annotated sequences from the training set, providing explicit examples of motifs that should be treated as discrete tokens, regardless of their relatively low frequency of occurrence. However, the current absence of such a comprehensive, annotated dataset presents a significant challenge. The development of a labeled dataset specifically for tokenizer training represents a valuable research opportunity. This could be achieved by systematically compiling and annotating sequences analyzed in relevant scientific literature. Such a dataset would provide a foundation for more accurate and context-aware tokenization, potentially enhancing the overall performance of models such as 3UTRBERT-tok.

One contributing factor is the extensive vocabulary size, which, when reduced, can lower the training loss and simplify the MLM task. Nevertheless, a smaller vocabulary size may result in the loss of significant motifs represented within it. For example, the second-ranking AU-rich motif (UUAUUUAUU) holds a position of 4486 in the vocabulary. If the vocabulary size is reduced to 1000, this motif would no longer be included as a single entry and could be fragmented into multiple subunits, such as [UU AU, UU AUU] or [UU A, UUUA, UU]. Regardless of how it is segmented, maintaining consistent subword segmentation is crucial. Further experiments are necessary to determine the optimal vocabulary size.

To conclude, mRMA-LM is the first full-length mRNA SLM to incorporate both codonand nucleotide-based representations. mRNA-LM is implemented in Python and available as an open-source software at https:// github.com/ SanofiPublic/mRNA-LM .
