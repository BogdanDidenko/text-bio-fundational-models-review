#### Abstract

Single-cell sequencing has enabled unprecedented characterization of distinct cell types and advanced our understanding of disease pathogenesis. The rapid expansion of single-cell data presents both tremendous opportunities and challenges. These rich datasets can reveal cellular heterogeneity, enable lineage tracking, and elucidate pathogenic mechanisms for developing personalized therapeutics. However, effectively analyzing the increasingly massive data also requires scalable computational approaches. As single-cell datasets grow exponentially, a critical need emerges to develop methodologies that can harness, enhance, and adapt to these burgeoning resources, and learning the representation for single-cell omics is one of the core tasks for understanding the exponentially growing sequencing data.

The thesis addresses this need by demonstrating a framework to extract meaningful patterns from the single-cell sequencing data. We develop versatile computational models capable of learning from large-scale data. The key innovation is a pretraining and transfer learning paradigm to construct foundation models tailored to single-cell analysis. We design self-supervised learning tasks to enable scalable training and pre-train the transformer foundation model, scGPT, on diverse unlabeled single-cell data to gain general capabilities. We then demonstrate transfer learning by adapting the model to achieve strong performance on specialized downstream tasks: 1) Multi-batch and multi-omic integration, Cell type annotation, and perturbation prediction leveraging the pre-trained cell embeddings. 2) Analyzing developmental dynamics based on newly proposed adaptive neighborhood learning. 3) Continual pre-training expanding the foundation model to spatial transcriptomics.

By developing robust generalizable models that inherit knowledge from massive datasets, this thesis provides a blueprint to propel new biological insights in a data-driven way while scaling computational analysis to the exponentially expanding space of single-cell data.

## Chapter 1

## Introduction

The advent of single-cell omics technologies has enabled biologists to analyze individual cells at an unprecedented resolution. Whereas previous techniques at bulk level could only measure average properties of cell populations, single-cell methods can reveal the tremendous heterogeneity that exists even among seemingly identical cells [Haniffa et al., 2021]. This paradigm shift has also unleashed a torrent of complex genomic datasets capturing biological systems at unprecedented resolution. Effectively analyzing these massive heterogeneous data represents a pressing computational challenge. While deep learning provides a powerful paradigm, prevailing methods rely on simple architectures and objectives ill-suited to single-cell genomics intricacies. This thesis develops a versatile modeling framework to extract meaningful patterns from the explosion of single-cell sequencing data.

The theme of the thesis recognizes the great opportunity of building large-scale computation models that can match the accumulating of available sequencing data and capture the rules and knowledge of biology in a data-driven manner. For this purpose, we construct the pretraining and transfer learning paradigm for building the foundation model for singlecell data. We adapt the foundation model for various applications across single-cell data analysis and demonstrate both the strong performance of the method and the effectiveness of transfer learning.

Outline of Thesis Chapter 2 provides background on single-cell omics and deep learning, establishing key terminology and foundations. We overview single-cell assay modalities and computational analysis tasks. We also introduce deep learning concepts including model architectures, optimization, pretraining, and transfer learning.

Chapter 3 presents our pretraining framework for constructing versatile single-cell foundation models. We depict the motivation and rationale of building the foundation model and detail the model architecture, input data representation, self-supervised objectives, and training scheme to enable effective pretraining on diverse unlabeled atlases. We also analyze the pretrained model properties.

Chapter 4 demonstrates transfer learning for various single-cell analysis tasks, including multi-batch data integration, multi-omic data integration, cell type annotation, and perturbation response prediction. We show how fine-tuning the pretrained foundation model allows combining data across assays and batches in a unified representation space. We present learned adapters for integration and analyze performance on joint analysis tasks. In the chapter, we also conduct transfer learning for cell type annotation and perturbation response prediction. We elaborate on the fine-tuning methods that rapidly adapt the foundation model for specialized downstream tasks. State-of-the-art level performances are demonstrated on all the above tasks across our benchmark experiment.

Chapter 5 leverages transfer learning for analyzing developmental dynamics. We first develop a framework of Adaptive Neighborhood Learning (ANL) for modeling cell state dynamics. We demonstrate its effectiveness for temporal estimation tasks such as RNA velocity and trajectory inference. We present applications on RNA velocity estimation for cell populations from a variety of normal and disease tissue samples.

Chapter 6 extends our approach to spatial transcriptomics via continuous pretraining. We discuss augmenting the model architecture and pretraining paradigm to expand the foundation model to this emerging domain. We demonstrate applications for spatial cell type deconvolution and spatial domain clustering.

Chapter 7 concludes by summarizing our framework for harnessing single-cell sequencing's data revolution through adaptable deep learning and transferable foundation models. We discuss future directions for expanding this data-driven approach to propel biological insights while scaling computational analysis.

## Chapter 2

## Background for single-cell omics and computational analysis

This chapter provides the necessary background concepts of machine learning and singlecell sequencing that make the thesis self-contained. Particularly, we depict the idea of the foundation model and its application for single-cell omics.

### 2.1 The emergence of single-cell omics

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was pioneered in 2009 and has since become a workhorse technique to profile the transcriptomes of individual cells [Tang et al., 2009]. By sequencing the messenger RNA from thousands of single cells in parallel, researchers can uncover new cell subtypes, lineage relationships, biomarkers, and regulatory networks. The droplet microfluidics developed for scRNA-seq allows massively parallel processing of single cells. Ongoing improvements in throughput, sensitivity, and cell capture efficiency continue to push the boundaries of what is possible with scRNA-seq.

Beyond gene expression, methods for profiling chromatin accessibility, methylation, and spatial conformation at the single-cell level have also emerged. Assay for Accessible Chromatin using sequencing (scATAC-seq) maps open chromatin regions in single cells that correspond to regulatory elements [Buenrostro et al., 2015]. Single-cell DNA methylation sequencing quantifies epigenetic regulation [Smallwood et al., 2014]. As illustrated in Figure 2.1, the burgeoning technologies have created tools for multi-omics data across the central dogma and now open opportunities for reconstructing cellular dynamics for various applications.

These single-cell omics assays are now being applied at large scales to create comprehensive atlases. The Human Cell Atlas project aims to create reference maps of all human cells as a basis for understanding health and disease [Regev et al., 2017]. Similar efforts for building cell atlases include HuBMAP [Consortium et al., 2019] and ENCODE [Luo et al., 2020]. These cell atlases provide an invaluable resource for the research community. The high-dimensional data enable the discovery of new cell states, biomarkers, and regulatory mechanisms. Ultimately, single-cell analysis promises to transform our understanding of tissue development, homeostasis, and disease pathogenesis, while also suggesting novel targets for drug discovery.

### 2.2 Computational questions for single-cell omics

Data integration Clustering and visualization of single-cell sequencing data encounter significant challenges due to batch effects arising from technical variation across samples and sequencing machines. This is particularly problematic for single-cell multi-omic (scMultiomic) data, which combines multiple views of genetic regulation including epigenetic, transcriptomic, and translational activities. Integrating scMultiomic data requires aggregating cell representations across assays, technologies, labs, and conditions, while preserving biological signals critical to downstream analysis [Subramanian et al., 2020, Miao et al., 2021].

However, current methods struggle to achieve this balance. Simple concatenation mixes signals irreconcilably. Canonical correlation analysis (CCA) discards complex nonlinear relationships. Existing deep learning approaches [Lopez et al., 2018, Ashuach et al., 2023] usually rely on Multi-layer perceptions (MLPs), failing to learn sufficiently expressive joint representations across large datasets. More powerful integration approaches are needed to align heterogeneous datasets into a unified encoding space that captures salient factors of variation. The ideal integrative representation would disentangle technical noise from biological variability and elucidate relationships between regulatory layers. Achieving this will enable synergistic multi-omic analyses like joint clustering, trajectory modeling, and cell-cell interaction mapping across data modalities.

Constructing integrative single-cell representations requires addressing key challenges: 1) Modeling complex nonlinear relationships between diverse assays while filtering technical noise. 2) Preventing collapse to simple mappings that destroy subtle signals. 3) Learning invariant encodings scalable to massive datasets. Tackling these issues will unlock the full potential of multi-omic single-cell analysis.

Celltype annotation Cell type annotation is a crucial step in single-cell analysis, enabling the identification and characterization of distinct cell populations within sequenced tissues. Accurate annotation allows researchers to delineate the composition of complex tissues, discover new subtypes, and understand cell lineages. While several methods have been proposed for cell annotation [Zhang et al., 2019, Aran et al., 2019, De Kanter et al., 2019], they face key limitations. Many rely on dimension reduction prior to model input, which can discard subtle transcriptional signals critical for disambiguating closely related popula- tions. Predefining clusters or references may bias downstream analyses. Finally, extensive retraining is often required to adapt models to new datasets, hindering generalization.

More powerful and generalizable approaches are needed to overcome these challenges. Ideal cell type annotation should 1) leverage full transcriptional signatures without information loss. 2) Detect novel cell states beyond predefined references. 3) Rapidly adapt to diverse datasets and taxonomies. Achieving this requires learning scalable and transferable representations directly from raw expression data. Recent advances in deep learning provide promising pathways, including end-to-end training of complex models on full gene expression and transfer learning to inherit knowledge across datasets. Developing annotation methods that balance model expressiveness, generalization, and adaptability will be crucial for unraveling complex tissue landscapes.

Differential Expression Analysis The identification of differentially expressed (DE) genes across distinct cellular populations is a fundamental aspect of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis. This process entails the comparison of cells subjected to varying experimental conditions, such as between cell types, disease subtypes, or varying differentiation stages. Subsequent to DE analysis, functional pathway enrichment is commonly employed as a subsequent phase. The elucidation of the functional roles of these DE genes enables researchers to ascertain the underlying mechanisms contributing to phenotypic variations observed under diverse conditions. This involves utilizing DE genes as inputs to scrutinize their involvement in shared biological pathways, thereby aiding in the interpretation of the data. It is noteworthy that common enrichment analysis methodologies, originally designed for bulk sequencing data, have also been applied to scRNA-seq data analysis.

Perturbation response prediction Recent advancements in sequencing and gene editing techniques have greatly facilitated large-scale perturbation experiments, allowing for the investigation of cellular responses to various genetic perturbations. Methods like CRISPR and siRNA knockdowns enable precise editing to elucidate gene function. The systematic perturbation of genes holds immense promise for uncovering novel interactions and advancing regenerative medicine. However, the vast combinatorial space of potential targets quickly surpasses the practical limits of experimental feasibility. Testing all possible combinations and dosages is infeasible. To address this challenge, computational methods aim to leverage knowledge gained from responses in known experiments and extrapolate to predict responses in unseen perturbations.

Accurate perturbation response prediction can guide experimental designs to critical manipulations. However, modeling the complex nonlinear dynamics of genetic networks requires learning interpretable representations of gene interaction mechanisms from limited training data. Simple regression or matrix factorization struggle to capture non-additive effects and higher-order relationships. More sophisticated approaches are needed to distill perturbation influences on transcriptional states into robust and transferable representations. Ideal methods should: 1) Model complex nonlinear gene relationships. 2) Generalize predictions to diverse cell types and doses. 3) Enable interpretation of key perturbation mechanisms. 4) Quantify uncertainties in extrapolation. Developing such predictive models will help systematically uncover gene regulatory networks and propel perturbation-based medicine.

Trajectory inference and RNA velocity Single-cell RNA-seq provides snapshots of transcriptomic states. However, biological systems undergo complex dynamic transitional processes between cell states, including differentiation, development, and disease progression. Trajectory inference aims to computationally order single cells along putative developmental trajectories to reconstruct lineage relationships and pseudotime progressions. This allows researchers to unravel cell fate decisions and model differentiation as a continuous process.

Recent innovations like RNA velocity utilize spliced/unspliced ratios to predict future state transitions based on inferred transcription dynamics [La Manno et al., 2018]. However, accurately reconstructing nonlinear developmental trajectories from sparse snapshots remains challenging. Methods that predefine trajectory topologies can bias analysis [Saelens et al., 2019]. Simple linear orderings fail to capture branching, cycling, and other complex dynamics [Bergen et al., 2021]. More flexible approaches are needed to de novo reconstruct globally optimal trajectories without topological constraints, while modeling complex nonlinearities like bifurcations.

Recent advances in deep learning provide promising directions for modeling cell state dynamics. Developing flexible, scalable, and theoretically grounded trajectory inference techniques will allow researchers to unravel complex developmental processes and disease progression at single-cell resolution. Accurately predicting cell fate decisions can also guide differentiation engineering efforts. This motivates the need for versatile trajectory modeling methods to reconstruct dynamics from sparse snapshots.

#### 2.2.1 Computational questions for spatial omics

The surrounding microenvironment of cells within tissue profoundly influences their behavior. Understanding the spatial arrangement and relative positioning of different cell types is crucial for deciphering the complexities of cellular organization and disease mechanisms. In tumor biology, for example, the knowledge of microenvironment and spatial profiling is key to advancing our understanding of cancer biology and devising targeted therapeutic strategies. Recent advancements in spatial profiling technologies have revolutionized gene expression analysis by incorporating spatial information within tissues. Currently, these spatial transcriptomics methods can be categorized into sequencing-based and imagingbased approaches.

Sequencing-based methods typically utilize spatial DNA barcoding. Examples include Spatial Transcriptomics (ST) [St˚ ahl et al., 2016] and SLIDE-seq Rodriques et al. [2019], which measure gene expression levels for thousands of genes at specific capture locations, termed spots. Notably, the commercialization of ST technology by 10x Genomics through their Visium platform has reduced the spot diameter from 100 µ m to 55 µ m. While SLIDE-seq offers higher resolution than Visium, it detects fewer unique molecules per spot, indicating a sensitivity trade-off. Furthermore, the recent 10x VisiumHD ( https: //10xgen.com/visiumhd ) platform achieves subcellular resolution with 2 µ m oligonucleotide arrays. However, most sequencing-based platforms still have a resolution larger than the single-cell level, so the gene signature of sequenced spots often reflects a mixture of local neighboring cells, necessitating cellular deconvolution.

Imaging-based spatial transcriptomics is exemplified by single-molecule fluorescent in situ hybridization (smFISH) [Raj et al., 2008], which allows for the simultaneous detection of multiple mRNA transcripts at subcellular resolution. Innovations like seqFISH [Lubeck et al., 2014, Shah et al., 2016], seqFISH+ [Eng et al., 2019], and MERFISH [Chen et al., 2015] have significantly increased the number of detectable mRNA species using multiplexed RNA probes. These methods enable the measurement of gene expression levels for hundreds to thousands of genes at subcellular resolution within individual cells.

Sequencing-based spatial transcriptomics offers a significant advantage in their ability to capture mRNAs without pre-specifying target genes. This capability allows for the characterization of both known and previously unidentified molecular features within tissue sections. By integrating gene expression data with histological features in a spatial context, these technologies provide researchers with additional layers of information. This enhanced insight is crucial for understanding developmental trajectories and dissecting the origins and progression of complex diseases. For example, spatial profiling contributed to tracing spatiotemporal development of the human heart [Asp et al., 2019], and identifying transcriptional changes in brain tissue domains in Alzheimer's disease [Chen et al., 2020]. Sequencing-based technologies have also proven instrumental in cancer research, offering novel insights into various cancer types [Berglund et al., 2018, Thrane et al., 2018, Moncada et al., 2020, Ji et al., 2020].

We highlight important computational analysis for spatial omics as follows.

Spatial domain clustering In spatial omics studies, a critical step involves clustering spots to delineate spatial domains that have coherent celltypes. Traditional clustering methods for single-cell data primarily focus on gene expression data alone, often resulting in clusters that lack spatial contiguity. To address this challenge, a new wave of spatial clustering methods has been developed, specifically designed to incorporate the spatial dependency of gene expression. Examples of such methods include those proposed by Pham et al. [2020], Zhao et al. [2021], and Hu et al. [2021]. These advanced techniques integrate spatial information into the clustering process, representing a pivotal development in the field of spatial omics.

The integration of spatial information in clustering enhances our understanding of the complex interplay between gene expression and tissue architecture. By considering both spatial and gene expression data, these methods provide a more comprehensive view of tissue organization and the underlying biological processes. This advancement in spatial clustering methodologies marks a significant step forward in the ability to study and interpret the intricate spatial relationships within tissues, ultimately contributing to a deeper understanding of cellular behavior and disease pathology.

Deconvolution of cell types Sequencing-based spatial transcriptomics typically has a resolution encompassing multiple cells, as previously mentioned, resulting in sequenced spots that are mixtures of nearby cells. The initial generation of ST technology featured spots with a diameter of 100 µ m, covering tens of cells. The more recent Visium platform has significantly improved this capability by reducing the spot diameter to 55 µ m.

In contrast, scRNA-seq provides gene expression profiling individual cells. The complementary nature of data from sequencing-based spatial transcriptomics and scRNA-seq presents opportunities for integrating these two datasets. One such approach is cell-type deconvolution, which aims to infer the spatial locations of different cell types within a tissue by matching spatial profiles with scRNA-seq references and determining the proportion of cells per spot. Although deconvolution is not a novel concept-it has been used in bulk RNA-seq to deduce cell-type composition using cell-type-specific gene expression from scRNA-seq [Newman et al., 2015, Wang et al., 2019], it encounters challenges when applied to sequencing-based spatial transcriptomics data. In bulk RNA-seq, deconvolution methods are designed to work with large aggregates of gene expression data, making them less suited for the nuanced spatial context required by spatial transcriptomics. Therefore, adapting these methods to spatial transcriptomics necessitates incorporating spatial information to accurately reflect the complex interactions within tissue architecture.

Predicting cell-cell communication Gene expression studies have historically emphasized the importance of intercellular communication in maintaining organ functionality and facilitating various crucial biological processes [Arneson et al., 2018, Armingol et al., 2021]. Since the physical interaction of ligands and receptors include the secreting and transportation of these molecules, the distance a ligand signal travels is pivotal in determining the nature of cell-cell signaling. Spatial omics significantly enriches this research area by providing comprehensive information about cell-cell communications within the spatial architecture of tissues.

Recently, several innovative methodologies have been introduced to analyze cell-cell communications based on gene expression data [Cang and Nie, 2020]. These approaches have opened new vistas for understanding the complex dynamics of cellular interactions in a spatially structured tissue environment. By leveraging spatial omics data, researchers can gain insights into how spatial context affects cellular communication and interaction networks. This understanding is crucial for elucidating the mechanisms underlying tissue development, homeostasis, and disease progression.

### 2.3 Machine learning concepts related to building single-cell foundation models

Foundation Model Foundation models are trained on expansive datasets, usually leveraging unlabeled data and self-supervised learning methods. These models are characterized by their adaptability, being fine-tuned for a wide array of downstream tasks Bommasani et al. [2021]. In the context of computational biology and particularly in single-cell analysis, these models offer the promise of broad applicability, allowing for the extraction of insights across a diverse spectrum of biological questions and challenges.

Self-supervised learning Self-supervised learning represents a paradigm shift in machine learning, wherein models autonomously generate their own supervisory signals from the input data, thereby obviating the need for externally provided labels. This approach contrasts starkly with conventional supervised learning methodologies, as self-supervised models are designed to create tasks grounded in the intrinsic characteristics of the data they process. Prominent examples of self-supervised tasks include predicting missing elements in sequences or reconstructing segments of input samples. In the realm of biological foundation models, a pertinent example of this approach would be the prediction of unexpressed or missing genes within a cell's gene expression profile. This methodology's relevance and potential in single-cell analysis, is profound, as it enables the extraction of meaningful insights from complex biological datasets without extensive manual annotation.

Pretraining It refers to the initial, extensive training phase of a model, utilizing a large dataset. This stage is predicated on the concept that a model can assimilate useful data representations, capturing general patterns and features before being fine-tuned for a specific application. During pretraining, the model optimizes a surrogate task, typically involving a self-supervised loss, allowing it to build a foundational understanding of the data. A prime example in the context of single-cell analysis is the scGPT, as detailed in (Chapter 3).

Transformer Transformers represent a seminal architecture in deep learning, employing a parallelized approach to computation, where the input is a set, and the output is a contextual representation of that set. Central to this architecture is the utilization of selfattention mechanisms (detailed in the next paragraph), which enable the model to assign varying degrees of importance to different elements in the input set. This capability is crucial for capturing long-range dependencies and complex relationships within the data, thus significantly enhancing the model's contextual understanding. Typically, a transformer comprises multiple stacked blocks, each performing self-attention functions and non-linear transformations. This architecture's adaptability and efficiency make it particularly suited for complex tasks in computational biology, such as modeling intricate cellular processes and interpreting vast genomic datasets.

This datadriven approach significantly enhances the model's ability to discern complex relationships and dependencies within the dataset, thereby facilitating a nuanced and context-aware processing capability. Token In the context of machine learning, particularly in Large Language Models (LLMs), a 'token' is the fundamental unit of data with semantic significance. For LLMs, tokens typically represent words or, in languages with complex character structures, smaller linguistic units [Vaswani et al., 2017]. In cellular foundation models, however, the concept of a token is adapted to represent genes, and we will demonstrate in later chapters the effectiveness of building single-cell foundation models with such gene tokens. It is crucial to note that the definition of a basic semantic unit varies depending on the data modality-ranging from peaks on nucleotide sequences in ATAC-Seq data to mRNAs in scRNA-seq. This adaptability in defining what constitutes a token is pivotal in ensuring that machine learning models are effectively tailored to the specificities of various biological datasets.

Tokenization Tokenization, a crucial step in the transformer pretraining workflow, involves decomposing input data into its elemental units-tokens. These tokens are subsequently transformed into numerical embeddings, which are then subjected to a series of non-linear operations within the model, particularly in the self-attention computation. The strategy employed in tokenization significantly influences the resultant model's performance and efficiency. Depending on the data modality and the specific task at hand, the approach to tokenization can vary significantly. For instance, in foundation models that utilize RNASeq data, the tokenization process typically involves decomposing the data of each cell into a set of tokens, each corresponding to a distinct gene. Similarly, in other modalities such as ATAC-Seq, tokens may represent sequences located at regions of open chromatin.

Fine-tuning Fine-tuning is the subsequent phase of model training after the pre-training stage, where a pre-trained model undergoes additional optimization specific to a target task. This stage involves the refinement of the model's parameters, tailored to enhance its performance on the desired task. In cellular foundation models, an example of fine-tuning could involve adapting a pre-trained model to excel in cell type annotation, achieved by directly minimizing the classification loss associated with this specific task.

In-context learning In-context learning is a methodology where a model learns to perform new tasks dynamically, without undergoing parameter updates. This process enables the model to adapt to a specific task merely by conditioning on relevant input-output examples present in its context window. By attending to these examples, the model learns to replicate the task, demonstrating a remarkable capacity for flexibility and adaptability, which is particularly advantageous in rapidly evolving fields such as computational biology and single-cell analysis.

Transfer learning Transfer learning is a technique term wherein knowledge acquired from one task is applied to another, distinct task. Both the aforementioned fine-tuning and in-context learning can be considered as common implementations of transfer learning. In foundational models, this typically involves a two-phase process: pretraining on a large, diverse dataset, followed by fine-tuning for a specific task. This methodology enables the transfer of a broad understanding, acquired during the pretraining phase, to the specific requirements of a particular task, thereby enhancing the model's effectiveness and adaptability in diverse application scenarios.

##### Translating biological data into computational representations using embed-

dings Embeddings play a critical role in machine learning, particularly in the context of computational biology. They are high-dimensional, continuous vector representations of data, which capture the underlying properties of tokens (such as genes or proteins) in a form that computational models can process. These representations are crucial as they allow the complex and high-dimensional nature of biological data to be translated into a format conducive to machine learning analysis. Embeddings are typically learned during the training process, enabling the model to encapsulate and leverage the inherent structures and relationships within the biological data, such as the functional relationships between genes or specific cell types.

### 2.4 Existing machine learning methods for single-cell omics

##### Learning cell and gene representation for scRNA-seq on downstream tasks. Cell

representation learning facilitates a variety of downstream tasks such as cell type annotation, multi-omic integration, and perturbation prediction. A popular framework Seurat [Satija et al., 2015, Stuart et al., 2019] employs nearest-neighbor-based alignment to remove batch effect via linear transformation in the embedding space. LIGER [Liu et al., 2020] uses matrix factorization to extract latent cell embeddings. Recently, Deep Learning methods and especially VAE-based generative models have gained increasing popularity, as they generate deep embeddings via non-linear transformations through neural networks [Kingma and Welling, 2013]. scVI [Lopez et al., 2018] learns latent cell representations by reconstructing original gene expression via variational inference. TotalVI [Gayoso et al., 2021], scGen [Lotfollahi et al., 2019], CPA [Lotfollahi et al., 2021] and MultiCPA [Inecik et al., 2022] utilized similar models and extended the application to multi-omics and perturbation prediction. On the other hand, gene representation learning also supports many downstream tasks including gene regulatory network and functional pathway analysis. As an example, GeneVector [Ceglia et al., 2022] detects gene-gene functional relations by factorizing the co-expression and mutual information matrix of the sequencing readout. VEGA [Seninge et al., 2021] utilizes a sparse VAE architecture to encode gene network activities for added interpretability. DeepMAPS [Ma et al., 2021] utilizes graph neural networks to encode cell and gene nodes for related tasks. Despite the importance of the two branches of research in cell and gene embedding learning, few approaches have worked on jointly learning both.

Transformers for modelling single-cell sequencing data. Transformer models employing self-attention mechanisms [Vaswani et al., 2017] have demonstrated remarkable success in the field of natural language processing (NLP) [Devlin et al., 2018], computer vision [Dosovitskiy et al., 2020], and protein folding prediction [Jumper et al., 2021]. However, there have been few efforts to incorporate the transformer architecture into single-cell biology and its related applications. Shen et al. [2022] utilize a transformer decoder setup to learn the sequence of the names of highly expressed genes, but they do not take into account the actual sequenced expression abundance, resulting in the loss of crucial biological information. scBERT [Wang et al., 2021] and TOSICA [Chen et al., 2023] used BERT-like architectures [Devlin et al., 2018] to train cell embeddings but only applied the model on the supervised task of cell annotation. Similarly, Connell et al. [2022] utilized transformer encoders mainly to predict genetic perturbation responses.

Pre-training on large cell atlases Although the idea of employing pre-training and fine-tuning on a wide range of downstream tasks as a unified framework remains largely unexplored, several works have attempted to use transfer learning on specific tasks. scArches [Lotfollahi et al., 2022] devised a transfer-learning-based approach for reference mapping by pre-training a conditional variational autoencoder (VAE) on the reference datasets. However, the scale of the reference datasets remains limited, and the VAE-based architecture does not incorporate attention computation. On the other hand, scBERT [Wang et al., 2021] is pre-trained on 1 million cells with a BERT architecture. Their downstream application focuses on cell type annotation only, thus restricting the generalizability of the pre-training and fine-tuning strategy. Our work, scGPT [Cui et al., 2024] pretrained on over 33 million normal cells and demonstrated strong performance on various tasks via fine-tuning and zero-shot learning. Geneformer [Theodoris et al., 2023], has extended pretrained transformers beyond cell type annotation to gene network analysis, but the work didn't demonstrate abilities for perturbation response prediction or multi-omic integration. UCE [Rosen et al., 2023] is a larger model with 650 million parameters focusing on learning cell embeddings. A recent work, Nicheformer [Schaar et al., 2024], also expands the pretraining onto spatial omics.

### 3.1 Motivation

Recent sequencing technology advancements in the diversity of data modalities extend our understanding beyond genetics to epigenetics, transcriptomics, and proteomics, thus providing multi-modal insights [Subramanian et al., 2020, Miao et al., 2021]. These breakthroughs have also raised new research questions such as reference mapping, perturbation prediction, and multi-omic integration [Lotfollahi et al., 2019, 2021, 2022, Cao and Gao, 2022, Zhang et al., 2023]. Given the rapid expansion of sequencing data, it is critical to parallelly develop methodologies capable of effectively harnessing, enhancing, and adapting to these burgeoning developments. One promising approach to address this challenge is the generative pre-training of foundation models [Bommasani et al., 2021, Moor et al., 2023]. Generative pre-training has recently achieved unprecedented success across various fields by learning from extensive datasets. The most well-known applications include computer vision and natural language generation (NLG) [OpenAI., 2023a, OpenAI et al., 2023, OpenAI., 2023b]. These foundation models such as DALL-E2 and GPT-4 follow a paradigm of pretraining transformers on large-scale diverse datasets [OpenAI et al., 2023, OpenAI., 2023b] that can be readily customized for a variety of downstream tasks and scenarios. More interestingly, these generative pre-trained models consistently outperform task-specific models trained from scratch [Gururangan et al., 2020, Sun et al., 2019, Qiu et al., 2020a]. This indicates a task-agnostic understanding of knowledge in these domains, inspiring us to explore its adoption for sing-cell omics research. However, current machine-learning-based methods in single-cell research are rather scattered, with specific models dedicated to distinct analysis tasks [Liu et al., 2021a, Oller-Moreno et al., 2021, Ji et al., 2021a]. As a result, the datasets used in each study are often limited in breadth and scale [Angerer et al., 2017]. To confront this limitation, there is a need for a foundation model that is pre-trained on large-scale data and can comprehend the complex interactions between genes across diverse tissues. We expect that such a model would provide a solid foundation and contribute to the discovery of new biological insights by leveraging the knowledge learned from millions of sequenced cells.

To enhance the modeling of large-scale data of single-cell sequencing, we draw inspiration from the self-supervised pre-training workflow in NLG. The self-attention transformer has been verified as an effective and efficient architecture to model input tokens of words [Vaswani et al., 2017]. While texts are made up of words, cells can be characterized by genes and the protein products they encode. By learning gene and cell embeddings simultaneously, akin to word and sentence embeddings in NLG, we can better comprehend the characteristics of cells based on the genes they express. Moreover, the flexible nature of transformer input tokens enables easy incorporation of additional features and meta information.

The combination of large-scale diverse datasets and pre-trained transformers has emerged as a promising approach for developing foundation models. Drawing parallels between linguistic constructs and cellular biology, we probe the applicability of foundation models to advance cellular biology and genetics research. Utilizing the burgeoning single-cell sequencing data, we have pioneered the construction of a foundation model for single-cell biology, scGPT, which is based on generative pre-trained transformer across a repository of over 33 million cells. Our findings illustrate that scGPT, a generative pre-trained transformer, effectively distills critical biological insights concerning genes and cells. Through the further adaptation of transfer learning, scGPT can be optimized to achieve superior performance across diverse downstream applications. This includes tasks such as cell-type annotation, multi-batch integration, multi-omic integration, genetic perturbation prediction, and gene network inference.

### 3.2 Data preparation and data structure for large-sclae pretraining

To collect diverse and extensive sequencing data for the self-supervised pre-training of scGPT, we assembled 33 million scRNA-seq data of human cells under normal (non-disease) conditions, obtained via the CELLxGENE collection [Chanzuckerberg Initiative., 2022]. This comprehensive dataset encompasses a wide range of cell types from 51 organs/tissues and 441 studies, providing a rich representation of cellular heterogeneity across the human body.

#### 3.2.1 Data collection

We collected data for the whole-human foundation model pre-training from the CELLxGENE portal [Chanzuckerberg Initiative., 2022] using the Census API 1 . We included sequencing protocols of scRNA-seq and snRNA-seq and filtered in samples without disease conditions. This resulted in sequencing data of 33 million cells. We also trained several organ-specific models on samples from individual organs and tissues. For example, we retrieved over 10.3 million human blood and bone marrow scRNA-seq samples [Chanzuckerberg Initiative., 2022] for the pre-training of the scGPT blood model specifically. A total of 65 datasets were collected from CELLxGENE by filtering on Organism (i.e., Homo sapiens), Tissue (i.e., blood, bone marrow), and Disease (i.e., normal, COVID-19, influenza). Additionally, we collected 5.7 million cells of various cancer types to train a pan-cancer model.

#### 3.2.2 Data processing

The single-cell sequencing data is processed into a cell-by-gene matrix, X ∈ R N × G , where each element X i,j ∈ R + represents the read count of an RNA molecule in the case of scRNAseq or a peak region in the case of scATAC-seq. Specifically, in scRNA-seq, the element denotes the RNA abundance for gene j ∈ 0 , 1 , . . . , G in cell i ∈ 0 , 1 , . . . , N . In subsequent sections, we will refer to this matrix as the raw matrix. The input to scGPT consists of three main components: (1) gene (or peak) tokens, (2) expression values, and (3) condition tokens. For each modelling task, the gene tokens and expression values are pre-processed from the raw count matrix X accordingly.

A fundamental challenge in gene expression modelling is the variability in absolute magnitudes across different sequencing protocols [Sarkar and Stephens, 2021]. #### 3.3.3 Cell representation

Each cell is analogous to a 'sentence' composed of genes, and its representation h ( i ) c ∈ R D is obtained by aggregating the learned gene-level representations h ( i ) n . Various pooling operations, such as element-wise mean-pooling or weighted-pooling, can be readily employed in this context. In this study, we opt to employ a special token &lt; cls &gt; for the cell representation, enabling the model to learn the pooling operation within transformer blocks. The &lt; cls &gt; token is appended to the beginning of the input tokens, and the final embedding at this position is extracted as the cell representation. Consequently, the cell embedding h ( i ) c can be extracted by the corresponding row in the stacked final-layer embeddings h ( i ) n [ &lt; cls &gt; ], where the [ &lt; cls &gt; ] operation retrieves the row at the index of the &lt; cls &gt; token position.

#### 3.3.4 Generative pre-training

##### Foundation model pre-training

The foundation model is designed to be a generalizable feature extractor that can benefit a diverse range of downstream tasks. The token vocabulary used in pre-training contains the entire set of genes in the human genome. The expression values were binned prior to model pre-training (Section 3.3.1). To speed up the training, we restrict the input to only genes with non-zero expression for each input cell. To efficiently train the model to capture gene-gene relation and gene-cell relation, we introduce a new generative training strategy with specialized attention masks as described in the following section.

##### Attention mask for generative pre-training

Self-attention has been widely used to capture the co-occurrence patterns among tokens. In natural language processing, this has been achieved mainly in two ways: (1) masked token prediction used in transformer encoder models such as BERT [Devlin et al., 2018] and Roberta [Liu et al., 2019a], where randomly masked tokens in the input sequence are predicted in the model's output; (2) auto-regressive generation with sequential prediction in causal transformer decoder models such as the OpenAI GPT series [Radford et al., 2019, Brown et al., 2020, OpenAI et al., 2023]. The generative pre-training used in OpenAI GPT3 [Brown et al., 2020] and GPT4 [OpenAI et al., 2023] employs a unified framework in which the model predicts the most likely next token from a 'prompt' consisting of known input tokens. This framework offers great flexibility to be utilized in various natural language generation (NLG) applications and demonstrates new capabilities such as contextual awareness in zero-shot and fine-tuned settings [Bubeck et al., 2023]. We believe that generative training can be beneficial to single-cell models in a similar manner. Specifically, we are interested in two tasks: (1) generating unknown gene expression values based on known gene expression, i.e., generation by 'gene prompts', and (2) generating whole genome expression given an input cell type condition, i.e., generation by 'cell prompts'.

Despite similar ideas of tokens and prompts, modelling genetic reads is inherently different from natural language due to the non-sequential nature of the data. Unlike words in a sentence, the order of genes within a cell is interchangeable, and there is no equivalent concept of the 'next gene' to predict. This makes it challenging to apply the causal masking formulation from GPT models directly in single-cell data. To address this challenge, we developed a specialized attention masking mechanism for scGPT that defines the order of prediction based on attention scores.

In each generation iteration, scGPT predicts the gene expression values of a new set of genes, and these genes in turn become the 'known genes' in the next iteration for attention computation. This approach reflects the causal masking design with next token prediction in the conventional transformer decoders by making sequential predictions in the non-sequential single-cell data.

During the inference for cell-prompt generation, scGPT generates all genomewide gene expression conditioned on the specific cell types. A trained cell embedding is inputted at the first position representing the cell type condition. The whole generation process of thousands of gene expression values is conducted in K iterative steps (i.e., K = 3 steps in Figure 3.2B). For example, in one iteration i ∈ { 1 , 2 , . . . K } , the attention masking mechanism allows attention with all predicted genes from previous 0 to i -1 iterations. In each iteration, scGPT selects the top 1 /K genes from the unknown set with the highest prediction confidence to be included as known genes in the next iteration i +1. Intuitively, this workflow streamlines the generation of gene expression in an auto-regressive manner, where gene expression values with highest prediction confidence are first generated and used to help subsequent rounds of generation. The gene-prompt generation works similarly in an iterative manner. The difference is that it starts with a set of known genes with observed expression values, instead of a cell embedding.

The scGPT attention masking unifies the encoding process of known genes and the generation of unknown genes. It also stands as one of the first transformer schemes to conduct auto-regressive generation for non-sequential data.

### 3.5 Summary

In this chapter, we present the approach for building a single-cell foundation model, scGPT, by generative pre-training on over 33 million cells. We introduce new techniques to address the methodology and engineering challenges of pre-training on large-scale single-cell omic data. To handle large-scale data, we use an in-memory data structure that allows fast access to store hundreds of datasets. We establish a unified generative pre-training workflow specifically for the non-sequential omic data. and adapt the transformer architecture to simultaneously learn cell and gene representations.

We introduce scGPT, a pioneering generative pretrained foundation model that harnesses the power of pre-trained transformers on a vast amount of single-cell data. Building upon the success of self-supervised pre-training in language models like chatGPT and GPT4 [OpenAI., 2023a, OpenAI et al., 2023], we adopted a similar approach in the single-cell domain to unravel complex biological interactions. The use of transformers in scGPT enables the simultaneous learning of gene and cell embeddings, which facilitates the modeling of various aspects of cellular processes. By leveraging the attention mechanism of transformers, scGPT captures gene-to-gene interactions at the single-cell level, providing an additional layer of interpretability.

Therefore, we aim to correct batch effects while preserving biological variances when integrating multiple scRNAseq datasets. ### 4.3 Perturbation response prediction

To fine-tune for the perturbation response prediction task, we selected highly variable genes and pre-processed the expression values prior to model training. The parameters of the embedding layers and transformer layers from the pre-trained model were utilized to initialize the fine-tuned model. During fine-tuning, all gene tokens with both zero and non-zero expression values were included. Two notable changes were adopted for the input in the perturbation prediction task: First, we used log 1 p transformed expression values as input and target values instead of binned values, to better predict the absolute post-perturbation expression for this task. Second, we appended a binary condition token at each input gene position to indicate whether the gene is perturbed. We adopted the perturb-GEP (Section 4.3.1) fine-tuning objective with further modifications to the training setup. Instead of utilizing the masked and unmasked expression values of the same cell as the input and learning target, we employed a control cell as the input and the perturbed cell as the target. This was achieved by randomly pairing a non-perturbed control cell with each perturbed cell to construct the input-target pairs. The input values consisted of all the non-perturbed gene expression values in the control cells. Consequently, the model learned to predict the post-perturbation responses based on the control gene expression.

#### 4.3.1 Learning objective

In perturbation prediction, the model is required to predict perturbed gene expression values instead of the original values. The learning is based on the aforementioned objective GEP as in equation 4.4. We refer to this variation as perturb-GEP . We maintain the MLP estimator in equation 4.4, but utilize the post-perturbation gene expression as the target x ( i ) j . In perturb-GEP, the model is supposed to predict the post-perturbation expression of all input genes.

#### 4.3.2 Experiments for Prediction of unseen gene perturbations

For the perturbation prediction task, we evaluated our model using three perturbation datasets of leukemia cell lines: the Adamson Pertub-seq dataset [Adamson et al., 2016] consisting of 87 one-gene perturbations, the curated Replogle Perturb-Seq dataset [Replogle et al., 2022] consisting of 1,823 one-gene perturbations, and the Norman Perturb-Seq dataset [Norman et al., 2019] consisting of 131 two-gene perturbations and 105 one-gene perturbations. To assess scGPT's perturbation prediction capability, we fine-tuned the model on a subset of perturbations. The model is trained to reconstruct the perturbed expression profile given an input control cell state and the genes of intervention. Then, it is tested on perturbations involving unseen genes. We calculated the Pearson delta metric, which measures the correlation between predicted and observed post-perturbation expression changes. Additionally, we reported this metric on the top 20 most significantly changed genes for each perturbation, namely DE genes, denoted as Pearson delta on DE genes. See Section 4.6.2 for the details on metric calculations. We conducted a performance comparison between scGPT and two other recent methods, GEARS [Roohani et al., 2022] and a linear regression (LR) baseline. Our results demonstrate that scGPT achieves the highest scores on all three datasets(Figure 4.4A, Table 4.4). Particularly, scGPT excels in predicting post-perturbation changes, consistently outperforming the other methods by 5 to 20% margins. Additionally, we visualize the predictions for two example perturbations in the Adamson dataset in Figure 4.4B, where scGPT accurately predicts the trend of expression change for all top 20 DE genes.

To elaborate on our benchmarking experiments, we compared scGPT against the recent perturbation prediction methods GEARS [Roohani et al., 2022] and a linear regression model. The linear regression model takes the gene expression values from a control cell and the binary encoding of perturbation status on each gene as input features. The model uses a linear combination of input features to estimate the post-perturbation expression of each gene by minimizing the regression error. To ensure consistency, we followed the preprocessing steps outlined by Roohani et al. [2022] in their benchmark. 25 % of perturbations were split into the test set and remained unseen during training. While the CPA method [Lotfollahi et al., 2021] was reported as suboptimal in the GEARS study, we corroborated these findings in our experiments. However, we attribute this performance gap largely to the disparity in experimental settings. CPA was primarily designed to learn shared perturbation embeddings applicable to unseen cell types, which deviates from our focus on entirely unseen perturbations. To ensure equitable benchmarking, we excluded CPA from the final comparison. We trained all models in the same settings as follows and reported the evaluation results. Initially, gene expression values were normalized per cell using the total counts across all genes, and a logarithmic transformation was applied. Subsequently, we selected 5,000 highly variable genes and incorporated any perturbed genes that were not initially considered into the gene set. In the experiments, for one-gene perturbations in all three datasets Adamson et al. [2016], Replogle et al. [2022], Norman et al. [2019], the perturbations are split to ensure that test perturbations are not seen in training, i.e., no cells in the training set has undergone any of the test perturbations. For two-gene perturbations in the Norman et al. [2019] dataset, the train-test split consists of three scenarios with increasing difficulty: (1) 0/2 unseen genes, (2) 1/2 unseen genes, and (3) 2/2 unseen genes in the training set.

To evaluate the accuracy of perturbation prediction, the expression changes ('delta') between the post-perturbation and the control cell states are examined. We calculated the Pearson correlation between the predicted and observed expression changes in data, denoted as Pearson delta . We also reported these Pearson metrics on the top 20 genes of most differentially expressed (DE genes). Thus, we presented the additional evaluation metric for the DE conditions, namely Pearson delta on DE genes. See Section 4.6.2 for details of the metric calculation. Additionally, we tested the evaluation of the Pearson correlation ( Pearson ) between the predicted gene expression and the ground-truth expression values. In a comparative analysis presented in Section 4.5.2, we found that delta-based evaluations render a more faithful representation of model performance than mere correlation with ground-truth expression levels.

#### 4.3.3 In-silico reverse perturbation prediction

scGPT is also capable of predicting the source of genetic perturbation for a given resulting cell state, which we refer to as in-silico reverse perturbation prediction. An ideal prediction model conducting such reverse prediction can be used to infer important driving genes for lineage development or facilitate the discovery of potential therapeutic gene targets. A hypothetical example application of such capability could be to predict CRISPR target genes that influence cells to recover from a disease state. To showcase the effectiveness of reverse perturbation prediction, we utilized a subset of the Norman dataset focusing on perturbations involving 20 genes (Figure 4.5A). This combinatorial space consists of a total of 210 one-gene or two-gene perturbation combinations. We fine-tuned scGPT using 40 (19%) known perturbations (the train group in Figure 4.5A). We then tested the model on queries of unseen perturbed cell states, where scGPT successfully predicted the source of perturbations that would generate the observed results. Specifically, after ranking the top predictions for seven test examples, we found that scGPT accurately retrieved the actual source perturbations. For example, scGPT ranked the correct perturbation of CNN1+MAPK1 as the top prediction for one test example, and the correct perturbation of FOSB+UBASH3B was ranked as the second prediction for another case (Figure 4.5A). Overall, scGPT identified on average 91.4% relevant perturbations (6.4/7) within the top 1 predictions (the blue bars in Figure 4.5B), and 65.7% correct perturbations (4.6/7 test cases) within the top 8 predictions (the red bars in Figure 4.5B), outperforming GEARS and the differential gene baseline by considerable margin. We envision these predictions can be used for planning perturbation experiments by maximizing the possibility of deriving target cell states. Compared to random tryouts, which would on average require 105.5 attempts out of the 210 possible perturbations in this subset, finding the correct source of genetic change within fewer attempts offers a valuable tool for accelerating the discovery of important genetic drivers and optimizing perturbation experiments.

Experiment settings for the reverse perturbation prediction: we selected 20 genes to construct a perturbation use case from the Norman dataset and to fine-tune and test a new perturbation model. This subset of 20 genes is selected by maximizing the proportion of ground-truth perturbation data for both training and test cases based on scGPT's traintest split. This selected subspace contains 40 training cases, 2 validation cases, and 7 test cases out of the 210 unique perturbation combinations. The rest are unseen cases without experiment results. The reverse perturbation prediction follows a topK retrieval task setting: we used the predicted responses from all 210 perturbation conditions as the reference data, and the ground-truth responses from the 7 test cases as the query set. The goal is to retrieve the top perturbation conditions that generate the most similar responses as to a query result. For the reference data, instead of having a single representative gene expression profile for each perturbation condition, we obtained the predicted responses from 30 randomly sampled control cells for added diversity. This yields a reference database containing 6,300 predicted post-perturbation gene expression profiles. For each test case of X+Y perturbation, we used the ground-truth gene expression profiles from all cells that underwent X+Y perturbation as the query set. For TopK retrieval, we designed an ensemble voting strategy that involves two rounds of selection. In the first round, each query cell selects its top K most similar expression profiles by Euclidean distance from the reference dataset. In the second round, we rank the candidate perturbation conditions by the number of votes received from all query cells. We report the top K most voted perturbation conditions as the predicted source perturbation conditions from the second round of ranking after ensemble voting. We evaluated the retrieval performance by a modified topK accuracy metric for correct retrieval (i.e., exact match) and relevant retrieval (i.e., matching at least one gene in the actual perturbation combination) as detailed in Section 4.6.2. We reported scGPT's average scores on 5 runs of different random seeds. We benchmarked scGPT's retrieval performance with GEARS [Roohani et al., 2022] and differential genes. For benchmark with GEARS, we used GEARS' predicted expression profiles as reference database, and reported the same metrics up to topK retrievals. For differential genes, we identified for each test perturbation the top two differentially expressed (DE) genes by the Wilcoxon rank-sum test between the perturbed cells and control cells. We considered these top two DE genes as the top one predicted 2-gene perturbation combination. We reported the top 1 retrieval metrics only for differential expressions since the perturbation combinations become ambiguous as the DE gene list expands.

#### 4.3.4 Perturbation expression prediction with KnockTF evaluation

We extended our perturbation prediction evaluation on the Replogle dataset [Replogle et al., 2022] by comparing further with the Transcription Factor (TF) expression profiles of bulk perturbation experiments from the KnockTF database [Feng et al., 2020]. Specifically, we used the model trained on the Perturb-seq Replogle dataset to predict the perturbation response and test the alignment of the results with the records in the KnockTF. This represents a unique challenge stemming from differences between training and inference data. The scGPT model was originally trained on single-cell data and evaluated on its ability to generalize to unseen bulk expression inputs and predictions.

The KnockTF database contains bulk gene expression profiles for 72 TFs that used the same cell line of K562 and CRISPRi intervention technology as in Replogle dataset. These profiles contain the pre- and post-perturbation mean expressions. After removing TFs with missing recorded gene expression, we retained 63 TF perturbations to be tested. These 63 test TFs can be split into two groups: 1) The perturbation TFs that have been included in the gene expression profiles of Replogle dataset (n=40), and 2) perturbations of totally unseen TFs (n=23). For each TF perturbation, we obtained a predicted bulk expression profile from the scGPT model by inputting the bulk gene expression record pre-perturbation and the information of the perturbed TF. We then compared the predicted expressions with the corresponding ground-truth post-perturb bulk expression profiles from KnockTF. Since the gene expression values are on different scales from single-cell to bulk experiments, we reported Spearman delta correlation scores on all gene expression changes, as well as the 20 highest-ranked DE genes for each TF perturbation condition. We benchmarked with the GEARS model that was trained on Replogle dataset and tested in the same settings as above.

In Table 4.5, scGPT showcased competitive performance in predicting gene expression changes captured by the Spearman delta and Spearman delta + de scores. This endorsed scGPT's ability to encode important changes in the gene network elicited by perturbations and to generalize to unseen datasets. GEARS on the other hand, tend to predict small expression changes post-perturbation similar to the predicted values on single-cell data that fail to generalize to the bulk gene expressions. Furthermore, scGPT offers the unique capability of predicting perturbation results for TFs that are not included in gene expression profiles originally (i.e. the unseen group), which is primarily thanks to the advantage of the transformer architecture, so the new gene tokens of the unseen TFs can be easily appended to the input.

### 4.5 Discussion

#### 4.5.1 Analyzing the transfer learning of pre-trained knowledge: the scaling and in-context effects

In previous sections, scGPT has demonstrated great potential via fine-tuning in a transfer learning manner. We further confirmed the benefits of using the foundation model by comparing it with similar transformer models trained for each downstream task from scratch without pre-training (denoted as scGPT (from-scratch)). The results are presented in Tables 4.3, 4.1, and 4.2, where the fine-tuned scGPT consistently showcases performance gain for tasks such as integration and cell type annotation. Given the observed contributions of the foundation model for downstream tasks, we are further interested in exploring the factors influencing the transfer learning process.

Firstly, we delve into the relationship between pre-training data size and the performance of fine-tuned models: For a certain analysis task, how much improvement can be gained by further adding sequencing data into atlases for pre-training. We pre-trained a series of scGPT models of the same number of parameters but using different sizes of data, from 30K to 33M sequenced normal human cells. Figure 4.7A illustrates the resulting performance of fine-tuning on various applications using these different pre-trained models. We observed the performance of fine-tuned models improved as the size of the pre-training data increased (Box 4.1). These results indicate a scaling effect, suggesting that larger pre-training data size leads to better pre-trained embeddings and improved performance on downstream tasks. On the other hand, we acknowledge the importance of considering model size alongside data size. Previous research has shown a correlation between optimal model size and data size, as discussed [Kaplan et al., 2020]. While some scaling trend curves (e.g. annotation and reference mapping) in Figure 4.7A may appear to reach a saturation point after the 3M data size, we hypothesize that increasing the model size would lead to improved performance even at 33M data size and beyond. We leave the exploration of varying model sizes to future work.

Notably, our findings also align with the scaling law reported in natural language models [Kaplan et al., 2020], highlighting the significant role of data size in model performance. The crucial role of pre-training data size in fine-tuning results points to a promising future for pre-trained models in the single-cell domain. As larger and more diverse datasets become available, we can anticipate further improvements in model performance, advancing our understanding of cellular processes.

##### Box 4.2: Context-specific pre-training and its influence on integration performance

We pre-trained scGPT on cell atlases including all human cell types, envisioning that it contributes to specific downstream applications primarily via fine-tuning. Given that downstream applications typically focus on a limited subset of tissues or cell types, we are prompted to consider whether it would be more beneficial to enhance performance for a specific fine-tuning task through context-specific pre-training. To be specific, for the downstream fine-tuning on specific contexts of certain cell types, we are interested in comparing the contributions of three different pre-trained models: (1) scGPT (whole-human), which is the model pre-trained generally on all human cell types; (2) in-context models, which during pre-training have seen similar cell types as in the downstream applications; (3) out-of-context models, which are pretrained primarily on different tissues or cell types. To conduct this comparison, we tested a range of pre-trained models on the scRNA-seq data integration task. These pre-trained models were originally trained using distinct tissue-specific datasets, including lung, blood, heart, kidney, brain, pancreas, intestine, and an all-inclusive, whole-human dataset. In particular, we subsampled from the original whole-human dataset to generate a dataset of 13.2 million cells, aligning it in size with the blood (10.3 million cells) and brain (13.2 million cells) datasets for a more direct comparison of the impact of context. We employed each of the eight pre-trained models to perform data integration on the COVID-19 dataset [Lotfollahi et al., 2022], followed by a comparative performance analysis. Given its diverse cellular composition, including Lung, PBMC, and Bone Marrow cells, this dataset provides an ideal platform for investigating the effects of employing pre-trained models from different cellular contexts.

Our analysis produced notable results, revealing a clear correlation between the relevance of the model context used in the pre-training initiatives and their subsequent performance on the COVID-19 dataset. Notably, the top performers in this analysis were models pre-trained on whole-human, blood, and lung datasets, which correspond closely to the cell types present in the COVID-19 dataset. Specifically, the cellular context of the blood model aligns more closely with the COVID-19 dataset, which includes immune cells, bone marrow cells, lung cells, and PBMCs. Thus, it becomes evident that the alignment of the cellular context in the pre-training phase plays a critical role in achieving superior results for downstream data integration tasks, even when datasets of similar sizes are compared. In light of our findings, the whole-human pre-trained model, embodying a vast spectrum of cell types, consistently demonstrates robust performance across diverse analyses.

In summary, our analysis demonstrates the contribution of pre-trained knowledge to downstream analysis, the scaling law between pre-training data size and fine-tuning results, and the impact of context-specific pre-training. These findings highlight the potential of pre-trained models to advance our understanding of cellular biology and pave the way for future discoveries.

#### 4.5.2 Revisiting evaluation metrics for perturbation response prediction

The field of perturbation response prediction represents an emergent yet highly promising application. Existing studies commonly employ correlation-based metrics for evaluation. Specifically, correlations between predicted post-perturbation gene expression values as well as gene expression changes ('delta'), have been reported in existing methods of GEARs [Roohani et al., 2022] and CPA [Lotfollahi et al., 2021].

In this work, we specifically examined these correlational metrics and opted for an evaluation paradigm focused on expression changes ('delta'): We found that the evaluations by Pearson delta and its variants, as reported in Table 4.4, can better capture the difference between methods and faithfully indicate the prediction quality. Contrary to this, we delineate the following reasons to argue that metrics based on raw expression levels may introduce bias:

- (1) Bias Toward Natural Transcriptional Variance: Utilizing Pearson correlation metrics on expression levels introduces bias as it predominantly captures natural variances in transcriptional abundance across genes. For instance, housekeeping genes, which are sufficiently expressed across varying conditions, can contribute to inflated correlation scores. To demonstrate this phenomenon, we report a naive baseline that employs the mean expression level of control cells as the prediction for perturbation responses. Remarkably, despite its inability to predict expression changes, this baseline yielded high Pearson scores across all evaluated datasets, as showcased in Table 4.7. This pinpoints the issue that the correlation on raw expression is mostly evaluating the background expression variances among genes, rather than the differences specific to the perturbed conditions.
- (2) Inflation of Performance Metrics: Metrics based on raw expression levels frequently present inflated scores, often surpassing the 0.95 threshold, as indicated in Table 4.7.

This overestimation can be misleading, as it may imply that the challenge of predicting perturbation responses has been adequately addressed. In contrast, as reported in Table 4.4, metrics on the delta-change reveal that the predictions are far from perfect, especially on the most challenging genome-wide Perturb-seq Replogle dataset. Although the comparative trends among different methodologies remain consistent under both metric categories, delta-change metrics provide a more stringent and informative evaluation.

In summary, our findings advocate for the adoption of delta-change metrics in evaluating perturbation response predictions. These metrics not only circumvent the biases inherent in raw-expression-based evaluations but also offer a more granular understanding of performance differences among competing methodologies.

#### 4.5.3 Discussion about future prospects and limitations

We have presented scGPT as a promising attempt to build single-cell foundation models. Although at a relatively early stage, we expect foundation models can leverage the knowledge from large-scale cell atlases and transfer them for contribution to the various analyses for specific research questions on custom data, potentially providing new biological insights. In this section, we identify some of the important prospects and limitations for this direction based on our observations and considerations.

Zero-shot applications In Section 4.4, we examined the zero-shot applications for data integration and reference mapping. The pre-trained scGPT model demonstrates considerable abilities for these tasks off-the-shell. Shown in Table 4.6, the zero-shot scGPT model displays noteworthy performance that is on par with other specialized methods. Shown in Figure 4.9, zero-shot embeddings provide better integration performance than principal component-based approaches for highly variable genes. The advantage is demonstrated by the higher AvgBIO scores on two out of three datasets and AvgBATCH scores on all three datasets. On the COVID-19 dataset, zero-shot performance closely approximates that of the fine-tuned model and surpasses both Seurat V3 and Harmony, as evidenced by comparisons between Figures 4.9. In the context of the Lung-Kim dataset, the zero-shot embeddings distinctly segregate NK cell populations from T cells within the UMAP visualization. Moreover, within the Multiple Sclerosis dataset, zero-shot embeddings effectively isolate neurons of cortical layers 5-6 from other layers, which is not accomplished by utilizing the UMAP of highly variable genes (HVGs). Based on these findings, we believe the pre-training introduced in this work demonstrates promising embeddings that align with biological features. With the growing model capacity and pre-training data diversity in future development, we envision the zero-shot embeddings from single-cell foundation models can serve as a fast and context-rich method for various applications such as visualizing, clustering, and reference mapping, etc.

While the demonstrated zero-shot capabilities of our model are indeed promising, we find it imperative to highlight two potential limitations: (1) The range and proportions of cell populations present in the pre-training dataset could limit the efficacy of zero-shot applications for underrepresented cell types. As observed in our collected pre-training data, cell atlases are commonly populated with specific cell types, such as neural and immune cells. Rarely occurring cell types or those from unique conditions are correspondingly underrepresented, which could curtail performance in subsequent, similar analytical contexts. We anticipate that future pre-training with more diverse and balanced cell types may ameliorate this limitation. (2) The pre-training protocol in its current form does not explicitly account for the removal of batch effects. Consequently, zero-shot embeddings generated directly from the pre-trained model may retain 'consistent' variations introduced by differing sequencing protocols or devices. The following paragraph delves into this subject in greater detail.

Pre-training design and its implications for batch correction The current pretraining is a purely data-driven and self-supervised procedure that optimizes the model to reconstruct gene

expressions in sequenced samples. This procedure offers the benefits of avoiding potential human bias in the meta-labels and treating all raw signals in the data 'equally'.

However, it inherently omits specific requirements that may be critical for downstream analyses. We draw particular attention to the fact that our pre-training strategy does not inherently include the mitigation of the technical variations introduced by sequencing protocols and devices.

In single-cell data analysis, technical batch effects indicate data distribution shifts that are driven by the sequencing procedure, rather than biological variations [Luecken et al., 2022]. The mitigation of such effects is instrumental for more accurate visualization and clustering of cell populations across multiple samples. Numerous elements contribute to the introduction of batch effects in the collation of cell atlases, including but not limited to, capturing times, personnel involved, reagent batches, types of equipment, sequencing protocols, and technological platforms [Tran et al., 2020]. Considering the pretraining process reconstructs binned gene expressions from other observed genes, we can classify these factors into two broad types based on their influence on pretraining: (1) The type I factors are the ones that may largely have global effects on the capturing of all gene expression, such as capturing times or sequencing depth. The pre-training may mitigate these factors through repeated training on large data examples. (2) The type II factors have gene-specific influences. As reported by existing literature [Tran et al., 2020], some of the technical batch effects (i.e. sequencing protocols and machines) can heavily shape the gene expression distributions. Rather than being ameliorated, these effects could be faithfully encapsulated during the pre-training process. From the standpoint of our pretraining objectives, these factors are not considered noise but important signals to the gene expression reconstruction task.

We would argue this limitation on the mitigating of type II technical effects is a necessary tradeoff for maintaining an 'unbiased' pre-training. While it may be plausible to incorporate a batch effect mitigation objective within the pre-training scheme, the added objective could compromise the original pre-training goals aimed at reconstructing biological gene expression because of the complex, often non-linear relationships between technical and biological factors. Consequently, we advocate maintaining an 'unbiased' data-driven approach during pre-training, thereby offering the flexibility to prioritize specific analytical objectives in subsequent fine-tuning stages.

Challenge of evaluation The evaluation for single-cell foundation models can be extremely challenging, primarily due to the absence of definitive 'ground truth' for a range of application tasks. Unlike in Natural Language Processing, where human judgment often serves as a reliable standard, biological data lack an unequivocal benchmark for performance evaluation. Furthermore, obtaining or validating expert annotations is both time-consuming and resource-intensive.

One specific challenge we encountered was evaluating the model performance on tasks that require human annotations as labels, such as data integration and cell type identifica- tion. The original annotations for cell types were usually derived from clustering analyses and linear separations based on marker gene expressions. Therefore, when evaluating the performance of models by matching with human annotations, linear and simple models may be favored in the assessment. This phenomenon has also been observed in the benchmarking study for cell type identification [Abdelaal et al., 2019], where conventional methods demonstrated superior performance than existing specialized approaches for single-cell analysis.

Expression prediction tasks such as perturbation response prediction offer potentially more authentic evaluations without the need for human annotations. However, the issue of data quality becomes prominent. In our analysis of three Perturb-seq datasets used for perturbation response prediction, we found the ratios of uncaptured gene expression to be notably high: 84% for the Adamson dataset, 92% for the Norman dataset, and 66% for the Replogle dataset. In the case of the Norman dataset, the extreme data sparsity not only reduced the preservation of genuine biological signals but also complicated both the training and evaluation processes. Conversely, the more recent Replogle dataset, with a higher capturing rate, provided a more rigorous test of methodological differences and revealed the intrinsic challenges of the prediction task, as indicated in Table 4.4. Moving forward, we anticipate that the continual improvement in the quality and scale of available datasets will enhance the utility of expression prediction tasks for evaluating single-cell foundation models.

### 4.6 Summary

We provide common pipelines with task-specific objectives for model fine-tuning, designed to facilitate the application of the pre-trained model across a range of downstream tasks. In the fine-tuning stage, the pre-trained model can be adapted to new datasets and specific tasks. We offer flexible fine-tuning pipelines suitable for a variety of essential downstream tasks in single-cell research, including scRNA-seq integration with batch correction, cell type annotation, multi-omic integration, perturbation prediction, and gene regulatory network inference. These pipelines enable researchers to leverage the power of scGPT for diverse applications in single-cell analysis. Through the comparison of gene embeddings and attention weights between the fine-tuned and raw pre-trained models, scGPT uncovers valuable biological insights into gene-gene interactions specific to various conditions, such as cell types and perturbation states. Third, our observations reveal a scaling law: larger pre-training data sizes yield superior pre-trained embeddings and further lead to improved performance on downstream tasks.

Single-cell sequencing provides snapshots of transcriptomic and multi-modal cell states. However, biological systems undergo complex dynamic transitional processes including differentiation, development, and disease progression. Methods like trajectory inference and RNA velocity computationally order cells along developmental trajectories to reconstruct lineage relationships and model differentiation as a continuous process.

Accurately inferring nonlinear dynamics from sparse snapshots remains challenging. Predefining trajectory topologies can bias analysis. Simple linear orderings fail to capture complex branching and cycling. More flexible approaches are needed to reconstruct globally optimal trajectories without topological constraints.

In this chapter, we propose a new learning framework called adaptive neighborhood learning (ANL) to relax limitations of previous dynamic modeling techniques. As a general framework, we apply it to train deep neural nets from scratch, and ANL improves performance for RNA velocity and trajectory inference. This chapter presents advances in modeling the dynamic processes underlying differentiation and development from single-cell snapshots. The proposed ANL framework and its potential integration with transfer learning provide more flexible and scalable solutions to reconstruct complex cellular dynamics.

### 5.1 Motivation

The concept of RNA velocity refers to the time derivative of the mRNA abundance in a cell, which reflects the changing rate of RNA processing and degradation. Current velocity estimation methods leverage the observation that the abundance and ratio between unspliced pre-messenger RNAs and spliced mature messenger RNAs can be used to infer changes in gene expression dynamics. Higher abundance and ratio of unspliced mRNAs to spliced mRNAs indicates increasing transcription of a certain gene in other words, up-regulation/induction and a high velocity estimate. Conversely, a lower abundance and indicated ratio lead to a low velocity estimate associated with down-regulation/repression. An equilibrium phase occurs when this dynamic process reaches a stable steady-state. Since unspliced mRNAs can be distinguished in common single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) protocols [La Manno et al., 2018], the idea of estimating dynamic RNA velocity using only static sequencing libraries becomes feasible.

The original RNA velocity approach [La Manno et al., 2018] utilized the assumption that the observed transcriptional phases in scRNA-seq last long enough to reach both an apex of induction and a quiescent steady-state equilibrium. This technique infers a pergene steady-state ratio using linear regression, and then RNA velocities are calculated as the deviation of the observed ratio from the steady-state level. This workflow implies two underlying assumptions, (1) the assumption of steady-state : For every gene, sufficient number of sequenced cells are at the steady states; (2) the assumption of cell-agnostic kinetic rates : The degradation and splicing rate for each gene is shared across all cells. These assumptions are often violated in complex biological systems and bring about limitations in downstream applications, particularly when cell states are partially observed or undergo transcription dynamics more complex than the steady-state pattern. Although a later approach, scVelo [Bergen et al., 2020], attempted to generalize the steady-state assumption by replacing these states with four transcriptional states and modeling them with a dynamical model, the aforementioned second limitation still remains.

Further, scVelo assumes a cyclic trajectory within the four transcriptional states for all observed genes, but this assumption also rarely holds in real-world single-cell datasets with complex differentiation trajectories and multifactorial kinetics [Gorin et al., 2022]. Although several related works have been further developed, including MultiVelo [Li et al., 2021], Chromatin Velocity [Tedesco et al., 2022], protaccel [Gorin et al., 2020] for extending Velocity beyond RNA, VeloAE [Qiao and Huang, 2021] for denoising velocity with Deep Neural Nets, Dynamo [Qiu et al., 2022] for exploiting the metabolic labeling sequencing data, the core velocity computation follows the original ideas and therefore the aforementioned limitations still hold.

Overall, existing techniques assume each gene follows a pre-defined trajectory depicted by constant cell-agnostic kinetic rates. This workflow implies that each gene goes through the same velocity trajectory across all cell-types, and limits the application in complex cell systems. To resolve these limitations, we highlight the need for cell-specific kinetics which enables the modeling of multi-lineage systems with heterogeneous cell populations. In this chapter, we propose a self-supervised learning framework ANL to modeling the cell state. We first apply ANL directly to train DeepVelo, a deep neural network based method for RNA velocity estimation. (1) DeepVelo is optimized using a newly introduced continuity framework, resulting in an approach that is unbiased from pre-defined kinetic patterns. (2) Empowered by Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN), DeepVelo infers gene-specific and cell-specific RNA splicing and degradation rates.

### 5.5 Summary

This chapter offers a novel velocity estimation framework that goes beyond assumptions of constant RNA splicing and degradation rates, and instead estimates these rates at a cell-specific level. By analyzing the performance of DeepVelo and existing velocity estimation techniques, we have demonstrated that DeepVelo's cell-specific estimation through a novel deep learning method allows for the detection and specification of multiple lineages in calculating RNA velocity. Realistic single-cell RNA sequencing settings will likely have more than one lineage/trajectory in a given sample, and thus it is imperative to develop methods that can account for these multifaceted dynamical systems. DeepVelo's ability to model these multifaceted dynamics was demonstrated through analysis of complex differentiation systems, such as the development of the dentate gyrus, pancreatic endocrinogenesis, and chondrocyte development. Lastly, we demonstrated that DeepVelo can be utilized to identify functionally relevant genes that are enriched along multi-furcating differentiation trajectories, in systems including hindbrain development and pilocytic astrocytoma. We envision that DeepVelo will be more readily applicable to these realistic developmental settings as compared to previous techniques.

RNA velocity techniques have allowed for insights into biological differentiation from single-cell RNA sequencing data that go beyond oversimplified trajectory inference models and instead infer dynamic processes that indicate the direction and magnitude of differentiation potential. Although many major limitations and assumptions for RNA velocity methods still exist, we anticipate that continued methodological development in this field will lead to better tools to study differentiation and development in a single-cell setting. DeepVelo overcomes limitations of previous techniques in a major aspect with regards to cell-specific model estimates and the continuity assumption. Our experiments on both normal developmental and tumor data demonstrate DeepVelo's ability for more robust velocity estimation in multi-lineage systems, yielding better biological insights into real and complex biological use cases.

DeepVelo internally predicts the first-order derivative of per gene expression based on the transcriptome wide reads of all selected genes. The ability to learn the interaction/regulation between genes could be further explored, for example, by replacing the GCN model with recent transformer networks [Vaswani et al., 2017] which could explicitly model the interaction of internal gene representations. This could allow for more interpretable velocity and driver-gene estimates, by considering correlations of kinetics and expression patterns between genes and cells. Recent work shows promising research directions by extending the velocity of cellular dynamics from RNA to proteins [Gorin et al., 2020], epigenomics [Tedesco et al., 2022], and multi-omics velocities [Li et al., 2021]. DeepVelo could be naturally updated and well fitted into these settings by enriching input and output space with additional -omics information. Ultimately, the estimation of cell-specific kinetics across multiple steps in the central dogma may increase the signal-to-noise ratio [Bergen et al., 2021] and further accurately capture information related to cellular development.

The continuity assumption is central to the DeepVelo model, and although this assumption allows DeepVelo to be more expressive and less constrained, it does have its limitations. One potential limitation is the applicability of DeepVelo to cell-types that may be less prevalent or difficult to characterize, such as rare cell-types. Finding the correct future time point neighbors for these cell-types may be challenging, thus hampering the applicability of the continuity assumption. However, we do show that the DeepVelo model is robust to the choice of neighbors in the future time point state calculation. Further, DeepVelo works in a manner that is not biased by cell-type annotation, and as such, results will not be affected by incorrect annotation of rarer cell-types. Regardless, the challenge of modelling RNA velocity for rare cell-types is an important future research direction for RNA velocity methods, irrespective of their central assumptions, and future studies should emphasize the inclusion of data with less studied/rare cell-types.

Retaining the foundation model's architecture, our continual pretraining maintains an embedding size of 512, constituted by 12 stacked transformer blocks, each equipped with 8 attention heads. ### 6.3 Transfer learning for spatial region clustering

Identifying spatial domains, regions characterized by coherent gene expression and histology, is a critical step in spatial omics. Traditional clustering methods, such as K-means and Louvain Traag et al. [2019], primarily utilize gene expression data for clustering purposes. However, these methods often produce clusters that lack spatial contiguity, as they do not incorporate spatial information and histology [Zhao et al., 2021]. To address the spatial dependency inherent in gene expression, novel methodologies have been developed.

For example, Zhu et al. [2018] proposed a Hidden-Markov random field (HMRF) approach to model the spatial dependency of gene expression. stLearn [Pham et al., 2020] incorporates features extracted from histology images and the expression of neighboring spots to spatially normalize gene expression data before clustering. BayesSpace [Zhao et al., 2021] employs a Bayesian clustering method, using a prior that assigns greater weight to physically adjacent spots. As the field of spatial omics advances with the development of new technologies, there is an increasing need for methods that can be easily adapted to new data and scenarios. This adaptability is crucial for maximizing the utility of spatial omics in diverse research settings, enabling more comprehensive and nuanced insights into the spatial dynamics of gene expression within tissues.

The fine-tuning workflow for spatial region clustering, tailored for spatial transcriptomics data. The fine-tuning process integrates the inherited learning objectives from scRNA-seq data integration with spatial omics-specific methodologies. Additionally, the spatial contrastive learning mechanism, similar in spirit to the ECS objective but adapted for spatial data, plays a pivotal role. This approach leverages both gene expression profiles and spatial locations of the cells, enhancing the model's ability to distinguish between different spatial regions based on their molecular signatures and physical locations.

The workflow enables the scGPT model to effectively learn robust and meaningful representations of spatial transcriptomics data. By incorporating both gene expression and spatial context, the model is equipped to perform accurate and insightful clustering of spatial regions, unveiling the complex biological processes and cellular interactions within diverse tissue environments.

### 6.4 Transfer learning for spatial deconvolution

The burgeoning field of spatial genomics has ushered in a transformative era in understanding the molecular and cellular architecture of tissues within their native spatial context. This advancement has been catalyzed by breakthroughs in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics, which collectively offer a comprehensive view of gene expression landscapes across different tissue regions. Within this context, spatial deconvolution becomes a critical task, involving the deciphering of complex cellular compositions and gene expression patterns within tissue sections where individual cells may not be distinctly resolved. This task is particularly challenging due to inherent limitations in current sequencing-based spatial transcriptomics technologies, such as 10x Visium, where the resolution between spots is at 55 µ m, larger than the size of individual cells.

Transfer learning emerges as a powerful strategy to address this challenge. By utilizing pre-existing models trained on extensive scRNA-seq datasets, transfer learning enables the adaptation of these models to spatial transcriptomics data. This approach holds the potential to bridge the gap between the high-resolution cellular insights offered by scRNA-seq and the spatial context provided by transcriptomics technologies. The objective is to achieve a more nuanced and detailed understanding of tissue architecture and function, accommodating the complex interplay of various cell types and their gene expression within the spatial framework of tissues.

### 7.1 Future directions

#### 7.1.1 Opportunities of single-cell foundation models

Applications of foundation models to biology are on the verge of becoming instrumental in revealing complex cellular landscapes. This could elucidate the roles of specific genes, cell types, and novel conditional gene interactions in dynamic contexts. Single-cell foundation models stand to provide unique biological insights, the discovery of novel cell types, and applications in drug development and disease research. This section highlights potential important applications.

Characterizing tissue heterogeneity and discovery of novel cell types in normal and disease conditions Recent advances in single-cell omics have enabled highresolution dissection of cell subpopulations beyond classical surface markers, and researchers are actively developing techniques to unravel heterogeneity within complex tissues like tumors. For instance, single-cell RNA-seq revealed transcriptional heterogeneity within glioblastomas associated with variable treatment response [Patel et al., 2014]. The epigenomic analysis further distinguished tumor subclones based on chromatin state indicative of different cells-of-origin [Eyler et al., 2020]. Integrating diverse measurements from the same cells can enable a more nuanced characterization of transitional states and lineages [Zhu et al., 2020, Hao et al., 2021]. Limited multi-omics data integration has already provided key insights [Angermueller et al., 2017], while realizing the full potential requires scaling up coordinated multi-omics.

Single-cell foundation models offer unique opportunities to define the continuous nature of cell states given their ability to incorporate diverse, large-scale omics datasets, in contrast to existing methods that have mainly focused on discrete definitions. The power of this modeling would be to infer a cell's past, future, and response to internal or external stimuli. By learning coordinated embeddings, models can potentially cluster cells based on complex multi-omic states beyond what manual annotation permits. Capturing epigenetic signals alongside expression may elucidate developmental trajectories [Chen et al., 2016, Li et al., 2023]. Overall, multi-omics integration gives models a more functional, high-dimensional profile of each cell for parsing subtler differences. This promises to uncover previously obscured heterogeneity and new biological insights.

We expect multi-modal foundation models to truly excel in their abilities to contextualize, compare, and complete cellular states (Figure 2.1C). (1) Contextualize cellular states:

foundation models are adept at embedding cells within an expansive continuum by assimilating diverse omics corpora during training. Reference mapping has been pioneered in studies of Seurat V4 [Hao et al., 2021] and scArches [Lotfollahi et al., 2022], where cell types and other meta information can be propagated from a rich context of cell atlases to new cells of interest. Now, in addition to discrete cell types, foundation models enable continuous cell state description, which may recapitulate the position of a cell in the developmental tree or in disease progression. (2) Compare cellular states: foundation models facilitate swift and robust integration across heterogenous single-cell measurements and across omics modalities assayed separately in the same cells. This could allow joint analysis of heterogeneous datasets and comparison of cellular states across healthy and disease conditions. (3) Complete cellular states: Given incomplete observations, foundation models can generate missing modalities to reconstruct full cell profiles in silico. This imputation helps overcome challenges like low capture efficiency. Single-cell foundation models trained on massive atlases easily match cues from each omics to known cell types. For example, metabolic labeling of RNA and protein can be used to measure dynamics in experimental models [Battich et al., 2020, Cao et al., 2020, Qiu et al., 2020b, 2022], but is not applicable in clinical samples. Now, by training on these experimental data and learning the cellular state dynamics, foundation models may help us fill in missing modalities through their generative properties and predict the cell fates in clinical samples without metabolic labeling. This inherent capability helps resolve traditional integration difficulties and utilize prior knowledge to tackle the multi-omics challenges of today.

Predicting gene functions and discovery of biomarkers In biomarker discovery, learning unified patterns in massive heterogeneous disease datasets may reveal predictive multi-omics signatures involving specific cell subsets, gene modules, proteomic markers, or metabolic profiles, contributing to precision diagnostics and medicine1. By learning from diverse omics data, single-cell foundation models can potentially elucidate gene functions and regulatory interactions while also revealing predictive biomarkers for complex diseases.

Recent works have demonstrated success in predicting gene functions from genome sequences alone [Ji et al., 2021b, Nguyen et al., 2023], and using models learning from cell atlases of scRNA-seq data, e.g., GeneFormer [Theodoris et al., 2023]. Furthermore, adding multi-omics contexts such as chromatin accessibility and methylation could improve inferences [Hao et al., 2021]. By integrating perturbations and time series data, models can distinguish causal regulators from reactive responses. Attention mechanism [Vaswani et al., 2017] has been used in computer vision and human language to interpret visual and text signals [Fan et al., 2021, Rogers et al., 2021], and it can be used to reveal influential genes indicative of lineage transitions [Vig et al., 2020]. Another opportunity is to reveal molecular biomarkers for specific lineages or diseases. Biomarker discovery currently relies on the differential analysis of case-control data. However, by assimilating massive heterogeneous disease datasets, foundation models can discern more universal patterns transcending specific cohorts. Capturing multiple omics also provides opportunities to construct multi-modal signatures.

In addition to predicting gene functions, multi-modal foundation models hold promise for reconstructing context-specific gene regulatory networks (GRNs). This promise is mainly driven by two observations: (1) The gene regulatory mechanism is inherently a process across multi-omics. Historically, GRNs have been predominantly compiled from experimentally validated regulatory events, as cataloged in various databases [Han et al., 2018, Liu et al., 2015], or inferred from gene co-expression analyses in bulk transcriptomics data [Margolin et al., 2006, Langfelder and Horvath, 2008]. However, capturing complete regulatory mechanisms requires not only transcriptomics data, but also other events along the central dogma, such as the DNA binding events, alternative splicing of RNAs, and posttranslational protein modifications. Therefore, by assimilating diverse omics data, multimodal models can potentially offer an integral and more accurate view [Badia-i Mompel et al., 2023]. (2) The gene regulatory mechanism is inherently context-specific. It is known that the transcription factor (TF) binding is a highly dynamic process specific to tissues and conditions [Badia-i Mompel et al., 2023, Kim and Wysocka, 2023]. Single-cell foundation models can address this challenge by uncovering conditional gene networks specific to cell types, developmental stages, and disease states. The standard workflow of pretraining and transfer learning in foundation models naturally fits this relation between generalistic and context-specific GRNs. Essentially, the foundation model can be expected to learn a default regulatory network during the large-scale pretraining with diverse contexts of multi-omics data, and the model can be flexibly adapted via transfer learning to elucidate specific GRNs by interpreting learned embeddings under different contexts. Thus, foundation models may fill key gaps in deciphering conditional GRNs for understanding the dynamical biological systems.

Additionally, an interesting direction for GRN reconstruction with foundation models would be harnessing the prior knowledge of molecular regulations from existing literature and databases, such as motif binding databases of CIS-BP [Weirauch et al., 2014], ENCODE [Kheradpour and Kellis, 2014], and TRANSFAC [Matys et al., 2006]. A relevant example is provided in the graph-based method, GEARS [Roohani et al., 2022], where increased accuracy of perturbation response prediction is observed after injecting prior knowledge of gene-gene relations from the GO database [Consortium, 2004]. Notably, these databases usually contain pair-wise connections between transcription factors (TFs) and cis-regulatory elements. In a transformer-based model, these pairs of connections can be readily injected into the self-attention operations as attention weight priors. This would provide an inspiring combination of 'human expertise' and data-driven knowledge in the model.

To summarize, foundation models offer unique opportunities to elucidate gene functions, interactions, and biomarkers which is key to demystifying the pathophysiology of complex diseases. Single-cell foundation models could reveal predictive biomarkers and insights into complex disease mechanisms. Single-cell foundation models develop rich contextual embeddings for genes and other biological components based on their coordinated activities across cells and conditions. Attention mechanisms can then highlight influential interactions, providing directionality to illuminate gene relationships.

Understanding development and temporal predictions Integrating longitudinal multi-omics data enables foundation models to elucidate complex cell differentiation processes by tracing developmental trajectories. Several methods have been proposed for reconstructing single-cell trajectories from transcriptomic snapshots, including RNA velocity for projecting future state transitions [La Manno et al., 2018, Bergen et al., 2020]. By assimilating diverse measurements, foundation models can potentially overcome key limitations.

For example, foundation models can potentially chart trajectories of stem cells differentiating into specialized lineages based on the observation of coordinated gene expression and epigenomic changes. Attention can highlight influential factors triggering state transitions. Models can also detect elusive intermediate states not apparent from start and endpoint snapshots. Incorporating multiple omics is expected to improve robustness against data sparsity and technical noise [Badia-i Mompel et al., 2023]. The pretraining data of foundation models can include cells at multiple time points and training on them contributes to the representation of development time in foundation models. The learned rich coordinated embeddings can situate cells along a continuum to enable the reconstruction of smooth trajectories.

Imputing missing modalities also aids in connecting fragmented observations into lineages. Once trained on temporal datasets, foundation models may also foresee future cell state transitions. Projecting cell embeddings can predict expression, morphology, and phenotype changes. Overall, temporal multi-omics data provides a valuable opportunity for foundation models to uncover new insights into the complex, stochastic processes guiding cell fate decisions during development and disease. Capturing lineage trajectories at high resolution promises to elucidate long-standing questions in stem cell biology and beyond.

In silico perturbation Single-cell foundation models trained on diverse omics data may predict the effects of hypothetical genetic or chemical perturbations on cell states. Recently, models like scGPT, CellOracle [Kamimoto et al., 2023], Geneformer [Theodoris et al., 2023], CellOT [Bunne et al., 2023], CPA [Lotfollahi et al., 2023b], chemcCPA [Hetzel et al., 2022], and GEARS [Roohani et al., 2022] have shown initial success in perturbing learned cell embeddings to influence predicted expression profiles. Future developments can expand the applications beyond transcriptomics. By assimilating multi-omics measurements, foundation models could enable more realistic in silico modeling.

The in silico perturbation can be built on the abilities described in Section 4.3: foundation models can first construct complete cell representations by integrating gene expression, epigenetics, protein levels, and morphology data. Conditioning these embeddings on different cell types and perturbing states would allow nuanced perturbation analysis. Models can then leverage learned pathway knowledge and gene regulatory networks to predict coordinated downstream effects of perturbations beyond just transcription. Incorporating spatial and temporal datasets provides additional opportunities to trace impacts across tissues and time points. Perturbing cell lineage trajectories could reveal branching effects on differentiation and development. Causal inference techniques may distinguish direct versus indirect consequences.

Overall, foundation models have the potential to advance in silico perturbation analysis by leveraging multi-omics knowledge to model emergent effects at multiple regulatory layers. However, caution is needed to avoid unrealistic extrapolation. Uncertainty metrics are needed to be deployed and the predictions can potentially be validated by wet lab experiments. With rigorous validation, predictive in silico modeling promises to accelerate discovery by guiding targeted experiments.

To fulfill the potential applications described previously, multi-modal foundation models for molecular cell biology should possess certain key technical properties. We outline design and technical considerations to develop these capable foundation models.

Data for training multi-modal foundation models Pretraining versatile multi-modal foundation models require large and diverse multi-omics datasets spanning bulk sequencing, single-cell assays, spatial transcriptomics, chromatin accessibility, proteomics, and small molecules across cell types, conditions, tissues, and species. However, current resources have limited paired measurements profiling the same cells or samples across modalities [Butler et al., 2018]. Paired data are generated by more recent sequencing protocols (such as 10X Multiome, scCITE-seq, and scASAP-seq) to capture different modalities simultaneously. For illuminating processes spanning the central dogma, such paired data would perform an essential role as anchor points when integrating other samples. Cross-species datasets may also provide helpful evolutionary context [Schaum et al., 2018, Yao et al., 2021].

Although only scRNA-seq and snRNA-seq data were used in the pretraining of scGPT, multi-omics data can particularly play a central role in training future foundation models. Here, we highlight the need for data generation and curation for future foundation model training, and use the observation on single-cell data as an example. First, a positive trend of creating and sharing data has been observed recently. For instance, the number of cells has doubled in the last year, from around 30 million to 61 million, in the CellxGENE [Biology et al., 2023] service (an online collection containing data from HuBMAP, HCA, etc.). However, the core challenge arises with data modalities beyond RNA sequencing. For instance, CELLxGENE currently hosts only about 200 thousand cells of human scATAC-seq data (and 880 thousand cells of mouse scATAC-seq data). Moving forward, while scRNAseq data may form the majority of the training data and provide backbone foundational knowledge, acquiring sufficient data that can encompass the tissue heterogeneity in each other modalities is equally critical. The generation of more comprehensive multi-modal data will be instrumental in enriching the training datasets for foundation models.

Aggregating and curating data from a large number of studies is an equally important step. The efforts include strait-forward quests such as harmonizing meta labels across studies, and also non-trivial challenges like unified quality control and normalization. Ideally, aggregated pretraining data should fully represent the heterogeneity of cell types and states. However, with current constraints, batch effects, and imbalanced representations must be addressed through careful normalization and data harmonization [Stuart et al., 2019, Tian et al., 2019]. Future coordinated efforts to generate very large paired multi-omics datasets representative of diverse contexts will be invaluable. Effective use of generative data augmentation may partially mitigate data limitations. In the interim, models can be pretrained on synthetic data, then fine-tuned on available real paired omics measurements. While acquiring sufficiently massive and diverse multi-omics data remains challenging, these strategies can help overcome current limitations.

Raw heterogeneous data need to be processed into unified representations amenable as model inputs. Taking into consideration that molecular data are provided at vastly different resolutions, from single nucleotides (e.g. the raw reads in next-generation sequencing) to whole proteins (e.g the fluorescence read in imaging-based omics), we envision tokenization techniques that can happen at multiple levels. For example, there should be low-level tokens (i.e., learnable numerical vectors) as summarization of nucleotide k-mers, medium-level tokens that cover longer motifs, and high-level tokens at the resolution of whole genes. Techniques like subword tokenization can encode raw nucleotides or amino acids into discrete vocabularies for modeling (This strategy has been used in the recent version of DNABert-2 [Zhou et al., 2023]), while higher-level tokens can represent genes or proteins as in scGPT and Geneformer [Theodoris et al., 2023].

Hybrid multi-level attention. As mentioned above, molecular data naturally exhibit structure at multiple scales, from individual base pairs to genes, pathways, and cellular networks. In line with the aforementioned idea of multi-level tokenization, we envision that hybrid transformer architectures with separate local and global self-attentions may effectively model interactions within and across these scales. Here, local attention stands for the operations of self-attention between tokens of the same level, such as the interactions that connect genes to genes, or nucleotides to nucleotides. Global attention refers to the inter-level operations that connect multi-level tokens, ideally generating an integral view of input data. While multi-level attention has been applied in milestone studies in computer vision, such as SwinTransformer [Liu et al., 2021b] and MultiScale ViT [Fan et al., 2021], similar ideas in biological foundation models are yet to be explored. Local attention mechanisms would understand relations within a specific modality (protein-protein, gene-gene interactions, etc.), while the global attention mechanisms would operate on a bigger scale, drawing connections between data modalities (gene-protein interactions, etc.). For example, specialized neural network encoders tailored for each data type can distill key signals [Stuart et al., 2019]. Cross-attention layers can subsequently integrate these encodings into joint representations capturing associations.

Integration of human knowledge. Integrating external knowledge like pathways, gene ontologies, protein interaction networks, and literature into pretraining can provide useful inductive biases for the otherwise purely data-driven models [AlQuraishi and Sorger, 2021]. Useful techniques include knowledge graph incorporation, textual self-supervision, and asking experts to validate model predictions.

We highlight two possible technique directions, in particular for structured and unstructured knowledge, respectively. (1) Current biomedical databases represent structured interactions of molecules (e.g. among genes, proteins, drug compounds, etc.) in the form of knowledge graphs (KGs), and injecting these interaction priors into the attention mechanism in transformers can be a natural bridge connecting data-driven and human knowledge. For example, given two genes that are annotated with related functions in databases (e.g. GO [Consortium, 2019] and Reactome [Fabregat et al., 2018]), their gene embeddings in the foundation model can be initialized with high similarity or can be optimized to increase pairwise similarity during training. Moreover, recent attempts, such as BioCypher [Lobentanzer et al., 2023], have proposed ways to integrate heterogeneous KGs across modalities. Having the multi-scale relations across genes, proteins and drugs can be used as priors for the aforementioned Hybrid multi-level attention specifically for building foundation mod- els. (2) The raw text in biomedical literature contains vast unstructured knowledge. They can be represented as vector embeddings in neural networks with the help of current large language models, such as recent BioGPT [Luo et al., 2022] and Med-PaLM [Singhal et al., 2022]. These knowledge embeddings can be appended to the input of foundation models, enabling the joint training of foundation models with both experimental data and literature knowledge representations.
