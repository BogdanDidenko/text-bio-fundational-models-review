## A fine-tuned genomic language model adds complementary nucleotide-context information to missense variant interpretation

Yaqi Su 1* † and Yu-Jen Lin 1* †

1 Department of Molecular and Cell Biology, University of California, Berkeley, Berkeley, California, 94720, United States.

*Corresponding author(s). E-mail(s): yaqisu@berkeley.edu; jenniferyjlin@berkeley.edu;

† These authors contributed equally to this work.

##### Abstract

Missense variant interpretation remains a central challenge in clinical genomics. Missense pathogenicity predictors achieve strong performance, but many emphasize protein-level consequences or overlapping annotation priors. Whether genomic language models add non-redundant nucleotide-context signal to missense interpretation remains unclear. Here, we systematically adapted genomic language models to ClinVar missense pathogenicity prediction across backbone architectures, representation strategies, classifier heads, and adaptation regimes. In our analysis, variant-position embeddings consistently outperformed pooled sequence representations, multi-species pretraining provided the strongest backbone-level advantage, and low-rank adaptation generalized better than full fine-tuning. The resulting fine-tuned model, GLM-Missense, substantially outperformed zero-shot scoring from the same pretrained model. To test whether GLM-Missense contributes information beyond existing methods, we built MetaMissense, an XGBoost ensemble combining GLM-Missense with AlphaMissense, ESM1b, REVEL, CADD, SIFT, and PolyPhen-2. GLMMissense showed the lowest concordance with other predictors, retained the strongest partial association with pathogenicity after controlling for the other predictors, and ranked as the most informative non-ensemble input to MetaMissense. MetaMissense achieved the best performance in both cross-validation and held-out testing. Analyses of variants correctly classified by GLM-Missense but misclassified by several established predictors suggested two patterns. First, part of the GLM-Missense signal may reflect splice-relevant exonic context. Second, GLM-Missense appears to add value in settings where other predictors may overweight allele frequency, gene-level constraint, or amino-acid-change severity. However, these features explained only about 10% of the distinction between the GLM-Missense-correct subset from the background. Together, our results demonstrate that fine-tuned genomic language models contribute complementary nucleotide-context information to missense variant interpretation.

## 1 Introduction 1

Missense variants are among the most common classes of coding variation identified 2 in clinical sequencing, yet their interpretation remains a major challenge in human 3 genetics Majewski et al. (2011); Tennessen et al. (2012); McLaughlin et al. (2014). 4 Clinical classification of missense variants is central to molecular diagnosis and variant 5 prioritization Richards et al. (2015). ClinVar aggregates clinical assertions of variant 6 significance together with supporting evidence and has become a key resource for 7 variant interpretation Landrum et al. (2018, 2020). However, accurate classification 8 of previously uncharacterized missense variants remains an unsolved challenge Starita 9 et al. (2017). 10

Most existing missense pathogenicity predictors were developed to model protein11 level consequences, typically through amino-acid sequence conservation, structural 12 context, and predicted effects on protein function Ng and Henikoff (2006) This empha13 sis does not arise because missense variants originate in protein space, but because 14 the encoded amino-acid change has historically been the most interpretable route 15 from coding DNA variation to altered function. In protein space, pathogenic missense 16 variants can disrupt stability, catalytic activity, binding interfaces, or other biochem17 ical functions, so these are natural targets for computational prediction (Stefl et al. 18 2013). Current methods span several methodological classes. Supervised annotation19 based models such as REVEL and CADD integrate conservation with diverse genomic 20 annotations Ioannidis et al. (2016); Kircher et al. (2014); Rentzsch et al. (2019). 21 Classical sequence- and structure-informed predictors such as SIFT and PolyPhen-2 22 estimate the functional impact of amino-acid substitutions using evolutionary con23 servation, physicochemical properties, and protein sequence or structural context Ng 24 and Henikoff (2003); Adzhubei et al. (2010). More recently, protein language models 25 and structure-informed methods, including ESM1b and AlphaMissense, have further 26 improved performance by leveraging large-scale proteomic and structural priors Bran27 des et al. (2023); Cheng et al. (2023). Large protein resources such as UniProt have 28 enabled self-supervised pretraining, and protein language models have shown strong 29 transfer across structural and functional tasks, including zero-shot mutation-effect pre30 diction Bateman et al. (2023); Lin et al. (2023); Meier et al. (2021). Together, these 31 approaches have advanced missense prediction Rastogi et al. (2025). Nevertheless, 32 independent benchmarking still reveals substantial residual error Rastogi et al. (2025). 33 It therefore remains unclear whether the remaining error mainly reflects irreducible 34 uncertainty or systematically missing information. 35

A missense annotation does not guarantee a protein-only mechanism. Exonic sub36 stitutions can alter splicing by perturbing splice-adjacent sequence, exonic splicing 37

enhancers or silencers, or cryptic splice sites Fairbrother et al. (2002); Jaganathan 38 et al. (2019). Previous studies estimated that approximately 10% of disease-causing 39 exonic mutations alter splicing, and missense exonic disease mutations with the least 40 predicted protein-level impact are especially enriched for splice disruption Soemedi 41 et al. (2017); Xiong et al. (2015). Coding DNA also carries information beyond the 42 encoded amino-acid sequence. Codon usage can influence mRNA stability and trans43 lation Presnyak et al. (2015); Wu et al. (2019). Local nucleotide changes can perturb 44 RNA structure Sabarinathan et al. (2013). Coding exons can contain overlapping reg45 ulatory code, including dual-use codons that also encode transcription-factor-binding 46 information Stergachis et al. (2013). The label 'missense' can therefore obscure a 47 broader set of mechanisms than protein-level consequence alone. We therefore hypoth48 esized that part of the residual error in current missense prediction arises because 49 many existing methods focus primarily on protein-level effects and do not fully capture 50 nucleotide-level context. 51

Genomic language models provide a practical framework that could potentially address this gap by learning DNA-sequence representations. DNABERT first showed that self-supervised learning on genomic sequence can produce transferable representations for downstream genomic tasks Ji et al. (2021). Subsequent models expanded both tokenization strategies and model scale, such as the Nucleotide Transformer family Dalla-Torre et al. (2025). The models examined in this study differ in both pretraining corpus and architecture. Nucleotide Transformer 1 (NT-1) refers to the original 500M Nucleotide Transformer pretrained with masked language modeling on over 3000 human genomes. Nucleotide Transformer 2 (NT-2) refers to the multispecies Nucleotide Transformer v2 pretrained on genomes across over 800 different species Dalla-Torre et al. (2025). Caduceus further broadened the architectural space by introducing a state-space genomic language model designed for long-range DNA context modeling Schiff et al. (2024). Caduceus-PS, the parameter-sharing (PS) implementation evaluated here, incorporates inductive biases such as reverse-complement symmetry and was pretrained on the human reference genome Schiff et al. (2024). Prior genomic language model studies showed that these models can perform well across multiple genomic prediction tasks and can score variants directly from sequence Benegas et al. (2025). However, to our knowledge, no previous study has directly tested whether genomic language models contribute additional information beyond existing missense predictors in missense interpretation.

Meta-predictors, or ensemble models, combine outputs or feature summaries from 72 multiple existing predictors into a single score. Their main purpose is to aggregate 73 partially complementary lines of evidence and improve overall predictive performance. 74 Existing examples, such as REVEL, show that combining multiple predictor scores 75 can improve missense prediction Ioannidis et al. (2016). Ensemble models are also 76 useful for a second reason: they provide a natural framework for testing whether a 77 new predictor adds information beyond what is already captured by the others. Nev78 ertheless, most benchmarking studies still focus on whether one method outperforms 79 another on standalone accuracy metrics. Much less attention has been paid to whether 80 a model contributes non-redundant signal that could help close the remaining gap 81

between current missense prediction and correct classification. That question is espe82 cially important when the new model is built from a different representation space 83 than existing predictors. 84 Effective fine-tuning is important to fully utilize genomic language models for mis85 sense prediction. Although zero-shot genomic scoring does not require task-specific 86 labels, recent studies suggest that zero-shot performance alone may not be suffi87 cient for accurate missense classification Benegas et al. (2025); Curtis (2025). These 88 observations motivate task-specific adaptation of genomic language models for clinical 89 missense prediction. Choosing an effective fine-tuning strategy is critical because clin90 ically labeled missense datasets are limited relative to the size of current pretrained 91 genomic models. Downstream performance may therefore depend strongly on how the 92 pretrained model backbone is adapted Veiner and Supek (2026); Feng et al. (2025). 93 It remains unclear which combination of pretrained backbone, pretraining corpus, 94 embedding extraction strategy, and fine-tuning strategy yields the most robust perfor95 mance for missense pathogenicity prediction. Parameter-efficient approaches such as 96 low-rank adaptation (LoRA), which keep the pretrained backbone fixed and learn only 97 low-rank updates, may be particularly useful when labeled missense data are limited 98 relative to model size Hu et al. (2022). Rigorous evaluation is also essential because 99 benchmarking of variant effect predictors is vulnerable to circularity and other sources 100 of optimistic bias Grimm et al. (2015); Livesey and Marsh (2020, 2023); Rastogi et al. 101 (2025). Chromosome-level holdout designs are commonly used as a more conservative 102 alternative to random splits in sequence-based genomic prediction, because they reduce 103 the risk of leakage from nearby genomic regions or linkage disequilibrium de Boer and 104 Taipale (2024); Feng et al. (2025). 105

Here, we systematically evaluated adapting genomic language models for mis106 sense pathogenicity prediction using curated ClinVar missense datasets. We compared 107 NT-1, NT-2, and Caduceus-PS as representative genomic language model backbones 108 and examined how pretraining corpus, embedding extraction strategy, classifier head, 109 and adaptation regime influence downstream performance. Building on the best110 performing configuration, we developed GLM-Missense, a genomic language model 111 fine-tuned for missense pathogenicity prediction. We then asked whether GLM112 Missense contributes information beyond existing missense predictors by constructing 113 MetaMissense, an XGBoost-based ensemble model that combines GLM-Missense with 114 AlphaMissense, ESM1b, REVEL, CADD, SIFT, and PolyPhen-2 Cheng et al. (2023); 115 Brandes et al. (2023); Ioannidis et al. (2016); Kircher et al. (2014); Ng and Henikoff 116 (2003); Adzhubei et al. (2010). In brief, variant-position embeddings outperformed 117 mean-pooled sequence representations, multi-species pretraining provided the clearest 118 backbone-level advantage, and LoRA generalized better than full fine-tuning under 119 limited supervision. GLM-Missense substantially improved over zero-shot scoring from 120 the same pretrained backbone. More importantly, GLM-Missense contributed comple121 mentary signal within the MetaMissense ensemble despite not being the top standalone 122 predictor: it showed the lowest concordance with the other predictors, retained strong 123 non-redundant association with the true label after accounting for the other predictor 124 scores, and was among the most important features used by MetaMissense. Consistent 125 with these findings, MetaMissense achieved the best overall performance compared 126

to other state-of-the-art missense predictors. Analyses of variants correctly classified 127 by GLM-Missense but misclassified by several established predictors suggested two 128 patterns. First, part of the GLM-Missense signal may reflect splice-relevant exonic con129 text. Second, GLM-Missense appears to add value in settings where other predictors 130 may overweight allele frequency, gene-level constraint, or amino-acid-change severity. 131 Moreover, these interpretable features explained only a small fraction of the overall 132 complementary signal contributed by GLM-Missense, suggesting that the model cap133 tures additional sequence-derived information beyond a small set of manually-curated 134 features, which is a key strength of genomic language models. 135

## 2 Results

### 2.1 Variant-position embeddings and multi-species pretraining yield the best frozen-backbone configuration

We first asked which frozen-backbone configuration provided the strongest baseline for clinical missense classification. In this setting, the backbone parameters were kept frozen and only a lightweight downstream classifier was trained. We compared three pretrained gLM backbones: NT-2, NT-1, and Caduceus-PS Dalla-Torre et al. (2025); Schiff et al. (2024). For each backbone, we evaluated multiple combinations of embedding extraction strategies and classifier heads to identify the optimal baseline configuration. (Fig. 1).

Embedding choice had significant effect on performance. We first compared the hidden state of the token containing the variant, which we call the variant-position embedding , against mean pooling across the full input sequence. Each representation was paired with an MLP, a CNN, or a shallow Transformer head. For CaduceusPS, we first performed downsampling of the full sequence to account for memory constraint when paired with CNN or Tranasformer head. Variant-position embeddings consistently outperformed mean pooling in the models tested (Fig. 2).

A natural follow-up is whether some intermediate representation such as averaging the embeddings only within a small window around the variant, might provide additional local context without diluting the signal across irrelevant positions. To test whether nearby tokens added useful flanking context, we replaced full-sequence mean pooling in the frozen NT-2 + CNN model with mean pooling over 8-, 16-, or 32-token windows centered on the variant (Supplementary Fig. S1). Performance decreased as the window widened and even the best local-window model remained below the variant-position baseline. Thus, preserving the representation at the variant position was better than averaging across nearby tokens.

Classifier-head choice had a smaller effect than embedding choice. In NT-2 with a variant-position embedding, the three classifier heads performed almost identically with validation AUROC ranged from 0 . 869 to 0 . 871 (Fig. 2). In NT-2 with mean pooling, the spread across heads increased to ∆AUROC = 0 . 027. This pattern suggests that when using suboptimal input representations, more sophisticated classifier architectures (e.g., CNN and Transformer) may partially compensate by learning to extract relevant features from the aggregated sequence information. ### 2.2 Parameter-efficient fine-tuning by LoRA retains the advantage of variant-position embeddings and improves generalization over full fine-tuning

We next asked whether the design principles from the frozen setting persisted under low-rank adaptation (LoRA). In LoRA, the pretrained backbone remains fixed, and only a small set of low-rank parameters is added and trained Hu et al. (2022). To test this, we evaluated 54 LoRA configurations on NT-2. The sweep varied embedding strategy (variant-position and mean pooling), classifier head (MLP, CNN, Transformer), LoRA rank ( r ∈ { 8 , 16 , 32 } ), and learning rate (1 × 10 -5 , 3 × 10 -5 , 5 × 10 -5 ). To enable rapid iteration, these experiments used a reduced 5k-sample subset from the Clinvar earlier-release strict training set (Table 1).

Variant-position embeddings remained the dominant design choice under LoRA. All 27 variant-position configurations outperformed all 27 mean-pooling configurations. The worst variant-position model (MLP, r = 8, lr = 1 × 10 -5 ) achieved an AUROC of 0 . 809 and an AUPRC of 0 . 747. The best mean-pooling model (Transformer, r = 32, lr = 3 × 10 -5 ) reached only 0 . 778 AUROC and 0 . 719 AUPRC. This complete separation shows that embedding choice remained the strongest factor for parameter-efficient fine-tuning.

Having identified the best LoRA configuration, we scaled it to the full 44k Clinvar earlier-release strict training set and compared it against full fine-tuning of NT-2 across five learning rates, from 1 × 10 -6 to 1 × 10 -4 (Fig. 3). All runs used the same early stopping rule: training stopped when the train-validation gap exceeded 0.1 or when the validation metric failed to improve for 2,000 consecutive steps. Four full finetuning runs (1 × 10 -6 to 3 × 10 -5 ) stopped because the train-validation gap crossed the 0.1 threshold. In these runs, training AUROC and AUPRC continued to rise while validation performance plateaued or declined, indicating patterns of overfitting. The most aggressive full fine-tuning learning rate (1 × 10 -4 ) stopped for failure to improve and remained near chance throughout training. By contrast, the LoRA run stopped only after validation performance plateaued at a higher level while maintaining a smaller train-validation gap.

LoRA achieved the highest validation AUROC and AUPRC across the comparison. Several full fine-tuning runs reached higher training metrics, but none of them matched LoRA on the validation set. LoRA also improved on the best frozen model from the previous subsection (validation AUROC 0 . 893 versus 0 . 871). Together, these results identify LoRA as the preferred adaptation strategy for NT-2.

### 2.3 Reference-alternate difference improves the allele-aware input representation

Explicitly encoding the reference-to-alternate change improved the input representation. Earlier experiments used only the variant-position embedding from the alternate sequence ( alt ). This representation captures the local context of the alternate allele, but it does not explicitly encode the reference state. We therefore compared two richer inputs. The ref-alt representation concatenates the reference and alternate variant-position embeddings. The ref-alt-diff representation concatenates the reference embedding, alternate embedding, and their element-wise difference.

Among the three input representations, ref-alt-diff performed best. It improved performance by ∆AUROC = 0 . 008 and ∆AUPRC = 0 . 023 over the alt baseline. By contrast, alt and ref-alt performed similarly (Supplementary Fig. S2). This result indicates that explicitly encoding the allelic change is more informative than providing the two allelic states without their difference.

### 2.4 Combining the best-performing design choices yields the standalone GLM-Missense model

We combined the best-performing design choices from the preceding analyses into a single model. The final configuration used the NT-2 backbone, LoRA fine-tuning ( r = 32, lr = 5 × 10 -5 ), an MLP classifier head, and the variant-position ref-altdiff input representation. We also expanded the training set to include Pathogenic, Benign, Likely Pathogenic, and Likely Benign ClinVar variants Landrum et al. (2018). We refer to this model as GLM-Missense. Under this training setup, GLM-Missense achieved a validation AUROC of 0 . 908 and an AUPRC of 0 . 822 (Fig. S3).

Fine-tuned GLM-Missense outperformed zero-shot scoring from the same NT2 backbone. Zero-shot scores were computed by masked marginal log-likelihood, a standard language-model scoring strategy for mutation-effect prediction without 258 task-specific supervision Meier et al. (2021). On the ClinVar later-release set, GLM259 Missense achieved an AUROC of 0 . 938 and an AUPRC of 0 . 871. Zero-shot scoring 260 achieved an AUROC of 0 . 728 and an AUPRC of 0 . 592 (Fig. S4). These results indi261 cate that supervised fine-tuning effectively adds task-specific signal beyond the general 262 representations learned by the pretrained backbone. 263

GLM-Missense had the lowest average correlation with the other methods. Its pairwise | τ | values ranged from 0 . 39 with SIFT to 0 . 50 with REVEL. The remaining scores were more tightly clustered, with pairwise | τ | values mostly between 0 . 51 and 0 . 62. This pattern suggested that GLM-Missense captured signal that was less redundant with the other predictors.

GLM-Missense retained the strongest association with the true label after accounting for the other predictor scores. Following the rationale proposed by Lin et al. (unpublished manuscript), we asked whether each method still tracked pathogenicity once the signal shared with the other six scores had been removed. To test this, we computed the partial Spearman correlation between each predictor and the true label while controlling for the other six predictor scores (Fig. 4B). ### 2.6 Analyses of variants correctly classified by GLM-Missense suggest the model adds value in several biological contexts

To understand how and when GLM-Missense adds value beyond existing predictors, we examined variants in the ClinVar later-release set that GLM-Missense classified correctly when other methods often did not. We defined the GLM-Missense-correct subset as variants correctly classified by GLM-Missense but misclassified by at least four of six established predictors (REVEL, AlphaMissense, CADD, PolyPhen-2, SIFT, and ESM1b) (Supplementary Table S2). We compared this subset with the remaining variants in the corresponding background. We then examined a small set of biologically interpretable candidate features that might help explain these differences. These features were chosen as examples, not as a complete explanation, since a language model can capture sequence signals beyond a small set of manually curated features.

Higher predicted splicing impact pointed to one plausible biological mechanism 331 associated with the GLM-Missense-correct subset. Missense variants can be pathogenic 332 through disruption of splicing rather than, or in addition to, altered amino-acid 333 chemistry. We summarized splicing impact by the maximum SpliceAI delta score 334 across acceptor gain, acceptor loss, donor gain, and donor loss Jaganathan et al. 335 (2019). SpliceAI scores did not differ significantly between benign variants in the 336 GLM-Missense-correct subset and the benign background ( p = 0 . 308; Fig. 6A). In con337 trast, pathogenic variants in the GLM-Missense-correct subset had significantly higher 338 SpliceAI scores than the pathogenic background ( p = 8 . 4 × 10 -5 ). This result suggests 339 that part of the complementary pathogenic signal captured by GLM-Missense is associ340 ated with splice-relevant exonic sequence context. ### 3.1 Multi-species pretraining and variant-centered representations capture core biological properties of missense variants

Multi-species pretraining was a crucial factor affecting performance in the frozen400 backbone setting (Fig. 2). In particular, NT-2 was pretrained on sequences from 850 401 species spanning diverse phyla Dalla-Torre et al. (2025), whereas NT-1 was trained 402 only on human sequences. This result is biologically plausible because evolutionary 403 conservation is a central signal used in missense variant interpretation Cooper and 404 Shendure (2011); Capriotti and Fariselli (2022). Positions preserved across species are 405 often functionally constrained, so variants at those sites are more likely to be deleteri406 ous. Multi-species pretraining therefore allows NT-2 to encode patterns of evolutionary 407 constraint that are not observable from human sequence alone. Together, these find408 ings suggest that pretraining corpus diversity is a key driver of performance in genomic 409 missense prediction. 410

### 3.2 Low-rank adaptation preserves pretrained representations while enabling effective task adaptation

LoRA was effective for adapting NT-2 to missense classification. It achieved the highest validation AUROC and AUPRC, whereas full fine-tuning overfit quickly (Fig. 3). This contrast is notable because the downstream dataset is small relative to the model size. With only 44k training examples for a 500M-parameter backbone, full fine-tuning grants the optimizer enough degrees of freedom to fit training-set-specific patterns that may not generalize, a phenomenon broadly termed catastrophic forgetting McCloskey and Cohen (1989); Kirkpatrick et al. (2017). NT-2 has likely already captured essential genomic features during pretraining on billions of nucleotides across 850 species DallaTorre et al. (2025), including codon usage patterns and evolutionary constraints that are directly relevant to variant pathogenicity. Updating all parameters therefore risks overwriting these broadly useful representations in favor of spurious correlations in the comparatively small labeled set.

LoRA reduces the risk of overfitting by constraining the update space. It freezes the pretrained weights W 0 and learning only low-rank perturbations, ∆ W = BA , with rank r = 32 ≪ d = 1 , 024. This method constrains the effective degrees of freedom 438 available for task adaptation Hu et al. (2022). This low-rank constraint limits the 439 model's capacity to memorize training-specific patterns while still permitting mean440 ingful task adaptation. This behavior is consistent with the 'learns less, forgets less' 441 characterization of LoRA in large language models Biderman et al. (2024), where 442 the reduced rank of the learned perturbation inherently limits divergence from the 443 pretrained weights. Importantly, in our experiments, LoRA not only resisted overfit444 ting but also achieved the highest validation performance among all runs, surpassing 445 every full fine-tuning configuration in both AUROC and AUPRC. This goes beyond 446 the typical finding that LoRA merely preserves base-model knowledge at the cost of 447 target-domain performance. This result suggests that, when pretrained representa448 tions are already well aligned with the downstream task, restricting adaptation to a 449 low-rank subspace acts as a useful inductive bias rather than a limitation. 450

### 3.3 GLM-Missense adds complementary nucleotide-context information beyond current missense predictors

Most leading missense predictors were built to estimate the protein-level consequences or to aggregate multiple established evidence sources. SIFT and PolyPhen-2 focus on amino-acid substitution tolerance and protein features Ng and Henikoff (2003); Adzhubei et al. (2010). ESM1b and AlphaMissense use amino-acid sequence, evolutionary constraint, and/or structural context Brandes et al. (2023); Cheng et al. (2023). CADD integrates diverse genomic annotations Kircher et al. (2014). REVEL is itself an ensemble predictor Ioannidis et al. (2016). Clinical pathogenicity labels also reflect population, computational, functional, segregation, and phenotype data, rather than sequence context alone Richards et al. (2015). In this setting, a DNA-sequence-based model like GLM-Missense would not necessarily be expected to outperform current 481 state-of-the-art predictors as a standalone classifier. 482 GLM-Missense was valuable not simply as a standalone predictor, but as a source 483 of information that remained useful after strong existing methods were already 484 included. Its lower concordance with other predictor scores, retained association 485 with pathogenicity after adjustment for those scores, and high feature importance 486 in MetaMissense indicate that it contributed signal not fully represented by existing 487 methods. The additional performance gain of MetaMissense, despite the strong per488 formance of current missense predictors, suggests that part of the remaining error 489 in missense interpretation is still addressable. By learning directly from coding DNA 490 sequence, GLM-Missense may help capture information that is underrepresented in 491 protein-, conservation-, and annotation-centered predictors. 492

The most plausible reason GLM-Missense adds non-redundant information is its input representation. Most current missense predictors may already capture proteinlevel, conservation-based, structural, or annotation-derived signals. Many of these signals are also partially overlapping. In most cases, they are centered on the aminoacid substitution or on curated genomic features. A coding single-nucleotide variant, however, is not only an amino-acid substitution. It is also a nucleotide substitution embedded in DNA and pre-mRNA sequence context. Coding sequence contains overlapping layers of information, including splice-site strength, exonic splicing regulatory elements, codon context, RNA stability, local RNA structure, and other regulatory features Dalla-Torre et al. (2025); Presnyak et al. (2015); Wu et al. (2019); Sabarinathan et al. (2013); Fairbrother et al. (2004); Stergachis et al. (2013). Protein language models and protein-centered missense predictors do not directly model these layers because they operate after translation into amino-acid space. GLM-Missense instead operates directly on DNA sequence and can, in principle, represent both protein-coding and nucleotide-level constraints.

This difference in input representation also has practical implications for variant interpretation. In many workflows, a variant annotated as missense is evaluated mainly with missense-specific predictors. If those effects are not considered, part of the variant information may be missed or deferred to a separate downstream analysis. Genomic language models offer one way to address this gap by incorporating nucleotide-level context directly into missense prediction, either as standalone predictors or as components of meta-predictors such as MetaMissense.

Rare benign variants points to one context in which GLM-Missense appears to add value. Very rare benign variants often lack strong population-frequency support for a benign interpretation, even though they are not disease-causing. As a result, they can be over-prioritized by methods that use rarity as direct or indirect evidence for pathogenicity. This pattern is consistent with the CAGI 6 (Critical Assessment of Genome Interpretation) Annotate-All-Missense assessment, which identified very rare benign variants as a systematic challenge for predictors that incorporate allele frequency Rastogi et al. (2025). The enrichment of lower-frequency benign variants in the GLM-Missense-correct subset therefore suggests that GLM-Missense may be less dependent on rarity-related priors and can better distinguish rare benign variants from truly pathogenic ones (Fig. 6B).

Benign variants in constrained genes highlight a second context in which GLMMissense differs from other predictors. Gene-level constraint is informative for variant interpretation, but it can also be overly coarse. A benign missense variant in a constrained gene may still lie in a locally tolerant region or produce a tolerated amino-acid substitution despite the gene's overall intolerance to damaging variation. The CAGI 6 assessment also noted that predictors trained on curated pathogenicity labels can inherit gene-level label imbalances, because disease-associated and constrained genes are disproportionately represented among pathogenic examples Rastogi et al. (2025). GLM-Missense is not immune to this issue, since it is also trained on clinical labels. However, its input representation does not directly encode gene-level annotations such as LOEUF or gene identity. This difference may allow the model to rely more strongly on local sequence context and positional information, which may help it distinguish tolerated variants in constrained genes from truly pathogenic variants (Fig. 6C).

Amino-acid physicochemical severity represents a third context in which GLMMissense behaves differently from conventional missense predictors. Grantham distance is a coarse proxy for the physicochemical severity of an amino-acid substitution, incorporating differences in composition, polarity, and molecular volume Grantham (1974). However, clinical pathogenicity is not determined by substitution severity alone. Some radical substitutions are tolerated, whereas some conservative substitu570 tions are pathogenic because they affect critical residues, alter interactions, perturb 571 local structure, or influence nucleotide-level functions such as splicing Stefl et al. 572 (2013). ## 4 Conclusion

We systematically evaluated how genomic language models can be adapted for clinical missense variant pathogenicity prediction and identified a robust configuration for this task. The strongest setup combined the NT-2 backbone, variant-position embeddings, LoRA fine-tuning, an MLP classifier head, and a reference-alternate difference representation. The resulting fine-tuned model, GLM-Missense, substantially outperformed zero-shot scoring from the same backbone.

More importantly, GLM-Missense contributed non-redundant information beyond existing methods. GLM-Missense showed lower concordance with other predictors, retained the strongest partial association with pathogenicity after accounting for other predictor scores, and remained one of the most important inputs to the MetaMissense ensemble. These results indicate that GLM-Missense was not simply recapitulating protein-based, conservation-based, or annotation-based predictors. Instead, it provides complementary nucleotide-context signal that improved prediction when combined with other predictors.

Analyses of variants correctly classified by GLM-Missense but misclassified by several established predictors suggested that this complementary signal may be associated with splice-relevant exonic context and with cases in which allele frequency, gene612 level constraint, or amino-acid-change severity provides incomplete evidence for true 613 pathogenicity. However, these candidate features explained only a small fraction of 614 the overall GLM-Missense-correct signal. This suggests that GLM-Missense captures 615 additional sequence-derived information beyond manually curated annotations. 616

Together, our results show that effective adaptation of genomic language models for missense classification requires careful choices about backbone, representation, allele encoding, and fine-tuning strategy. Our results also suggest that future evaluations of missense predictors may benefit from considering not only standalone accuracy, but also whether a method contributes non-redundant information to existing predictors. GLM-Missense demonstrates that fine-tuned genomic language models can provide such complementary nucleotide-context information and can broaden missense interpretation beyond amino-acid substitution effects alone.

## 5 Methods

### 5.1 ClinVar sets, label mappings, and chromosome-exclusive splits

We curated GRCh38 missense variants from ClinVar Landrum et al. (2018, 2020). We defined two non-overlapping time-based sets. The earlier-release set contained variants already present in the November 3, 2025 ClinVar release. This set was used for model selection and for training the final standalone GLM-Missense model. The later-release set contained variants absent from the November 3, 2025 release and first appearing in subsequent releases through March 9, 2026. This temporal separation reduced direct overlap between model development and downstream evaluation.

### 5.2 Backbone-specific input sequence construction

All sequence inputs were centered on the candidate missense variant so that the altered position occupied a fixed location after tokenization. Depending on the experiment, the model received either the alternate sequence alone or paired reference and alternate sequences processed separately and combined only at the classifier input. This design enabled consistent extraction of variant-position and local-context embeddings across backbones while allowing each model to operate at its native tokenization scheme and context length.

Nucleotide Transformer v2 (NT-2). For each variant, we extracted an 11,999bp sequence centered on the variant position and tokenized it using 6-mer encoding with stride 6. This yielded 2,000 tokens, with the variant located at token index 1,000. We used the pretrained Nucleotide Transformer v2 (NT-2, 500M parameters), a BERT-style model trained by masked language modeling on 850 genomes from diverse species Dalla-Torre et al. (2025). NT-2 produces a 1,024-dimensional embedding per token. For the matched NT-2 versus NT-1 comparison, we also evaluated NT-2 on a 5,999-bp input window.

Nucleotide Transformer v1 (NT-1). NT-1 is a 500M-parameter model with a similar architecture to NT-2 but pretrained on human genomes only. For NT-1, we extracted a 5,999-bp sequence centered on the variant and applied the same 6-mer tokenization strategy as NT-2, yielding 1,000 tokens with the variant at token index 500. NT-1 produces a 1,280-dimensional embedding per token.

Caduceus-PS. For Caduceus-PS, we extracted a 29,999-bp sequence centered on the variant. Caduceus-PS is a bi-directional Mamba-based DNA language model with reverse-complement equivariance achieved by parameter sharing Schiff et al. (2024). Unlike NT-1 and NT-2, it operates at single-nucleotide resolution. The resulting sequence therefore contains 29,999 tokens, with the variant at nucleotide index 15,000. 687 Caduceus-PS produces a 512-dimensional embedding per token. Although Caduceus 688 models were pretrained with substantially longer contexts (up to 131 kb) Schiff et al. 689 (2024), we limited inputs to ∼ 30 kb to fit our available GPU memory while still 690 capturing distal sequence context. 691

We systematically explored four model components: embedding extraction strategy, allele-aware input representation, classifier head, and training regime.

Embedding strategies. For frozen-backbone and LoRA experiments, we compared several ways to derive the classifier input from backbone hidden states. Variant-position embedding extracts the embedding at the variant position (i.e., 1,024d at token index 1000 for NT-2, 1,280-d at token index 500 for NT-1, 512-d at token index 15,000 for Caduceus; see Fig. 1). Mean pooling averages hidden states across all tokens in the input sequence, with attention masking for proper handling of padding tokens. For Caduceus-PS, we applied a downsampled sequence representation in which the full 30 kb hidden-state sequence was reduced to 2,048 positions by adaptive average pooling, thereby reducing memory requirements for downstream CNN and Transformer heads.

Local-window pooling. To test whether pooling over a narrow neighborhood around the variant could recover local context without full-sequence dilution, we evaluated local-window mean pooling in the frozen NT-2 with CNN setting. In this analysis, hidden states were mean-pooled over symmetric windows of ± 8, ± 16, or ± 32 tokens around the variant position. With 6-mer tokenization, these correspond to ± 48, ± 96, and ± 192 nucleotides, respectively.

Allele-aware input representations. Most frozen-backbone and LoRA experiments used only the variant-position embedding from the alternate sequence ( alt ). In the final input-representation comparison, we also evaluated ref-alt , which concatenates the reference and alternate variant-position embeddings, and ref-alt-diff , which concatenates the reference embedding, alternate embedding, and their element-wise difference. For NT-2, these inputs have dimensions 1,024 ( alt ), 2,048 ( ref-alt ), and 3,072 ( ref-alt-diff ), respectively.

### 5.4 Optimization and training settings

Across all fine-tuning experiments, we used AdamW with ϵ = 10 -8 , β 1 = 0 . 9, β 2 = 0 . 999, and weight decay = 0 . 01, following the PyTorch defaults. We used a linear learning-rate schedule with warmup over the first 6% of training steps and linear decay to zero thereafter Liu et al. (2019). We optimized binary cross-entropy loss throughout.

Unless otherwise noted, neural-network models were trained with early stopping using a patience of 2 epochs while monitoring validation AUROC. The comparison between LoRA and full fine-tuning used a stricter stopping rule. In that comparison, training stopped when either the train-validation gap exceeded 0.1 or the validation metric failed to improve for 2,000 consecutive steps.

Frozen-backbone experiments used batch size 32 and were run for up to 5,000 optimization steps. LoRA model selection experiments were performed on a reduced 5k subset obtained by stratified subsampling from each class. These experiments used batch size 4 and were run for up to 4000 steps. Final GLM-Missense training and full fine-tuning used per-step batch sizes of 8 or 4, depending on GPU memory, together with gradient accumulation to maintain an effective batch size of 32.

All experiments were run on 1-4 NVIDIA H100 GPUs (80 GB memory each). Frozen-backbone experiments typically required ∼ 1-2 days per NT run and ∼ 4-6 hours per Caduceus run. LoRA training on the full earlier-release expanded training set required ∼ 3-5 days on one GPU. Full fine-tuning of NT-2 required ∼ 4 days on two GPUs.

### 5.8 Analyses of the GLM-Missense-correct subset

For these analyses, variants were first stratified by truth label into benign/likely benign and pathogenic/likely pathogenic groups. GLM-Missense predictions were binarized at a threshold of 0.5, and comparator predictors were binarized using the fixed methodspecific thresholds summarized in Supplementary Table S2. Within each stratum, we then defined the GLM-Missense-correct subset as variants that were classified correctly by GLM-Missense but misclassified by at least four of six comparator predictors (REVEL, AlphaMissense, CADD, PolyPhen-2, SIFT, and ESM1b). The corresponding background set consisted of all remaining variants in that stratum that were not part of the GLM-Missense-correct subset. Unless otherwise stated, these analyses were performed on the full later-release set.
