### Abstract

The value and nature of the representations learned during the pretraining of genomic language models (gLMs) remain actively debated. We introduce Nucleotide Generative Pretrained Transformer (GPT), a decoder-only transformer with single-nucleotide tokenization, to dissect the role of pretraining. Through experiments varying repetitive element (RE) weights during pretraining (0.0 -1.0), comparative finetuning against random initialization, linear probing of internal representations, and sparse autoencoder (SAE)-based interpretability, we evaluated the impact of pretraining and how REs in genomic data influence model learning. Models with moderate RE downweighting (0.5) consistently achieved optimal performance across seven genomic classification tasks, with pretrained models providing substantial performance gains over baselines. SAE feature annotation via sequence alignment revealed substantial RE-associated patterns in the pretrained model internal representations, suggesting that REs -which comprise 30% -60% of mammalian genomes -may dominate the pretraining objective. Our findings support the utility of pretraining and underscore the need for pretraining strategies that better accommodate repetitive sequences across the genome while also fostering the learning of less common but biologically important representations. This study highlights a key challenge for gLMs: ensuring that models broadly learn functional genomic syntax beyond simply recognizing ubiquitous repeats.

Keywords genomic language models, deep learning, sequence analysis, machine learning interpretability, repetitive elements

Genomic language models (gLMs) leverage transformer architectures-originally developed for Natural Language Processing (NLP)to model genomic sequences, promising insights into complex regulatory relationships within DNA sequences, transcriptional regulation, the functional impacts of genomic elements, and the fundamental syntax of the genome [1]. The development of gLMs borrows heavily from advancements in NLP, adapting architectures and self-supervised learning paradigms originally designed for text. While pretraining is foundational to modern NLP, its necessity and impact within genomics remains an open question.

gLMs commonly employ a two-stage training paradigm involving self-supervised pretraining on large, unlabeled genomic corpora, followed by task-specific fi netuning on labeled data. Central to this are (i) the pretraining objectives, and (ii) the methods used for tokenization, which is the conversion of DNA sequences into model inputs. Both the pretr aining objectives and tokenization methods can significantly influence the learned representations and downstream capabilities.

The two dominant training objectives adapted from NLP are Masked Language Modeling (MLM) and Causal Language Modeling (CLM) [2]. MLM involves masking a fraction of input tokens and training the model to predict the originally masked tokens based on the surrounding bidirectional context, encour aging the model to learn rich contextual representations. Many gLMs employ MLM, including state-of-the-art models DNABERT and Nucleotide Transformer (NT) [3, 4]. CLM is the hallmark of Generative Pretrained Transformer (GPT)-style models such as DNAGPT, megaDNA, and Gene42, which trains the model to predict the next token in a sequence given only the preceding tokens [5-7].

Pretraining is thought to enable models to develop meaningful, context-aware representations that are then refined during finetuning

for diverse downstream biological tasks [8]. However, the necessity and effectiveness of current gLM pretraining strategies have been questioned in recent literature, challenging the notion that these models are truly "foundational" in the way Large Language Models (LLMs) are for natural language. Vishniakov et al. directly compared seven different pretrained gLMs against their counterparts initialized with random weights and found r andomly initialized models frequently performed on par with, or even surpassed, the pretrained models [9]. Tang et al. evaluated the representational power of several pretrained gLMs via probing, concluding that these representations offered little advantage over one-hot encoded sequences for various regulatory genomics tasks [10]. These fi ndings raise the question: if pretraining does not reliably outperform random initialization, then what representations are being learned, and are they biologically meaningful?

One potential explanation for what representations are being learned lies in the fundamental nature of how gLMs learn from sequence distributions. gLMs are inherently statistical learners that allocate representational capacity proportionally to the frequency of patterns in their training data [11]. Given that repetitive elements (REs) comprise 30%-60% of mammalian genomes and often contain highly predictable, self-similar sequences [12], these elements may disproportionately influence the pretraining objective. The selfsupervised learning task optimizes for accurate prediction of the most statistically prevalent patterns, potentially causing models to dedicate substantial parameters to modeling repetitive sequences rather than the more diverse but less frequent regulatory elements that govern gene expression.

Recent advances in mechanistic interpretability-particularly sparse autoencoders (SAEs)-offer ways to decompose complex model representations into more understandable components [13, 14]. By learning a sparse 'dictionary' of features that can reconstruct the original embeddings, SAEs can help disentangle superimposed representations and reveal if the model has learned interpretable biological concepts. Initial applications of SAEs to biological language models have yielded promising results, and analyzing SAE features could provide insights into the model's internal processing [15, 16].

To investigate these questions surrounding pretraining value and representation learning, we introduce Nucleotide GPT, a decoder-only transformer with single-nucleotide resolution that adopts a stable and efficient transformer architecture popularized by Meta AI's LLaMA models [17]. We systematically evaluate the impact of pretraining through multiple experimental approaches: (i) examining how different weightings of REs during pretraining affect downstream performance, (ii) comparing fi netuning performance against both random initialization and simple linear baselines, (iii) assessing the effect of different tokenization strategies on model performance, (iv) benchmarking against state-of-the-art gLMs, (v) probing learned representations across different training states using linear classifiers and, (vi) training an SAE on the pretrained model's intermediate representations to identify interpretable features learned during pretraining.

### Results

Nucleotide GPT employs the characteristic gLM two-stage training paradigm of pretraining and fi netuning to evaluate the role and nature of learned representations (Fig. 1A). Architecturally, Nucleotide GPT is inspired by the LLaMA-style decoder-only transformer design, adopting several key components shown to improve training stability and performance in NLP: (i) applying Root Mean Square Normalization (RMS) before the attention and feed-forward blocks within each layer, instead of the traditional post-layer normalization, and (ii) employing the Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE) for positional encoding (Fig. 1B) [17-19]. The model comprises 12 such transformer la yers with a model dimension of 2048.

A crucial design choice is its single-nucleotide tokenization, where each base is treated as an individual token, preserving the highest sequence resolution. The initial pretraining stage aims to develop broad genomic sequence understanding by training the model on the human reference genome (GRCh38) using a self-supervised CLM objective-predicting the next nucleotide in 8192 bp sequences. In the subsequent fi netuning stage, these learned representations are refined for genomic understanding classification tasks, requiring minimal architectural modifications to the base model.

Models were evaluated using the Genomic Benchmarks collection [20]. We fi rst evaluated the models on a binary classification task distinguishing coding from intergenic sequences. The mammalian genome can be broadly separated into coding (sequences related to protein coding genes) and intergenic (sequences that do not code for protein and lie between coding genes). Coding genes account for ∼ 2% of the genome, and intergenic regions contain a broad array of other elements, such as regulatory sequences and genes for noncoding RNAs. To evaluate models' ability to classify regulatory elements, we use a multi-class dataset of human regulatory sequences derived from the Ensembl database, in addition to binary tasks classifying each type of regulatory sequence individually. These tasks focus on discriminating between three key categories involved in transcriptional control: promoters, which are sequences typically found upstream of gene start sites that serve as the binding site for RNA polymerase and initiate transcription; enhancers, which act as regulatory 'switches' that can be located far from the gene they influence but significantly boost its expression level by interacting with the promoter machinery; and open chromatin regions (OCRs). Open chromatin does not represent a single element type but rather signifies areas where the DNA is physically accessible to regulatory proteins because the surrounding chromatin structure is relaxed. While functionally related, accurately distinguishing promoters, enhancers, and the OCRs based on their underlying sequence features is crucial for understanding the logic on gene regulation. Models were also evaluated on classifying nonTATA promoter sequences, regulatory regions that initiate gene transcription despite lacking the canonical TATA-box sequence motif, often relying on other elements like Initiator (Inr) or Downstream Promoter Element (DPE) sequences [21].

#### Downweighting of REs optimizes performance

Given that REs comprise 30%-60% of mammalian genomes, we fi rst investigated how their treatment during pretraining affects downstream performance. We trained four variants of Nucleotide GPT with different loss weights applied to tokens identified as REs: 0.0 (complete exclusion), 0.1 (90% downweighting), 0.5 (50% downweighting), and 1.0 (no downweighting) (Table 1). Across seven genomic classification tasks, the model with 0.5 weighting consistently achieved optimal or near-optimal performance.

#### Single-nucleotide tokenization outperforms k-mer approaches

We compared single-nucleotide tokenization against two k-mer tokenization strategies: overlapping and non-overlapping 6-mers

(Table 3). Single-nucleotide tokenization achieved the highest performance on most tasks, with particularly large advantages for nonTATA promoters (94.5% versus 88.4% for overlapping 6-mers and 89.0% for non-overlapping) and Ensembl OCRs (77.1% versus 75.1% and 73.3%). The overlapping 6-mer approach showed competitive performance on the Ensembl Regulatory Sequences task (93.6% versus 93.2%), suggesting that for some multi-class regulatory classification problems, capturing fixed-length sequence patterns may provide slight benefits. However, single-nucleotide tokenization's consistent strong performance across diverse tasks, combined with its preservation of biological resolution, validates this design choice.

#### Nucleotide GPT achieves competitive performance against state-of-the-art models

Whenbenchmarkedagainstpublishedstate-of-the-art gLMs, Nucleotide GPT demonstrated strong performance, achieving the highest accuracy on four out of seven tasks (Table 4). The comparison included DNABERT [3], which uses k-mer tokenization and MLM; and HyenaDNA [22] with 32 K context length, which employs long-r ange sequence modeling; and Nucleotide Transformer [4], which combines single-nucleotide tokenization with bidirectional attention. To ensure fair comparison, all baseline models were fi netuned on each task for one epoch using consistent hyperparameters (AdamW optimizer, learning rate of 3e-5 for DNABERT and Nucleotide Transformer, 1e4 f or HyenaDNA), matching the fi netuning approach for Nucleotide GPT.

#### Linear probing reveals strong representations in Pretrained models

To further assess representation quality, we applied linear probes (logistic regression) to middle layer activations under three conditions: the base pretrained model, the model fi netuned after pretraining, and the model fi netuned from random initialization (Table 5). For most tasks, the pretrained model's representations showed strong linear separability even before fi netuning. For example, on Coding versus Intergenic classification, the pretrained model achieved 90.0% probe accuracy, nearly matching the fi netuned model (90.5%) and substantially exceeding the randomly initialized model (84.2%). Similarly, for non-TATA promoters, the pretrained (86.8%) and fi netuned (86.9%) models performed comparably, both outperforming random initialization (81.6%) by over 5%. The Ensembl Regulatory multi-class task was the exception to this trend, with the randomly initialized model's probe (76.6%) outperforming both pretrained (64.5%) and finetuned (67.2%) models, suggesting that task-specific fi netuning can reorganize representations for better linear separability even from r andom initialization, though the full model performance remained superior with pretraining (Table 2).

To understand how representations develop across model depth, we extended our linear probing analysis to all 12 transformer layers for four representative tasks: Coding versus Intergenic, Non-TATA Promoters, Human or Worm, and Ensembl Enhancers (Fig. 2). Since our probes extract activations from the residual stream at each la yer, we can track how task-relevant information accumulates through the network.

Across all four tasks, the pretrained and fi netuned models exhibited a consistent pattern: probe accuracy rose sharply in early layers (layers 1-4), then plateaued, with minimal further improvement in deeper layers. This early convergence pattern aligns with the information bottleneck principle: the network rapidly compresses task-relevant information in early-to-middle la yers, and subsequent layers maintain rather than substantially refine these compressed representations [23].

Randomly initialized models showed fundamentally different dynamics. These models achieved moderately high probe accuracy in the earliest layers and immediately plateaued with no meaningful improvement across the remaining layers. While random models started with higher early-layer performance, pretrained and finetuned models surpassed them by layer 4 and maintained this advantage through all deeper layers. This pattern suggests the residual stream of randomly initialized models accumulates information that plateaus early rather than undergoing the compression and abstraction process characteristic of pretrained networks.

To assess whether different layers learn distinct versus redundant representations, we computed Pearson correlations between linear probe predictions from all layer pairs for pretrained, fi netuned, and random initialization models (Fig. 3). Pretrained and fi netuned models exhibited nearly identical hierarchical block structures across all tasks. Early layers showed low prediction correlations with later layers while predictions within each block were highly correlated ( r &gt; .95 among layers 5-12) The transition point around layer 4 corresponds precisely to where probe accuracy plateaus, suggesting that layers 14 progressively build task-relevant representations, while layers 5-12 maintain these compressed representations with minimal additional transformation. The similarity between pretrained and fi netuned correlation structures indicates that task-specific fi netuning preserves the hierarchical organization established during pretraining rather than fundamentally reorganizing it. In contrast, randomly initialized models showed uniformly high correlations ( r &gt; .90) across nearly all layer pairs, with minimal block structure. Combined with the fl at probe accuracy profiles, this suggests random initialization leads to redundant rather than hierarchical processing across depth.

#### SAE features are enriched for REs

After pretraining the 0.5-weighted Nucleotide GPT, we trained an SAE on the intermediate activations of the model's residual stream. The SAE expanded the 2048-dimensional activations to a sparse 8192-dimensional latent space and then reconstructed the original representation. Linear probes trained on these sparse SAE features achieved compar able performance to probes trained on dense activations across classification tasks (Supplementary Table 1), suggesting that sparse decomposition may preserve task-relevant information. By extracting the top sequences that maximally activated each latent feature, we manually annotated f eatures using RE alignment tool Dfam [24]. REs are DNA sequences present in multiple copies that constitute 30-60% of mammalian genomes. Transposable elements (TEs), a major class of REs, are mobile DNA sequences that propagate within genomes [25]. While they can threaten genomic integrity if not properly regulated and may have deleterious effects, they can also represent a significant source of genetic variation that has contributed to evolutionary innovation [26].

Several SAE features were notably enriched for TEs. Two latent features consistently responded to sequences with high similarity to the THE1D and THE1B repeats -elements belonging to the endogenous retrovirus-like mammalian-apparent long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposon (ERVL-MaLR) class (Table 6) [27]. Additional features highlighted distinct regions of Long Interspersed Nuclear Element (LINE) retrotransposons. One mapped primarily to the 3 ′ untranslated regions, which contain conserved polyadenylation signals and RNA motifs, while another concentrated on 5 ′ regions that harbor internal promoters for certain L1PA subfamilies (Table 6) [28]. Features also showed strong activation on sequences aligning to subfamilies of the highly abundant Alu family of Short Interspersed Nuclear Elements (SINEs) (Table 6). Alu elements are mobile DNA sequences comprising over 10% of the human genome and have been found to contribute significantly to genomic evolution through RNA editing, exonization, and transcript variation [29].

### Discussion

Pretraining Nucleotide GPT with moderate downweighting of REs yielded broadly generalizable representations that significantly improved downstream task performance. The optimal performance achieved with 0.5 RE weighting across all seven classification benchmarks suggests that neither complete exclusion nor full inclusion of these abundant sequences produces the best representations. Models with this balanced weighting outperformed both random initialization and linear baselines, with striking gains on non-TATA promoters (14% improvement over random initialization) and coding versus intergenic classification (16% over linear baseline). This benefit was further supported when linear classifiers were applied to frozen pretrained activations, which achieved 90.0% accuracy on coding versus intergenic sequences -nearly matching the fully finetuned model and substantially exceeding the 84.2% achieved by probing randomly initialized models. Extended layer-wise probing and inter-layer correlation analysis revealed that pretrained and finetuned models develop representations that stabilize around layer 4 and substantially outperform random initialization from that point onward, while random models show fl at performance and uniformly high inter-layer correlations across all depths. These results demonstrate that appropriate pretraining effectively organizes the internal representation space in a task-relevant manner even before supervised fi netuning.

The choice of tokenization has significant implications for model performance and interpretation. Early models like DNABERT often used k-mer tok enization (fixed-length substrings), but this approach disrupts functional elements spanning k-mer boundaries and suffers from sampling inefficiencies [3]. Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE), adopted by models like DNABERT-2 and GENA-LM, learns variable-length tokens based on frequency, offering better compression but potentially creating statistically-driven units that lack biological meaning and limit sequence granularity [1, 30]. In contrast, the single-nucleotide tokenization employed by Nucleotide GPT as well as Hyena DNA and Evo/Evo 2, preserves the highest biological resolution [16, 22]. Our direct comparison validates this design choice-single-nucleotide tokenization outperformed both overlapping and non-overlapping 6mer approaches on most tasks, with particularly large advantages for Non-TATA promoters (94.5% versus 88.4% for overlapping 6mers) and Ensembl OCRs (77.1% versus 75.1%). However, this granularity also increases sensitivity to the raw statistical distribution of nucleotides, meaning highly abundant sequences, such as REs, could disproportionately influence the pretraining objective.

REs, including transposons (LTRs, LINEs, SINEs) and simple repeats, constitute a large fraction of many eukaryotic genomes, particularly in mammals. While historically dismissed as 'junk DNA,' REs are increasingly recognized for their significant roles in genome evolution, structure, and gene regulation, with many regulatory elements like enhancers and promoters having originated from RE sequences. Training an SAE on Nucleotide GPT's intermediate representations provides evidence that the model learns to recognize these ubiquitous genomic features during self-supervised pretraining-even with 0.5 downweighting. The SAE yielded sparse latent features that were strongly and consistently activated by sequences corresponding to distinct types of REs. Features were identified for: LTR retrotransposons -specific features mapped to THE1D and THE1B (both from the ERVLMaLR family); LINE-1 elements -multiple distinct features were associated with different functional regions of LINE-1 retrotransposons, including the 3'UTR, the 5 ′ region, and parts of the ORF-2 coding region; and SINEs-specifically Alu subfamilies. The decomposition of LINE-1 elements into features representing distinct sub-regions suggests that the model is not simply identifying LINEs as a monolithic category but is learning to recognize the characteristic sequence patterns associated with their different components. This capability likely arises from the statistical regularities in the genome, where these subregions appear frequently across numerous LINE-1 insertions.

RE enrichment analysis provides biological context for why RE weighting matters so much. Significant enrichment and depletion patterns we observe demonstrate that RE distributions can be informative for distinguishing regulatory regions. Major TE classes showed progressive depletion from promoters to OCRs (LINE elements: 1.00fold to 0.34-fold; SINE elements: 1.13-fold to 0.33-fold), while small RNA genes showed strong promoter enrichment (scRNA: 8.74-fold; snRNA: 6.85-fold). These patterns explain why complete exclusion of REs from the loss function hurts performance. However, the abundance of SAE features that activate on REs suggests that even when downweighted in the loss function, they can still dominate the learning objective and disproportionately take up representational space. The model becomes overly proficient at predicting nucleotides with common repeats, leading to inflated performance on the pretraining task itself and any downstream tasks that have notable repeat

|

enrichment, without necessarily translating to better understanding of genomic syntax. The functional heterogeneity of REs also presents a challenge. Some are actively transcribed, act as regulatory elements, or contribute to genome structure, while others are inactive remnants. A foundational model needs to ideally distinguish between these different functional states, which may not be achievable with standard pretraining objectives solely based on sequence modeling without biological context. Our SAE fi ndings strongly suggest that developing more sophisticated pretraining strategies could be a fruitful avenue for bridging the gap between gLM benchmark success and genuine understanding of genomic syntax. One emerging avenue for improvement is biologically-informed tokenization, which aims to embed biological inductive bias creating tokens that explicitly encode not just sequence but also genomic variants and structural annotations like gene boundaries, exon/intron locations, or REs.

While our results demonstrate clear benefits from pretraining Nucleotide GPT, this contrasts with recent reports by Vishniakov et al . and Tang et al. that found limited value in pretraining other gLMs [9, 10]. Several factors likely contribute to this divergence. Methodologically, our study emphasized improved finetuning performance as evidence of value, whereas Tang et al. focused on probing only frozen pretrained representations, fi nding they offered little advantage over one-hot encoding for complex regulatory tasks without finetuning [10]. Architecturally, Nucleotide GPT's combination of single-nucleotide tokenization and a LLaMA-style decoder may capture pretraining benefits more effectively than the other architectures assessed by Vishniakov et al . [9]. Notably, Vishniakov et al. found that randomly initialized decoder models using character tokenizationlike Nucleotide GPT-could perform surprisingly well, sometimes exceeding larger pretrained models, highlighting the importance of architecture and tokenization choices [9]. Our combination of linear probing showing strong linear separability in pretrained embeddings and SAE analysis revealing interpretable biological features directly addresses the question of what is learned during pretraining. These findings suggest that for Nucleotide GPT, pretraining provides more than just a favorable random initialization; it imparts substantive, linearly decodable, and interpretable-though RE-biasedstructur al information about the genome.

Several limitations of our study should be acknowledged. Our RE weighting experiments were conducted only on the human genome, and optimal weights may differ for other species with different RE compositions. Additionally, we employed CLM as our pretraining objective without comparison to MLM. While CLM's objective of predicting every next token provides a denser training signal compared to MLM's typical 15% masking rate, potentially leading to more sample-efficient learning, MLM could capture bidirectional context that might benefit certain regulatory element classification tasks where both upstream and downstream context are important. Our strong performance across all benchmarks suggests that CLM effectively learns genomic representations, but direct comparison between CLM and MLM objectives and their implications for model interpretability remain unexplored. The SAE-based interpretability analysis faces several methodological constraints inherent to this emerging technique. We analyzed only the 0.5-weighted pretrained model's middle layer activations. SAEs remain a novel interpretability method with limited established practices for systematic feature annotation and validation. Annotating SAE features is challenging - while we identified clear RE-associated features through manual inspection and sequence alignment, many latent features likely

|

capture more subtle patterns that resist straightforward biological interpretation. The lack of standardized methods for comparing features between different SAEs presents a significant barrier to understanding how representations evolve from across layers or between training stages. This prevented us from examining how different weighting schemes affect the distribution of learned features or how fi netuning reorganizes the RE-dominated feature space we observed. Furthermore, validating that identified features are causally important for model behavior, rather than merely correlational patterns in the training data, remains an open challenge in SAE interpretability. Future work developing rigorous methods for SAE feature comparison and validation will be essential for understanding how genomic representations evolve across training stages and architectural choices.

### Methods

#### Datasets

For pretraining, Nucleotide GPT was trained on the human reference genome (GRCh38), comprising ∼ 3.1 billion nucleotides. The genome was divided into non-overlapping 8192 bp sequences for model input. REs in the genome were identified using RepeatMasker annotations to enable weighted loss experiments.

We evaluated Nucleotide GPT on seven benchmark datasets from the Genomic Benchmarks collection (accessible via the Genomic Benchmarks repository bmcgenomdata.biomedcentral.com): (i) Coding Versus Intergenic-binary classification of protein-coding versus intergenic sequences, (ii) Ensembl Regulatory-multi-class classification of promoters, enhancers, and OCRs, (iii) Non-TATA Promoters-binary classification of human non-TATA promoter sequences, (iv) Ensembl Enhancers-binary classification of enhancer sequences, (v) Cohn Enhancers-binary classification of enhancer sequences from an alternative dataset, (vi) Ensembl OCRs-binary classification of OCRs, and (vii) Human or worm-species classification between human and Caenorhabditis elegans sequences. Each dataset is supplied with predefined training and test splits. All input sequences c onsist of standard DNA nucleotides (A, C, G, T).

The datasets used for fi netuning are derived entirely from genomic sequences, with both positive and negative samples extracted from reference genomes. For human datasets, positive samples represent annotated functional elements (promoters, enhancers, open chromatin regions) from databases such as Ensembl Regulatory Build, FANTOM5, and EPD. Negative samples were randomly generated from the same reference genome (GRCh38) to match the length distribution of positive samples while avoiding overlap with annotated elements. Since our model was pretrained on the GRCh38 human reference genome, there is inherent overlap: all benchmark sequences (both positive and negative) are subsequences of the pretraining data. However, this does not constitute data leakage for several reasons: (i) pretraining used unsupervised causal language modeling on continuous 8192 bp windows without any functional labels, (ii) finetuning uses much shorter sequences with supervised labels, and (iii) the model must learn to distinguish functional elements from genomic background based on sequence patterns alone, not memorized position-label associations. This setup mirrors the intended use case where models pretrained on reference genomes are fine-tuned to identify functional elements within those same genomes.

#### Tokenization

We investigated three tok enization approaches:

- (i) Single-nucleotide tokenization: Each nucleotide (A, T, G, C) is represented as an individual token, preserving maximum biological resolution.
- (ii) Overlapping 6-mer tokenization: Sequences are tokenized using sliding windows of 6 nucleotides with a stride of 1.
- (iii) Non-overlapping 6-mer tokenization: Sequences are divided into consecutive, non-overlapping 6-nucleotide tokens.

For all strategies, special tokens were included for padding and unknown bases.

#### Linear probing e xperiments

To evaluate the quality and linear separability of learned representations, linear probing experiments were conducted on the Genomic Benchmarks tasks.

For each task and each model state under evaluation, sequencelevel features were extracted as follows: Input DNA sequences were tokenized and processed by the relevant model forward pass. Activations from the transformer layers were extracted. For raw activation probes, the extracted raw activation vectors from the specified la yer were processed using global average pooling over all non-padding token positions to yield a single 2048-dimensional feature vector per sequence. These pooled feature vectors were extracted for the training and test sets of the respective downstream tasks using different source model weights.

A linear probe classifier ('sklearn.linear model.LogisticRegression') was trained on the extracted feature vectors from the training set and evaluated on the features from the test set for each condition. For binary classification tasks, the following hyperparameters were used: 'solver = 'liblinear'', 'penalty = 'L2'', 'C = 1.0', 'max iter = 2000', and 'random state = 42'. For multi-class tasks, multinomial logistic regression was employed with 'multi class = 'multinomial'', 'solver = 'lbfgs'', 'C = 1.0', 'max iter = 2000', and 'random state = 42'. Performance was evaluated using overall accuracy, F 1-score, and MCC.

### Key Points

- Moderate downweighting (0.5) of repetitive elements (REs) during pretraining optimizes performance across all genomic classification t asks.
- Linear probing reveals that pretraining creates representations with strong linear separability for biological concepts even before task-specific f inetuning.
- Sparse autoencoder (SAE) feature annotation finds RE-associated features even with downweighting.
- RE enrichment analysis demonstrates distinct distribution patterns across regulatory regions, explaining why completely excluding REs f rom pretraining hurts performance.
