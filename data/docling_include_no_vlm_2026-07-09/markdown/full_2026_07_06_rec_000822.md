### Abstract

Background RNA, an essential component of the central dogma of molecular biology, plays versatile roles in all cellular processes. RNA large language models (LLMs) are emerging as powerful methods in RNA research to decipher its intricate network of function and regulation. However, previous RNA LLMs were based on the Transformer model and pre-trained on short segment of non-coding RNAs, which limits their general usability. Here we present the first full-length RNA foundation model, HydraRNA, which is based on a hybrid architecture of bidirectional state space model and multi-head attention mechanism.

Results HydraRNA is pre-trained on a large amount of both protein-coding mRNAs and non-coding RNAs. Despite being pre-trained with the fewest parameters and the least GPU resources, HydraRNA learns better RNA representations and outperforms the existing foundation models on a variety of mRNA-related tasks, including coding/ non-coding RNA classification, prediction of RNA secondary structure, RBP binding sites, splicing and polyadenylation sites, mRNA stability and translation efficiency. Furthermore, HydraRNA can accurately predict the effect of mutations and estimate the relative contributions of different mRNA regions to the RNA stability and translation.

Conclusions Our results demonstrate that the hybrid architecture outperforms the pure Transformer architectures in RNA language modeling. We anticipate that HydraRNA will enable dissecting the diverse properties of mRNA, accelerating the research of mRNA regulation and facilitating the optimal design of mRNA therapeutics.

### Background

Ribonucleic acids (RNAs) can be categorized into two classes: protein-coding RNAs and non-coding RNAs. The former serves as carriers of genetic information in the central dogma of molecular biology, whereas the latter plays versatile roles in various important  cellular  processes  [1].  The  significance  of  RNA  has  been  highlighted  in  numerous  fields,  from  fundamental  molecular  biology  and  genetics  to  biotechnology  and medicine [2, 3]. For instance, the role of mRNA in the translation process has made it a  focal  point  for  RNA-based  therapies,  particularly  in  the  context  of  mRNA  vaccines for infectious diseases such as COVID-19 [4, 5]. Given the importance of RNA and the complexity of  RNA biology, there  has  been  increasing  interest  in  the  development  of

Large language models (LLMs), a form of artificial intelligence originating from natural language processing, have emerged as powerful methods in RNA research. By pre-training on vast amounts of RNA sequences and fine-tuning on experimental data, LLMs for RNA show promising performance in various RNA-related downstream tasks, such as predicting RNA functions and structures. RNA-FM [6], the first RNA foundation model, was  pre-trained  on  non-coding  RNA  segments  from  RNAcentral  [7]  database  and fine-tuned  to  solve  multiple  tasks  including  prediction  of  RNA  secondary  structures. RNAErnie [8], which was built upon the Enhanced Representation through Knowledge Integration (ERNIE) [9] framework, used a motif-aware pre-training strategy on RNAcentral  database,  and  showed  superior  performance  in  multiple  tasks  compared  with other baselines [8], including RNABERT [10], RNA-MSM [11] and RNA-FM. RiNALMo with 650 M parameters pre-trained on non-coding RNAs, is the largest published RNA language model to date, and achieves state-of-the-art performances on several downstream tasks including RNA secondary structure prediction [12]. All these methods were essentially based on the Transformer [13] architecture. However, since attention mechanisms scale quadratically with sequence length [13], these models are limited by input size,  and  are  often  unable  to  handle  full-length  mRNA  sequence  as  a  whole  unit.  For example, the maximum input lengths for RNA-FM, RiNALMo and RNAErnie are 1024, 1024 and 512 nucleotides, respectively. Moreover, current models were pre-trained on truncated short segments of non-coding RNAs, which may hamper its power on fulllength mRNA-related tasks. In addition to these general RNA LLMs, there are also specialized RNA LLMs, such as 3'UTRBERT [14] and UTR-LM [15], which focus only on the untranslated regions (UTRs) of the mRNA. These methods, by design, are not optimal for general tasks. Indeed, whether such specialized RNA LLMs have an advantage over  general  RNA  foundation  model  even  on  UTR-related  tasks  is  also  controversial. Moreover, coding sequences are well-known to affect the mRNA translation [16, 17]. Therefore, although predicting the effect of 5'UTR or 3'UTR on RNA properties, such as mRNA translation efficiency and stability, is valuable for designing sequences of mRNA therapeutics, the accurate prediction of the properties of mRNA in its full-length would be necessary for the further sequence optimization. This is increasingly relevant with the rise of mRNA-based therapies. To accelerate the AI-empowered research in RNA biology and medicine, there is a growing demand for a new RNA foundation model that can handle both non-coding RNA and protein-coding mRNA in its full-length.

To tackle these problems, we developed a new full-length RNA language model named HydraRNA. It was based on a hybrid architecture combining bidirectional state space model  [18]  with  multi-head  attention  mechanism  [13],  and  was  pre-trained  on  both mRNA and non-coding RNA sequences. We applied HydraRNA on 10 types of RNArelevant  tasks,  including  RNA  classification,  prediction  of  RNA  secondary  structure, RBP  binding  sites,  splice  and  polyadenylation  sites,  mRNA  stability  and  translation efficiency.  HydraRNA outperformed current RNA LLM models in 8 out of 10 downstream tasks. Furthermore, HydraRNA accurately predicted the effects of mutations and estimated the relative contribution of different parts of mRNA to the RNA stability and translation.  Overall,  we  demonstrated  HydraRNA as a valuable method for dissecting the  diverse  properties  of  mRNA,  which  could  further  accelerate  the  AI-empowered research in RNA biology and medicine.

### Results

In the following sections, we first introduce the development of HydraRNA. Then, we present  the  experiment  results  for  HydraRNA  evaluation,  which  includes  both  unsupervised  and  supervised  learning.  Our  analysis  includes  evaluation  not  only  at  RNA segment level (5'UTR and 3'UTR), but also at the full-length mRNA level. We fully or partially fine-tuned the other RNA LLM models using the same approach and prediction head as for HydrRNA. Unless otherwise specified, a simple multi-layer perceptron (MLP) predictor with only one hidden layer was used for each downstream task.

#### Development of HydraRNA

HydraRNA is based on a hybrid architecture consisting of both Hydra [18] and multihead attention (MHA) [13] layers (Fig. 1a). It has 12 layers in total. Each layer contains a Hydra module except the 6th and 12th layer, which contains an MHA module. Hydra is  a  natural  bidirectional  extension  of  the  structured  state  space  model  [18]  (Fig.  1b). HydraRNA takes advantages of Hydra's linear scalability of sequence length and high efficiency in in-context learning. The MHA layer was inserted into the stack of Hydra layers  to  further  improve  the  model  quality  as  well  as  to  enhance  the  explainability.

Each layer has a hidden state dimension of 1024. HydraRNA was pre-trained on 28 million sequences of non-coding RNAs and protein-coding mRNAs from RNAcentral and NCBI databases (Fig. 1c). Each nucleotide is processed as a single token. A BERT-like random masking strategy was used in the pre-training step. To speed up training, RNA sequences longer than 4096 nt were split into segments of length at most 4096 nt. About 90% of RNAs were pre-trained as full-length  sequences  without  segmentation  (Additional file 1: Figure S1). It took only about 92 h to pre-train HydraRNA on a server with 8  consumer-grade 4090D GPU cards. The time and cost of training were significantly reduced compared to previous RNA LLMs (Additional file 1: Table S1). We conducted several ablation studies to determine the optimal choice of architecture and pre-training  strategy.  More  technical  details  were  provided  in  the  Methods  section.  For  each downstream task, a task-specific prediction head was added to the HydraRNA encoder (Fig. 1d). Unless otherwise specified, a simple MLP predictor with a single 128-dimensional hidden layer was employed for each task. We fully or partially fine-tuned other RNA LLM models using the same approach as we employed for HydrRNA. A radar plot (Fig. 1e) was shown for overview of the prediction performance comparison.

#### Visualization of the learned embedding of RNA

RNAs can be categorized into two types: protein-coding mRNAs and ncRNAs. It has been  shown  previously  that  the  characteristics  of  various  ncRNA  types  are  partially captured within the embeddings learned by the RNA language models [8]. To test the capability of the RNA embeddings to distinguish human protein-coding and non-coding RNAs, we visualized the RNA sequences by employing 2D principal component analysis  (PCA)  to  the  embeddings and applied K-means clustering in an unsupervised setting.  The  lncRNA\_H  dataset  was  obtained  from  the  RNAErnie  paper.  We  compared HydraRNA to three published RNA foundation models, including RNA-FM, RNAErnie  and  RiNALMo  (Fig.  2a).  Since  RNA-FM,  RNAErnie  and  RiNALMo  were  pretrained only on non-coding RNAs, they struggle to separate these two types of RNA. HydraRNA, in contrast, captures the intrinsic characteristic differences between these two types of RNA and the 2D embedding exhibits a clearer separable clustering structure with a normalized mutual information (NMI) score of 0.45. We next test whether the embeddings  contain  sufficient  information  to  distinguish  human  protein-coding  and non-coding RNAs in a supervised classification task (Additional file 1: Table S2). Again, HydraRNA  achieves  an  AUC  of  0.991,  which  outperforms  the  other  RNA  language models (0.925, 0.902 and 0.986 for RNA-FM, RNAErnie and RiNALMo, respectively). HydraRNA also outperforms the three models in terms of F1-score and accuracy, and the same trend holds for the mouse dataset (Additional file 1: Table S2).

We further checked whether HydraRNA could distinguish the 5'UTR, CDS and 3'UTR of  the  mRNA  without  supervised  learning.  The  dataset  was  curated  from  a  previous study [19]. HydraRNA captures the intrinsic characteristic differences among the three mRNA regions, and the 2D embedding exhibits a clearer separable clustering structure with an NMI score of 0.80, which is higher than other three methods (Fig. 2b). Interestingly,  for  the  HydraRNA embedding, CDS sequences are clearly separated from UTR sequences, while some of the 5'UTRs and 3'UTRs are not separable. These results demonstrated the emergent abilities of HydraRNA acquired through pre-training on millions of mRNA and ncRNA sequences. Because pre-training was unsupervised, the emergent ability to distinguish long-noncoding RNAs from protein coding RNAs, and the emergent  ability  to  distinguish  different  parts  of  mRNA  is  non-trivial,  although  the  better performances achieved by HydraRNA are likely due to the fact that only HydraRNA was pre-trained on both ncRNA and mRNA sequences.

#### Prediction of RNA secondary structure

RNA secondary structure is vital for its stability and function. We utilized the dataset proposed in Singh et al. [20] derived from bpRNA-1m database [21] to benchmark the task of RNA secondary structure prediction. This dataset was split into three subsets: TR0  (10,814  structures)  for  training,  TV0  (1,300  structures)  for  validation  and  TS0 (1,305  structures)  for  testing,  and  these  are  non-redundant  at  the  sequence  similarity level.  The task is to classify each nucleotide pair as base-paired or unpaired. We finetuned HydraRNA on this binary classification task with binary cross-entropy loss, and compared it to RiNALMo, RNA-FM, RNAErnie and other popular methods specialized for secondary structure prediction, such as SPOT-RNA [20] and UFold [22] (Table 1). A fine-tuned without pre-training version, HydraRNA\_random, was also included as control. We found that HydraRNAv1 improved the average F1-score by a large margin over HydraRNA\_random (from 0.64 to 0.73). This result encouraged us to further pre-train HydraRNA using more ncRNA sequences, which leaded to HydraRNAv2 (see Methods section).  HydraRNAv2  further  improved  the  average  F1-score  to  0.76,  which  is  even higher than that of RiNALMo (0.75). Notably, RiNALMo is almost 8 times larger than HydraRNA (650 M vs 84 M).This indicates pre-training indeed could boost prediction performance in this task. HydraRNAv2 achieves statistically higher F1-scores than those of RiNALMo (0.75, p =  1.8e-5), RNA-FM (0.68, p &lt;  2.2e-16) and RNAErnie (0.66, p &lt;  2.2e16) (Fig. 3a, Table 1). Among the 1,305 RNAs in TS0 test set, HydraRNAv2 outperforms RiNALMo, RNA-FM and RNAErnie for 693, 844 and 895 RNAs, respectively, while it underperforms RiNALMo, RNA-FM and RNAErnie for 510, 247 and 214 RNAs, respectively (Fig. 3b, c, d). Whereas all the methods performed well for the relatively simple structures (Fig. 3e and Additional file 1: Figure S2), for the complex and longer ones, such as telomerase RNA, as shown in Fig. 3f, the prediction from HydraRNAv2 is much closer to the native structure.

#### Prediction of RNA-binding protein binding site

RNA-binding  proteins  (RBPs)  typically  recognize  and  bind  to  specific  sequences or  structures  on  the  RNA  transcripts  to  realize  their  regulatory  functions.  To  benchmark the performance of HydraRNA on predicting the binding sites of RBPs, we used a dataset including 31 CLIP experiments on 19 RBPs curated by Yang et al. [14]. For the benchmark study, we included RiNALMo, RNA-FM, RNAErnie as well as 3UTRBERT which previously showed state-of-the-art performance on this dataset [14]. As shown in  Fig.  4a,  HydraRNA  achieved  similar  performance  (average  AUROC = 0.858,  average AUPRC = 0.663) compared to RiNALMo (average AUROC = 0.859, average AUPRC = 0.666)  and  3UTRBERT  (average  AUROC = 0.862,  average  AUPRC = 0.667) (Fig.  4a,  b).  Among these 31 CLIP experiments, HydraRNA outperformed RiNALMo, RNAErnie  and  RNA-FM  in  16,  25  and  27  experiments  in  terms  of  AUPRC  metric, respectively  (Additional  file  1:  Figure  S3).  Detailed  result  for  each  RBP  was  listed  in Additional file 1: Table S3 and S4. Intriguingly, as shown in Fig. 4c, the 31 CLIP experiments  could  be  separated  into  two  groups  based  on  the  performance  of  all  the  four methods. For example, whereas all the four methods could relatively well predict  the binding sites of PUM2, hnRNPC and eIF4AIII, the performance was poor for NSUN2, AGO2 and IGF2BP1. We hypothesized that the prediction performance is dependent on the inherent sequence features determining RBP binding affinity. Some RBPs recognize and bind strongly to distinct sequence motifs, while the binding motifs of other RBPs are more ambiguous. Conceivably, it would be much easier for the models that have already learned the RNA sequence features by pre-training to predict the binding sites for the RBPs with strong and distinct binding motifs. We check the RBP consensus motifs from mCrossBase [23] database. Indeed, compared to those with satisfactory prediction performance, the more challenging RBPs tend to have lower motif scores (Fig. 4d, Additional  file  1:  Table  S5),  indicating  that  RNA  language  models  may  contribute  little  to boosting the prediction performance for these RBPs.

To  determine  which  nucleotides  of  RNA  influence  the  RBP  binding,  we  applied  in silico mutagenesis (ISM) anaylsis using the fine-tuned HydraRNA as scoring function. The ISM-derived sequence logo for RBP binding site resemble the RBP binding motifs quite well. #### Prediction of 3'UTR effect on mRNA stability

The 3' untranslated region (3'UTR) contains cis-regulatory elements that control mRNA stability and translation [26, 27]. We then evaluated the performance of our model on a benchmark datasets where the effect of 3'UTR on mRNA stability was measured based on  massively  parallel  reporter  assays  performed  in  human  T  lymphoblast  Jurkat  and human lung epithelial Beas2B cells [28, 29]. This datasets were randomly split into training, validation and testing set at 8:1:1 ratio. HydraRNA outperforms RiNALMo, RNAFM and RNAErnie, with a Pearson correlation of 0.694 in Beas2B cells (Fig. 5a), which is  higher than all the other baseline methods, including a specialized 3'UTR language model, 3'UTRBERT ( R =  0.686). We also tested the ability of HydraRNA in cross-celltype prediction (Additional file 1: Figure S4). After fine-tuning on Beas2B dataset, the prediction performance in Jurkat dataset remained high (Pearson R =  0.471) but was lower than within-celltype performance (Pearson R =  0.694 for Beas2B, and Pearson R =  0.708 for Jurkat). We further tested the ability of HydraRNA in predicting the effect of mutations  on  mRNA  stability  (Fig.  5b).  In  the  same  study  that  generated  the  benchmark dataset, the RNA stability was measured for a fast-UTR systematic mutagenesis library that  included  201  single-base  substitutions  within  a  67-nucleotide  region  of  CXCL2 3'UTR [26]. We removed all the sequences derived from CXCL2 genes from the training dataset, so the CXCL2 dataset could be treated as an independent testing dataset, since the wild-type and mutant CXCL2 sequences were not used in the training process. As shown in Fig. 5b, HydraRNA accurately predicted the effect of mutations in CXCL2 3'UTR on RNA stability with a Pearson R of 0.705. Notably, the mutations inside the AU-rich element substantially increased the RNA stability (Fig. 5c), which is consistent with the well-known RNA distablizing effect of the AU-rich element [30].

#### Prediction of RNA polyadenylation site

RNA alternative polyadenylation (APA) is a prevalent regulatory process by which multiple RNA transcripts with distinct 3' ends can be produced from a single gene. To test HydraRNA's  ability  in  the  prediction  of  RNA  polyadenylation  site,  we  used  a  dataset from Seelig's lab [31].  In  that  study,  the  authors  transiently  expressed  a  minigene  followed by different 3'UTR libraries and measured the usage of cleavage sites. After filtering out sequences with low read counts, we used 95% of the remaining data from 9 out of 13 libraries for training, 2% for validation and 3% for testing. Four libraries (including the 1,085 human reference PASs) were held out entirely to test generalization ability.

As shown in Fig. 5d, both HydraRNA and 3'UTRBERT get the same high R 2 score of  0.898,  which  is  higher  than  the  other  methods  in  the  test  dataset.  Furthermore, HydraRNA outperforms all the other methods in the human dataset (Fig. 5e). For the other three held-out datasets, HydraRNA ranked first in the Hspe1 and Whammp2 datasets, while RiNALMo ranked first in the Snhg6 dataset (Additional file 1: Figure S5a). Notably, although fine-tuning the HydraRNA model without pre-training (HydraRNA\_ random) could get high a R 2 score in the test dataset, it got much lower R 2 scores on the four held-out datasets compared to the pre-trained one. These results indicates pretrained  HydraRNA achieved higher prediction performance and better generalization ability in APA prediction task.

To determine which nucleotides of RNA influence the APA process, we applied ISM anaylsis  using  the  fine-tuned  HydraRNA  as  the  scoring  function.  As  shown  in  Fig.  5f and Additional file 1: Figure S5b, the ISM-derived sequence logo not only resembled the canonical polyadenylation signal (AAUAAA motif), but also the downstream U-rich element [32] (Fig. 5g).

#### Prediction of 5'UTR effect on ribosome loading

Besides 3'UTRs, the 5' untranslated region (5' UTR) also plays a critical role in regulating the translation from mRNAs to proteins [33]. The mean ribosome loading (MRL), a widely used measure of mRNA translation efficiency, can be experimentally assessed through ribosome profiling. We evaluated the performance of our proposed model on a  benchmark dataset [34], which was also used in previous studies [6, 15]. This dataset contains both random sequences and sequences from human 5'UTRs, with lengths ranging from 25 to 100 nt. We fine-tuned the HydraRNA on the 76,319 random 5'UTRs. Then we tested and compared HydraRNA with other baselines on 7,600 random and 7,600  human  5'UTRs,  respectively.  HydraRNA  outperformed  all  the  other  methods, with a Pearson R 2 score of 0.91 on the random test set (Fig. 6a) and 0.85 on the human test  set  (Fig.  6b).  Notably,  UTR-LM,  a  specialized  5'UTR  language  model,  performed better than RNA-FM, RNAErnie and Optimus on this task.

More recently, DART (direct analysis of ribosome targeting) has been developed to more directly measure the translation initiation  rate  [17],  which  is  believed  to  be  the rate-limiting step for translation regulation. To evaluate the performance of HydraRNA on this  type  of  data,  we  tested  it  on  a  recently  published  DART  dataset,  where  ribosome recruitment scores were measured for more than 30,000 human 5'UTRs and their mutant variants [17]. After full parameter fine-tuning, HydraRNA achieved a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.779, which was the same as RiNALMo but higher than all the other baseline methods (Fig. 6c). Notably, HydraRNA predicted the effect of various mutations on ribosome recruitment reasonably well, with a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.479 (Fig. 6d). Assuringly, HydraRNA accurately predicted the effect of most mutations with large effect size in the correct direction, indicating the potential use of HydraRNA in identifying the functional mutations from the whole genome or exome sequencing data from patients.

#### Prediction of RNA splice sites

RNA splicing is a pivotal process in eukaryotic gene expression. Accurately identifying splice sites, specifically the donor and acceptor sites that demarcate boundaries between exons and introns, is critical for precise prediction of gene structure. Identifying splice sites can be formulated as a binary sequence-level classification task. We used a widely utilized  dataset  comprising  positive  and  negative  subsets  of  splice-site  sequences  to benchmark HydraRNA. This dataset was constructed by randomly sampling sequences from  the  exon/intron  regions  of  G3PO + genomic  sequences  [36,  37],  which  contains splice  site  sequences  derived  from  a  diverse  collection  of  148  eukaryotic  organisms, including humans. Notably, the test dataset comprises four distinct species that were not included in the training dataset.

We fine-tuned the model, first for donor and then for acceptor splice-site prediction, respectively. We included RNA-FM, RNAErnie, RiNALMo and Spliceator for comparison. As shown in Fig. 6e. HydraRNA outperforms RNA-FM, RNAErnie and Spliceator, but underperforms RiNALMo. The superior performance of RiNALMo is probably due to its much larger model size (650 M), since HydraRNA is comparable or slighter better than RiNALMo-150 M in this task.

To determine which nucleotides of RNA influence the splice site recognition, we again applied  ISM  anaylsis  using  the  fine-tuned  HydraRNA  as  the  scoring  function.  As  an example, the ISM-derived sequence logo not only resembled the canonical dinucleotide GU at the donor site, but also highlighted the AGGUA motif (Fig. 6f). Indeed, mutations at this motif site have been reported and validated to cause RNA splicing alterations [35].

#### Prediction of mRNA translation and stability based on full-length mRNA sequences

Previous massive parallel  reporter  assays  based  screening  usually  used  the  same  coding sequence (CDS), which often encodes fluorescent or luciferase proteins. Although the models trained on these datasets achieved impressive prediction accuracy on heldout data, it has been shown that such models did not predict the translation output of endogenous mRNAs well [38]. The different CDS of endogenous mRNA likely contribute to the discrepancy in prediction performance. Therefore we evaluated the ability of HydraRNA in predicting the translation efficiency of the endogenous full-length mRNA, including 5'UTR, 3'UTR and CDS. This task is expected to be more challenging, especially for those models that were not designed for, thus could not learn the long context information of mRNA.

To benchmark, we collected a dataset where mRNA and protein abundance as well as  their  turnover  were  measured  by  parallel  metabolic  pulse  labeling  and  translation rates were subsequently estimated based on kinetic modeling [19]. For this prediction task, HydraRNA achieved the highest Pearson R 2 . With the full-length mRNA sequence as  input,  HydraRNA  could  explained  45.0%  of  the  variance  in  translation  rate,  while RiNALMo explained 37.6%, and RNAErnie and RNA-FM only explained less then 18% (Fig. 7a). To estimate the relative contributions of 5'UTR, CDS and 3'UTR to translation control, we further trained and tested HydraRNA on 5'UTR, CDS and 3'UTR sequences with  the  same  data-split,  respectively.  Surprisingly,  CDS  alone  could  explained  42.6% of  the  variance in translation rate, which is almost the same as the full-length mRNA seqeunce (Fig. 7a). 5'UTR alone explained 11.6% while 3'UTR alone only explained 4.5% of the variance. The same rank by contribution size (CDS &gt; 5'UTR &gt; 3'UTR) was revealed by other models as well (Fig. 7a). For mRNA half-life prediction, HydraRNA also outperformed RNA-FM and RNAErnie with a Pearson R 2 of  0.334  (Fig.  7b).  The  rank  by contribution size for mRNA half-life from top to bottom is CDS (31.4%), 3'UTR (9.4%) and 5'UTR (2.0%). And again, this pattern was also revealed by RiNALMo, RNA-FM and RNAErnie (Fig. 7b). As a control, in predicting the protein stability, as expected, CDS  explained  much  more  variance  than  either  5'UTR  or  3'UTR  alone.  Interestingly, CDS alone explained better than the full-length mRNA sequences, likely due to the noise attributed by UTR sequences (Fig. 7c). To clarify whether this phenomenon is  unique to this dataset, we examined it in another dataset which was generated by a modified  pulsed-SILAC  approach  in  mouse  immune  bone  marrow-derived  dendritic cells [39]. The prediction based on this dataset also revealed the same trend for translation rate (Fig. 7d) and protein degradation rate (Fig. 7e). Together, these results show that HydraRNA outperforms other RNA language models in long sequence related tasks and intriguingly, CDS contributes the most to mRNA translation rate as well as mRNA stability,  compared to 5'UTR and 3'UTR. This emphasizes the important role of CDS in  regulating  mRNA  fate,  which  needs  to  be  considered  in  optimal  design  of  mRNA therapeutics.

### Discussion

The lengths of mRNA and lncRNA often exceed thousands of nucleotide. Previous RNA foundation models used Transformer architecture as the backbone, which is powerful in relatively short RNA learning, but with limited learning capability in long RNA context. Accordingly, these RNA foundation models were pre-trained on short segments of noncoding RNA sequences, which limited their power in diverse mRNA-related tasks.

The performance of HydraRNA across different downstream tasks varies greatly, from R 2 as high as 0.91 in predicting 5'UTR MRL to as low as 0.13 in estimating protein halflife.  Even  for  the  same  kind  of  task,  such  as  predicting  the  binding  sties  for  different RBPs, the performance varies considerably. This is probably due to at least two reasons. First, the performance of any computational model relies on the quality of training data. Due to the nature of the biological problems as well as the complexity of experimental  setups, some experiments could generate highly reproducible datasets whereas the others could not. Theoretically, the correlation between the biological replicates sets the ceiling for the model performance. Second, all the LLM models learn the sequence features by pre-training. They would not perform well for those tasks which are less determined by the  RNA sequences,  as  exemplified  by  the  poor  performance  in  predicting binding sites for the RBPs with ambiguous binding motifs.

It  has been well recognized that mRNA translation and turnover could be regulated by cis-elements. Most of the known regulatory elements are located in the UTR regions [40-42]. In particular, 5'UTR sequences are considered more important for modulating translation, whereas 3' UTR sequences are more involved in mRNA stability. However, the relative contributions of these different regions in determining the translation and stability of mRNA have not yet been systematically quantified. Using HydraRNA with either full-length mRNA sequences or only the sequences from isolated regions as input, we estimated for the first time the relative contributions of 5'UTR, 3'UTR and CDS in determining mRNA translation and stability. On the one hand, compared to 5' UTR, 3'UTR  sequence  exerts  a  greater  effect  on  mRNA  stability,  but  less  effect  on  mRNA translation, which is largely consistent with current knowledge. On the other hand, surprisingly, we revealed the dominate contributions of the CDS on explaining the variance in  translation  rate  as  well  as  stability  of  endogenous  mRNAs.  We  cannot  completely exclude  the  possibility  that  this  is  a  technical  artifact,  although  the  two  observations argue against this. First, although dominant for mRNA translation, stability as well as protein half-life, the relative contributions of the CDS is quantitatively very different. As a control, the CDS explained much more variance than either UTR region, even marginally more than the the full-length mRNA sequences in predicting protein stability. This is expected given that the stability of protein would be largely determined by its protein sequences, which is solely based on the CDS. Second, the same trend was also found by the other three RNA LLMs, although they overall performed not as well as HydraRNA.

It  should be also noted that there are limitations in this study. First, the HydraRNA model still includes two multi-head attention layers. This prevents HydraRNA from handling  much longer RNA sequences, such as 100 K nucleotide in length. In this study, to  balance  the  computation efficiency  and  full-length  RNA  context,  RNA longer than 4 K nucleotide were truncated in the pre-training step. Fortunately, 88.3% and 99.0% of known mRNAs are shorter than 4 K and 10 K nucleotide, so in most practical cases, 10 K nucleotide would be sufficient. Second, RNA transcripts with high sequence identity were also excluded in the pre-training step. So the valuable evolutionary information encoded in these similar RNA transcripts across different species and isoforms were not fully utilized. Third, the current HydraRNA model is relatively much smaller than the LLMs for protein sequences. Given that multiple mRNA sequences can encode the same protein for any protein sequence, coupled with the existence of non-coding RNAs, the sequence space of RNAs is clearly far larger than that of proteins. While there are endeavors in scaling up the Transformer-based RNA LLMs [43, 44], it would be interesting  to  see  the  extent  of  gain  in  power  when  HydraRNA,  a  non-Transformer-based method, is scaled up in the future study. Finally, in this study, HydraRNA was mainly used  to  predict  the  RNA  sequence  properties.  Generative  models  could  sample  large amount  of  diverse  RNA  sequences  from  the  unexplored  RNA  space.  Integration  of HydraRNA with generative models to efficiently generate and prioritize top RNA candidates with satisfied properties for wet-laboratory validation will be an exciting future direction in the field of RNA biotechnology and medicine.

### Conclusions

In this study, we developed a full-length RNA language model HydraRNA, which was built based on a hybrid architecture of Hydra and MHA. HydraRNA learned meaningful representations of RNA sequences by pre-training on a large corpus of both non-coding and protein-coding RNAs with a masking strategy. We demonstrated that HydraRNA achieved  the  state-of-the-art  performance  across  diverse  RNA-related  tasks.  Furthermore, HydraRNA can accurately predict the effect of mutations and estimate the relative contributions of different mRNA regions to the RNA stability and translation.

Notably, HydraRNA has a total of 84 million parameters. Although being trained with the least GPU resources, it outperforms other RNA LLMs for 8 out of 10 downstream tasks. This emphasizes the importance of model architecture, data processing and training strategy in the biological applications of LLMs. By integrating Hydra modules into its  architecture,  which  scale  linearly  instead  of  quadratically  with  the  length  of  input sequences,  HydraRNA  can  handle  long  full-length  RNAs  efficiently  and  accurately. Taking advantage of this, we pre-trained HydraRNA with both non-coding and coding sequences. These factors together led to improved performance over the other models in predicting mRNA translation and stability based on the full-length mRNA sequences, demonstrating HydraRNA as a powerful method to facilitate the study of RNA regulation as well as the optimal sequence design for mRNA therapeutics.

### Methods

#### HydraRNA architecture and pre-training strategy

We proposed a novel and hybrid architecture for RNA masked language modeling. The model is pre-trained in a self-supervised learning manner via masked token reconstruction. Then it is partially or fully fine-tuned for a variety of downstream prediction tasks. The basic building blocks of HydraRNA include two types: Hydra and MHA. HydraRNA employs a 12-layer architecture and a hidden state dimension of 1024. Each layer contains  a  Hydra  module  except  the  6th  and  12th  layer,  which  contains  a  MHA  module. Hydra is a bidirectional structured state space model (SSM) [45]. Its strong performance in NLP and linear-time computational efficiency attract us to test whether Hydra is competitive in long RNA sequence modeling. Previous work suggest that a hybrid architecture with both SSM layers and attention layers could improve the model quality over that of a pure SSM (e.g., Mamba2) or a Transformer model [45]. Inspired by this result from NLP field, we inserted two MHA layers into the stack of Hydra architecture. FlashAttention [46] was implemented in the MHA to speed up and reduce GPU memory usage.

We conducted several ablation studies to determine the optimal architecture choice and  pre-training  strategy.  We  first  did  an  ablation  study  on  the  architecture  choices (Additional file 1: Table S6) and found that the hybrid HydraRNA performed marginally better than pure Hydra or Transformer model for the RNA secondary structure prediction  task  and  MRL  prediciton  task.  Then  we  did  the  second  ablation  study  on  the length of span mask using the hybrid HydraRNA architecture (Additional file 1: Table S7). For RNA secondary structure prediction task and MRL prediciton task, pre-training with span length of 1 outperformed that with span length of 4 or 8, which is inconsistent with our initial  expectation.  Lastly,  we  conducted  another  ablation  study  by  changing the length of RNA sequence in the pre-training (1 K vs 4 K) using the hybrid HydraRNA model with span mask length equaling to 1 (Additional file 1: Table S8). Training with longer RNA sequences indeed improved the model performance in general. This is more evident in the mRNA translation rate and half-life prediction task, in which long mRNA sequences were input. For mRNA translation rate prediction, the Pearson correlation coefficient  R  increased  from  0.63  to  0.67  as  the  pre-training  length  increasing  from  1 to 4 K. For mRNA half-life prediction, the Pearson correlation coefficient R increased from 0.52  to  0.58.  Based  on  these  results,  we  chose  the  hybrid  architecture  that  pretrained with span mask length of 1 nt and RNA input length of 4 K as the final version of HydraRNA.

For downstream task, we use the average embedding across the tokens of the sequence to  represent the entire sequence. For fine-tuning the downstream tasks, unless otherwise specified, we freeze the pre-training model and only fine-tuning the MLP predictor which takes one dimensional embedding from RNA language model as input. The MLP predictor has only one hidden layer with 128 nodes. This configuration leads us to better quantify the quality of embedding from different RNA language models. More details about fine-training are provided in Additional file 1: Table S9 and Additional file 1: Table S10.

For  RNA  secondary  structure  prediction,  we  used  the  same  prediction  head  as RiNALMo [12, 47]. Specifically, we used a 2 layers 2D residual neural network (ResNet) [48] as the prediction head, which is attached to the pre-trained HydraRNA model. An operation  of  outer  concatenation  is  applied  to  the  HydraRNA  embedding  to  connect with  the  2D  ResNet.  We  also  used  the  same  metrics  as  RiNALMo  to  assess  the  performance of  secondary  structure  prediction.  For  a  nucleotide  pair  (i,  j)  where  i  and  j represent nucleotide indices in the RNA sequence, (i ± 1,j) and (i, j ± 1) pairs are also considered correct predictions.

#### Random span masked language model

The  masking  strategy  used  in  RNA-FM,  RNABERT  [10],  RNA-MSM  [11],  LAMAR [49],  3UTRBERT and UTR-LM was originally from BERT [50], which is essentially at base-level.  Specifically,  15%  of  the  nucleobases  are  randomly  selected  within  an  RNA sequence.  Among  the  selected  tokens,  80%  are  masked,  10%  are  preserved  without any alterations, and the remaining 10% are replaced with other nucleobases. Based on ERNIE,  RNAErnie  introduced  a  motif-aware  multilevel  masking  strategy  to  pre-train the model. It maintained a proportion of 1:1:1 across the three different masking levels (base-level, subsequence-level and motif-level), with the training algorithm randomly selecting  one  strategy  for  each  training  session.  Although  this  motif-aware  masking strategy  could  incorporate  biological  priors  into  the  training  process,  it  may  complicate the training process and biased to the known motif databases. We also noticed that another RNA foundation model, ERNIE-RNA [51] also used ERNIE masking strategy during pre-training.

We  proposed  a  novel  unified  masking  strategy  for  masked  language  modelling, which is called random span masking. In this strategy, multiple span regions (contiguous nucleobase sequences) that covering 15% of the nucleobases are randomly selected. Among  the  selected  nucleobases,  80%  are  masked  with &lt; mask &gt; token,  10%  are  preserved without any alterations, and the remaining 10% are replaced with other nucleobases. This unified masking strategy generalize the classical BERT-like masking strategy to implicitly capture the deeper biological information underling the consecutive region in the whole RNA transcript context, while it also avoid explicitly using biased motif priors. If the mean length of the span masks is set to be 1, it degenerates to the conventional BERT-like masking strategy.

#### In Silico Mutagenesis (ISM) analysis for contribution scores

To determine which nucleotides in a target sequence make contributions to HydraRNA's  prediction  regarding  specific  task,  we  utilized  an  in  silico  mutagenesis  (ISM) approach [52]. This method calculates contribution score E ij for each position i within the sequence. Briefly, this approach first generates single nucleotide saturation mutation for each position of a sequence. Then a scoring function, HydraRNA predictor in this case, is used to calculated a single scalar value P ij for each mutant or P ref for reference nucleotide, which resulting in an effect matrix of size L × 4, where L is the length of the reference sequence. The score of reference nucleotide P ref is subtracted from each entry in the matrix to get the relative effect matrix. To normalize the effect, the mean of the three variant scores is subtracted from all four entries for each position in the matrix, respectively.

#### Data for pre-training

RNA  sequences  from  RNAcentral  database  were  first  filtered  to  include  only  RNAs with length between 18nt and 20,000nt, resulting in 32.5 million RNAs. Redundant and highly  similar  sequences  were  removed  by  using  mmseqs  [53]  with  sequence  identity cutoff at 0.8. 14.4 million RNAs were retained. 58.1 million RNA sequences from NCBI/ RefSeq database were downloaded, which spread across 1,277 species. The transcripts of  different  species  were  downloaded  from    h  t  t  p  s  :  /  /  f  t  p  .  n  c  b  i  .  n  l  m  .  n  i  h  .  g o  v  / g  e n  o  m  e  s /  a l  l /  a n  n  o  t  a  t  i  o  n  \_  r  e  l  e  a  s  e  s  /, which contains the products of the NCBI Eukaryotic Genome   A  n n  o  t  a  t  i  o  n Pipeline. The data in this website are organized by taxonomy ID. Redundant and highly similar RefSeq sequences were also removed by using mmseqs with sequence identity  cutoff  at  0.8,  which  results  in  28.07  million  RNAs.  Then  these  42.48  million filtered  sequences from RNAcentral and NCBI were combined and further filtered by mmseq with sequence identity cutoff at 0.5. Here, we did not manually select or discard the transcripts but just used the representative transcripts produced by mmseqs clustering. Finally 28.09 million RNA sequences (including 13.9 million RNAcentral sequences and 14.2 NCBI sequences) were used for pre-training, covering about 51 billion nucleotide tokens. The mean and median length of these RNA is 1811 and 1119 nt, respectively, with 89.7% shorter than 4096 nt. To speed up training, sequences longer than 4096 nt were split into segments of length at most 4096 nt. Each nucleotide is a single token. All 'U' in the sequences were replaced with 'T' . This leads to a vocabulary of standard IUPAC  nucleotide  codes  (A,T,G,C,N,R,Y,K,M,W,S,D,H,V,B).  Special  tokens  that  commonly used in MLM, such as &lt; s &gt;, &lt; pad &gt;, &lt;/s &gt;, &lt; unk &gt; and &lt; mask &gt;, were added to the vocabulary. Special token &lt; s &gt; and &lt;/s &gt; were added at the start and end of each sequence during pre-training, respectively.

For  the  RNA  secondary  structure  prediction  task,  we  performed  a  second  stage  of pre-training using a curated dataset of non-coding RNAs. Specifically, sequences from bpRNA-1m and archiveII dataset were combined as queries to extract similar sequences from  RNAcentral  dataset  with  sequence  identity  cutoff  of  0.5.  Then  the  retrieved sequences  were  combined  with  the  queries  as  well  as  the  Rfam  dataset.  Next,  highly similar sequences were removed by using mmseqs with sequence identity cutoff at 0.9, which resulted in finally 2.3 million sequences.

#### Hyperparameters and configurations

Fairseq [55] framework was used for pretraining the HydraRNA model. In the pretraining stage, dynamic batch size was used. In each batch, max tokens per device was set to 40 K. We utilized the Adam [56] optimizer, which was regulated by a cosine learning-rate schedule involving anneal warm-up and decay. The initial learning rate was set at  1  ×  10 -7 ,  with  a  maximum learning rate of 2 × 10 -4 .  The  learning-rate scheduler was designed  to  warm  up  during  the  first  10,000  steps.  Gradients  update  frequency  were set to 10. The model was pre-trained on eight Nvidia 4090D 24 GB graphics processing units for around 92 h.
