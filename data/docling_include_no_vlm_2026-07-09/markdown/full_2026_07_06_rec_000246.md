## Cross -species modeling of plant genomes at single -nucleotide resolution using a pretrained DNA language model

Interpreting function and fitness effects in diverse plant genomes requires transferable models. Language models (LMs) pretrained on large -scale biological sequences can capture evolutionary conservation and offer cross -species prediction better than supervised models through fine -tuning limited labeled data. We introduce PlantCaduceus, a plant DNA LM that learns evolutionary conservation patterns in 16 angiosperm genomes by modeling both DNA strands simultaneously. When fine -tuned on a small set of labeled Arabidopsis data for tasks such as predicting translation initiation/termination sites and splice donor/acceptor sites, PlantCaduceus demonstrated remarkable transferability to maize, which diverged 160 Mya. The model outperformed the best existing DNA language model by 1.45 -fold in maize splice donor prediction and 7.23 -fold in maize translation initiation site prediction. In variant effect prediction, PlantCaduceus showed performance comparative to state -of -the -art protein LMs. Mutations predicted to be deleterious by PlantCaduceus showed threefold lower average minor allele frequencies compared to those identified by multiple sequence alignment -based methods. Additionally, PlantCaduceus successfully identifies well -known causal variants in both Arabidopsis and maize. Overall, PlantCaduceus is a versatile DNA LM that can accelerate plant genomics and crop breeding applications.

Over 1,000 plant genomes have been published during the past 20 y, and this number will continue to increase significantly in the coming decades (  1  -  3  ). Understanding the functional elements and fitness effects of these genomes at both transcriptional and translational levels is crucial for advancing plant genomics and crop breeding. Plant genomes exhibit remarkable diversity in size, composition, and complexity, with dramatic variation even among closely related species (  4  ). Extensive genomic resources have been generated for model plants, such as Arabidopsis (  5  ), rice (  6  ), and maize (  6  ), significantly advancing plant genomics research. However, generating analogous genomic resources experimentally for all plant genomes is time consuming, costly, and impractical. This highlights the need for developing cross -species models capable of capturing evolutionary conservation across diverse plant species.

Supervised deep learning (DL) sequence models are successful in understanding DNA sequence functions such as transcription initiation (  7  ), alternative splicing (  8  ), and gene expression (  9  ). However, supervised DL models typically require large -scale labeled data, such as ENCODE -scale datasets (  10  ,   11  ), to achieve robust performance. Such extensive labeled data are often scarce in plant genomics. Moreover, training supervised models on model species, such as Arabidopsis , presents challenges when transferring to other plant species. Language models (LMs) were originally developed to understand text in nature language processing, by predicting a missing word or the next word in sentences without requiring explicit labels-known as self -supervised pretraining. When applied to genomic data, this approach can recognize biological sequence patterns and learn from vast amounts of unlabeled sequence data across multiple species. These pretrained models can be utilized in two ways: through fine -tuning, where the model parameters are further optimized on a smaller set of labeled data for specific tasks, or through zero -shot prediction, where the model directly estimates the impact of sequence variations without any task -specific training. For example, protein LMs, pretrained on diverse protein sequences spanning the evolutionary  tree,  have  shown  successful  applications:  predicting  atomic -level  protein structure  through  fine -tuning  (  12  ),  identifying  disease -causing  variants  (  13  )  through zero -shot prediction, and engineering protein design (  14  ). These models provide valuable tools for understanding protein function and facilitating innovative solutions in biotechnology and medicine (  15  ).

### Significance

Cross -species modeling of gene annotation and fitness remains a significant challenge in plant genomics, particularly in nonmodel organisms, due to the remarkable diversity and complexity of plant genomes. To address this, we introduce PlantCaduceus, a DNA language model leveraging a unique architecture and pretrained on diverse angiosperm genomes. The model demonstrates exceptional cross -species transferability, exemplified by its ability to generalize from Arabidopsis to maize despite 160 My of evolutionary divergence. This capability underscores PlantCaduceus's ability to capture conserved sequence patterns across plant species, enabling accurate predictions for diverse sequence -based tasks, from genome annotation to complex fitness analyses. The model's versatility and cross -species applicability enable it as a transformative tool for advancing both fundamental plant genomics research and crop improvement.

Unlike protein LMs that are limited to coding regions, DNA LMs enable a genome -wide perspective, offering deeper insights into gene regulation and evolution. Protein LMs have shown success in identifying pathogenic missense mutations in human genetics (  13  ,   16  ), but increasing evidence shows that mutations in noncoding regions, including both intergenic and intronic regions, contribute significantly to both agronomic traits (  17  ) and human diseases (  18  ,   19  ). Additionally, training multispecies DNA LMs can capture evolutionary conservation in both coding and noncoding regions, enhancing our understanding of genetic variation across different species.

However, DNA LMs face significant challenges compared to protein LMs. In these models, DNA sequences are typically broken into individual units called tokens, which can be single nucleotides or k -mers. First, eukaryotes, including plants (  20  ), contain varied percentages of repetitive sequences, complicating the pretraining task. Given that LMs are pretrained to either predict the next token or tokens are masked arbitrarily in a sequence, repetitive sequences that are easier to predict but do not necessarily improve downstream applications can reduce overall model quality (  21  ). Additionally, noncoding regions are less conserved than coding  regions,  leading  to  potential  biases  if  entire  genomes  are included in pretraining. Finally, unlike protein sequences, modeling  double -stranded  DNA  requires  consideration  of  reverse complementary base pairing (  22  ) and a bidirectional model that accounts for both upstream and downstream sequences.

We evaluated PlantCaduceus through three complementary analyses. First, we tested cross -species generalization using four gene annotation tasks that transfer models trained on Arabidopsis to other crop species. Second, we assessed evolutionary constraint prediction to evaluate the model's ability to identify conserved genomic elements. Third, we explored zero -shot variant effect prediction to identify putative deleterious mutations, validating its practical utility through in silico mutagenesis, enrichment in rare alleles, and known causal variants in Arabidopsis and maize. Together, these analyses establish PlantCaduceus as a versatile foundation model for advancing plant genomics research and crop improvement.

### Results

To tackle these challenges, we introduce PlantCaduceus, a DNA LM pretrained  on  16  angiosperm  genomes  (  Fig. 1 A )  using  the Caduceus architecture (  23  )-a DNA -specific model that builds on the Mamba (  24  ) and supports reverse complement (RC) equivariance.  Inspired  by  GPN  (Genomic  Pre -trained  Network)  (  21  ), which has demonstrated powerful predictive ability for whole -genome  variant effects in Arabidopsis , PlantCaduceus employs single -nucleotide tokenization, enabling precise modeling at the base -pair -resolution across diverse plant genomes. Similarly, following GPN's data processing strategy, we generated an unbiased genomic  dataset  for  pretraining  by  down -sampling  noncoding regions and down -weighting repetitive sequences. In contrast, other publicly available DNA LMs, such as AgroNT (  25  ) and Nucleotide Transformer (  26  ), use entire genomes for pretraining, potentially introducing biases toward certain genomes and repetitive sequences. Additionally, both models use nonoverlapping kmer tokenizers that disrupt the genome into arbitrary segments. Furthermore, unlike the unidirectional HyenaDNA (  27  ) or Evo (  28  ), PlantCaduceus PlantCaduceus:  A  Pretrained  DNA  Language  Model  with  16 Angiosperm Genomes. Caduceus (23) is a DNA LM architecture that builds upon the recently introduced Mamba (24) architecture, a  selective  state  space  sequence  model  that  has  demonstrated competitive performance to transformers (29) in various natural language  processing  tasks,  with  more  efficient  scaling  for  longer range sequences. Unlike Mamba, Caduceus is specifically designed for DNA sequences, taking into account the bidirectional nature of  DNA  and  introducing  RC  equivariance.  Here,  we  trained PlantCaduceus using the Caduceus architecture on 16 angiosperm genomes (Fig. 1 A and B and Dataset S1), spanning 160 My of evolutionary  history  ( Methods ).  PlantCaduceus  takes  512  base pair (bp) windows of input sequences, tokenizing them into single nucleotides, and is pretrained using a masked language modeling objective  (Fig.  1 B and Methods ).  Following  GPN's (21) strategy, we addressed the substantial variation in genome sizes and the high proportion of repetitive sequences in these genomes by emphasizing nonrepetitive  sequences  through  down -weighting  and  down -sampling repetitive sequences during pretraining ( Methods ).  This approach reduces overfitting to repetitive sequences, which are easier to predict in masked language modeling tasks, enabling the model to focus on learning patterns in coding and other nonrepetitive regions that are more functionally relevant. T o scale Caduceus, we trained a series of PlantCaduceus models with varying depths (20, 24, 28, and 32 Caduceus blocks) (Fig. 1 B ),  resulting  in  models  ranging from 20 million to 225 million parameters (Table 1). The training and validation  losses  for  each  model  are  detailed  in  Dataset  S2.

After pretraining, we conducted a preliminary assessment to verify the model's learning capabilities. T aking the sorghum genome as an example, we employed Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) (30) to visualize the embeddings generated by the four pretrained PlantCaduceus models. By segmenting the genome into 512 bp windows, we observed distinct clustering in the UMAP visualization, corresponding to different genomic regions (Fig. 1 C ). Due to the high proportion of repetitive intergenic sequences in the sorghum genome, the embedding spaces appeared dispersed in the UMAP visualization (Fig. 1 D and SI Appendix , Fig. S1). Even without any supervision, PlantCaduceus was able to differentiate between coding and noncoding regions with high clarity.

Improving the Accuracy and Cross -Species Transferability  of Modeling Transcription and Translation through Fine -Tuning PlantCaduceus. Transcription and  translation are two  key processes in the central dogma of molecular biology, and the precise identification of junction sites during these processes is essential for  comprehensive  gene  annotation. To  assess  PlantCaduceus's performance in modeling these processes, we designed four gene annotation tasks: predicting the translation initiation site (TIS), translation termination site (TTS), and splice donor and acceptor sites ( Methods ). We employed two complementary feature -based approaches to evaluate PlantCaduceus embeddings: 1) training an XGBoost (31) classifier, which can capture complex nonlinear patterns in the embedding space, and 2) training a simple linear layer, which tests whether the learned representations are linearly separable. Both approaches were applied on frozen embeddings extracted from the last hidden state of PlantCaduceus, where the models' parameters remained unchanged during analysis (Fig. 2 A ). Compared to full fine -tuning, these feature -based approaches allow us to assess the quality of learned representations while minimizing the  usage  of  computational  resources.  Previous  LMs  focus  on evaluation within the same species (25-27, 32, 33). However, given that the DNA LM is pretrained on multiple species, we wanted  to  investigate  whether  a  model  trained  with  limited labeled data in Arabidopsis could be used for prediction in other species. Therefore, we trained and validated XGBoost and linear models in Arabidopsis and tested their performance on both species included ( Oryza sativa and Sorghum bicolor )  and  not  included ( Gossypium hirsutum , Glycine max, and Zea mays ) in the pretraining (Fig. 2 B and Dataset S3). We benchmarked the performance of PlantCaduceus  against  three  DNA  LMs:  GPN  (21),  AgroNT (25), and Nucleotide Transformer (26), as well as a supervised hybrid model comprising a convolutional neural network (CNN) and a long short -term memory (LSTM) network (34), hereafter referred to as CNN+LSTM. For DNA LMs, we used the same feature -based approaches as PlantCaduceus (Fig. 2 A ) to train both XGBoost and linear models using embeddings extracted from the last hidden state of each DNA LM (Fig. 2 C ). The CNN+LSTM model was trained from scratch in a supervised manner.

Given the real prediction scenario of junction sites in the genome, where true sites are much fewer than false sites, we generated highly imbalanced test datasets with positive observations (true sites) being far fewer than negative observations (Dataset S3  ). We used the area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) as our evaluation metric, as it better captures model performance on imbalanced datasets compared to traditional Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (AUROC),  which  can  overestimate  performance  when  negative observations are major classes. AUPRC ranges from 0 to 1, where 1 represents perfect prediction and a random classifier would achieve an AUPRC equal to the positive class proportion (typically &lt;0.01 in our testing datasets, Dataset S3  ). First, focusing on within -species evaluation on the Arabidopsis hold -out test set, both XGBoost and linear models showed  strong performance across four tasks ( SI Appendix ,  Fig. S2  ).  XGBoost models trained with PlantCaduceus\_ l32 embeddings consistently outperformed the linear models across four tasks (average AUPRC: 0.949 vs. 0.929, respectively). This performance gap was consistent across different PlantCaduceus model variants: PlantCaduceus\_l28 (0.950 vs. 0.909), PlantCaduceus\_l24 (0.949 vs. 0.904), PlantCaduceus\_l20 (0.949 vs. 0.875), and GPN (0.944  vs.  0.926)  ( SI Appendix ,  Fig. S2  and  Dataset S4  ).  The XGBoost models trained with PlantCaduceus\_l32 frozen embeddings showed the best performance across the four tasks of predicting TIS (  Fig. 2 C ),  TTS (  Fig. 2 D ), splice donor site (  Fig. 2 E ), and splice acceptor site (  Fig. 2 F ). Other DNA LMs like GPN and AgroNT also performed well either with an XGBoost model or a simple linear layer,  particularly  in  predicting  splice  donor  and  acceptor  sites ( SI Appendix , Fig. S2 and   Fig. 2  ). Additionally, for splice donor and acceptor site prediction, even the supervised CNN+LSTM model achieved near perfect AUPRC values, indicating that within -species prediction is a relatively straightforward task.

We then assessed the cross -species generalization ability of these models by testing them on O. sativa and S. bicolor , which were included in pretraining, as well as G. hirsutum , G. max , and Z. mays , which were not (  Figs. 1 A and   2 B ). With XGBoost classification head, when tested across these five species, all models except PlantCaduceus exhibited a significant drop in average AUPRC, decreasing from 0.789 in Arabidopsis thaliana to 0.237 in these species  (  Fig. 2 C -F ).  For  instance,  transferring  the  supervised CNN+LSTM model to Z.  mays -which diverged 160 Myaresulted in an AUPRC drop from 0.713 to nearly zero for the TIS task. This significant drop was expected, as the supervised model had never seen sequences from these species, making cross -species generalization  challenging.  Although  GPN  maintained  decent cross -species predictions, it still showed significant performance drops,  with  the  average  AUPRC  decreasing  from  0.944  in A. thaliana to 0.509 in other species (  Fig. 2 C -F and Dataset S4  ). As expected, the nonplant DNA NT -v2 model performed poorly on these tasks due to the significant divergence between plant and animal genomes. Even though AgroNT was pretrained on 48 plant genomes, its performance fell short of expectations in cross -species evaluations. In contrast, PlantCaduceus consistently maintained high AUPRC values across all species, with an average AUPRC of 0.764, regardless of whether the species were included in pretraining, demonstrating its superior generalization ability across diverse plant species (  Fig. 2 C -F and Dataset S4  ).

When comparing the two feature -based approaches (XGBoost vs. a simple linear layer) in cross -species scenarios, we observed that the performance gap between XGBoost and a simple linear layer became more pronounced. Taking maize as an example, the average AUPRC of  PlantCaduceus\_l32  across  four  tasks  decreased  from  0.668 (XGBoost) to 0.509 (a simple linear layer), and GPN also showed a similar  trend  with  AUPRC  dropping  from  0.327  to  0.206 ( SI Appendix , Fig. S2 and Dataset S4  ). Both AgroNT and NT -v2 showed consistently poor performance regardless of the downstream approach, with AUPRC scores remaining low for both AgroNT (from  0.106  to  0.100)  and  NT -v2  (from  0.031  to  0.027).  This widening performance gap between XGBoost and linear layer suggests that the rich representations captured in the embeddings require more complex modeling to fully utilize their predictive power, particularly in cross -species scenarios where the relationship between sequence  features  and  functional  sites  becomes  more  complex. Nevertheless, even with the simpler linear layer, PlantCaduceus\_l32 still outperformed other models across all tasks in cross -species evaluation, demonstrating the robustness of its pretrained representations regardless of the downstream architecture.

GPN, as the second -best DNA LM, was not pretrained on any of the five testing species. T o ensure a fairer comparison with GPN and to understand which components contribute to PlantCaduceus's superior performance on these cross -species tasks, we conducted two ablation tests to estimate the effect of model architecture. We used the XGBoost classifier which had demonstrated consistently superior performance over the linear layer. First, we retrained a custom GPN model ( Methods ) using the same datasets as PlantCaduceus and scaling it to 130 million parameters, on the same order of magnitude as PlantCaduceus. We observed that including more genomes in the pretraining and scaling model size significantly improved GPN's cross -species predictability ( SI Appendix , Fig. S3 and Dataset S4  ), especially for TIS and TTS tasks. These results indicate that when more genomes are included during pretraining, the embeddings learned by DNA LMs are more general across species. However, PlantCaduceus still exhibited the best performance, indicating that its architecture is superior to that of GPN. Moreover, even with a parameter size of 20 million-6.5 times smaller than the custom 130  million  GPN  and  3.25×  times  smaller  than  the  original GPN-PlantCaduceus still outperformed all models in predicting TIS,  TTS,  splice  donor,  and  splice  acceptor  sites.  Second,  we explored the impact of RC sequences. Unlike PlantCaduceus, which is  inherently  RC -equivariant,  to  consider  the  RC  sequences,  we averaged predictions from both forward and RC sequences for GPN. This averaging provided modest improvements in performance: The average AUPRC across all cross -species tests and four tasks increased from 0.509 to 0.550 for the original GPN and from 0.622 to 0.649 for our custom GPN ( SI Appendix , Fig. S4  ). Despite these improvements, PlantCaduceus maintained its superior performance across all  tasks. These results demonstrate that PlantCaduceus not only captures broader evolutionary conservation features but also is more parameter -efficient than other DNA LMs.

Given that AgroNT was pretrained on more plant genomes (48 species) and with more parameters (one billion), we conducted an additional ablation study using Low -Rank Adaptation (LoRA)  fine -tuning  (  35  )  to  further  explore  its  performance. While pretraining a new AgroNT model with our 16 genomes was computationally infeasible due to its size, LoRA offered a resource -efficient alternative which allows partial model updating. Our initial evaluation by training XGBoost on top of frozen embeddings from AgroNT showed relatively poor performance (average AUPRC of 0.189 across all tests;   Fig. 2 and Dataset S4  ). However, LoRA fine -tuning improved AgroNT's performance significantly (average AUPRC increased to 0.524; SI Appendix , Fig. S5  ). These results indicate the frozen embeddings from last hidden state of AgroNT are not sufficiently informative to predict single -bp  function. Nevertheless, PlantCaduceus  still demonstrated superior performance (average AUPRC of 0.764; SI Appendix , Fig. S5  ) using only frozen embeddings. This comparison further validates PlantCaduceus's strong representational power, as it achieves better results with a simpler feature -based approach compared to models using more sophisticated fine -tuning  techniques,  even  when  constrained  by  computational resources.

Cross -Species Evolutionary Constraint Prediction through Fine -Tuning PlantCaduceus. Genome -wide association studies (GWAS) have  identified  thousands  of  variants  associated  with  complex traits  (36).  However, identifying causal variants is complicated by linkage disequilibrium (LD), as significant SNPs identified by GWAS are usually in LD with causal variants (37). In contrast, evolutionary constraint, as evidenced by DNA conservation across species, can identify potential causal mutations by revealing their fitness effects (38). Given that PlantCaduceus is pretrained on 16 angiosperm genomes, we hypothesize that it can be fine -tuned to predict evolutionary constraint using DNA sequences alone. Maize and sorghum are both members of the Andropogoneae clade, descended from a common ancestor approximately 18 Mya (39). To generate evolutionary constraints in the sorghum genome, we aligned 34 genomes from the Andropogoneae clade, with rice as  an  outgroup  (Dataset  S5),  to  the Sorghum bicolor reference genome ( SI Appendix , Fig. S6). We focused on the 277 million sites that had high -quality coverage (present in at least 34 out of 35 species) and classified these sites as either conserved or neutral based on their identity score: Sites with an identity score ≥34 were labeled as conserved, while sites with an identity score &lt;15 were labeled as neutral (Fig. 3 A ).

Before exploring supervised learning approaches, we first evaluated the  models'  zero -shot  ability  to  predict  evolutionary  constraint through their reference allele prediction probabilities. Since these models are pretrained to predict masked nucleotides, we hypothesized that their prediction probabilities for reference alleles would correlate with evolutionary conservation. Our analysis showed that PlantCaduceus showed modest performance in predicting evolutionary constraint with an AUROC of 0.690 and AUPRC of 0.707 in this zero -shot setting ( SI Appendix , Fig. S7  ). Similarly, both GPN and Custom GPN showed comparable performance. In contrast, AgroNT, despite being pretrained on 48 plant species, showed no predictive power on evolutionary conservation with zero -shot setting. These results suggest that PlantCaduceus can capture evolutionary constraints during pretraining, even without explicit supervision.

While zero -shot results were promising, we sought to further improve performance in predicting evolutionary constraint. We trained an XGBoost classifier on top of embeddings extracted from the last hidden state of the pretrained models. We used sites from chromosomes 1 to 9 to train an XGBoost model and evaluated it on sorghum chromosome 10. We benchmarked this task against GPN, AgroNT, NT -v2, and the supervised CNN+LSTM model. On the validation set, PlantCaduceus achieved the best performance, with an AUROC of 0.896 (  Fig. 3 B ) and an AUPRC of 0.876 (  Fig. 3 C ). In comparison, the best AUROC and AUPRC for other DNA LMs were 0.790 and 0.778, respectively. The custom  GPN  model  performed  comparably  to  PlantCaduceus, achieving an AUROC of 0.833 and AUPRC of 0.814 ( SI Appendix , Fig. S8  ). As expected, the supervised CNN+LSTM model performed the worst, with an AUROC of 0.638, as it had only seen sequences from sorghum (  Fig. 3 B and C ). These results demonstrate  that  PlantCaduceus  can  effectively  predict  evolutionary constraint without multiple sequence alignment.

To gain deeper insights into the model's predictive capabilities, we stratified our analysis by genomic regions and codon degeneracy.  Interestingly,  contrary  to  the  expectation  that  conserved protein -coding regions might be easier to predict, PlantCaduceus achieved its highest performance in noncoding regions (AUROC =  0.904)  ( SI Appendix ,  Fig. S9  ).  This  superior  performance  in noncoding regions likely reflects  the  model's  ability  to  capture regulatory motifs and other functional elements that are under strong evolutionary constraint. Within coding regions, we further examined the impact of codon degeneracy on prediction accuracy. Performance varied across different levels of degeneracy, with nondegenerate sites showing AUROC of 0.888, while twofold, threefold,  and fourfold degenerate sites achieved AUROC values of 0.891, 0.873, and 0.866, respectively. These results demonstrate the models' consistent performance regardless of genomic context and highlight PlantCaduceus's capability to identify functional elements under evolutionary constraint, particularly in noncoding regions where regulatory motifs play crucial roles.

Finally, to evaluate the cross -species predictive power of the model trained on sorghum evolutionary constraint data, we generated an analogous testing dataset for maize ( Methods ). Remarkably, when our  PlantCaduceus  model,  originally  trained  on  sorghum,  was applied to the maize dataset, it demonstrated strong cross -species prediction performance, achieving an AUROC of 0.829 (  Fig. 3 D ) and an AUPRC of 0.797 (  Fig. 3 E ). In contrast, all other models consistently showed poor performance on maize (  Fig. 3 D and E ). We also  evaluated  the  performance  of  our  custom  GPN  model which was trained on the same dataset as PlantCaduceus. While the custom  GPN  model  showed  improved  performance  with  an AUROC of 0.760 and an AUPRC of 0.729, PlantCaduceus, with only 20 million parameters, outperformed both the original GPN and the custom GPN models ( SI Appendix , Fig. S8  ). For AgroNT , even with LoRA fine -tuning, PlantCaduceus still achieved better performance  ( SI Appendix ,  Fig. S10  ).  These  results  highlight  the robustness and effectiveness of our DNA LM for cross -species predictions  of  evolutionary  constraints  using  only  sequence  data  as input. The transferability of our model across different species within the  Andropogoneae  clade  suggests  that  it  captures  fundamental evolutionary patterns and can be readily adapted to predict evolutionary constraint in related species with limited additional training data.

Zero -Shot Variant Effect Prediction Identifies Deleterious Mutations  in  Different  Species. The  pretraining  objective  of PlantCaduceus is to predict masked nucleotides based on sequence context; if a pretrained multispecies DNA LM can accurately predict masked tokens, it suggests that similar sequence patterns, conserved across different species, were frequently observed during pretraining. We  hypothesize  that  the  predicted  likelihood  of  the  reference allele vs. the alternate allele can identify deleterious mutations, as mutations in conserved regions across species are likely deleterious (40-43). To test this hypothesis, we used the commonly used zero -shot strategy (13) to estimate mutation effects, where the model assesses variant effects without any mutation -specific training data by calculating the difference in log -likelihood between reference and alternate alleles, which we refer to as 'zero -shot score' (Fig. 4 A ). This approach is similar to that used in GPN (21), where they referred to it as 'GPN score.' For each mutation, a more negative value indicates higher conservation. We generated 1.1 million sites from sorghum chromosome 8 (included in pretraining) and 1.3 million sites from maize chromosome 8 (not included) through in silico mutagenesis of SNPs. We then calculated zero -shot scores for  these  mutations  to  assess  how  PlantCaduceus  performs  on both seen and unseen genomes. As expected, mutations in highly conserved functional regions-such as stop -gained, splice acceptor, splice  donor,  and  start -lost  sites-exhibited  the  most  negative zero -shot  scores,  underscoring  their  potential  deleterious  effects (Fig.  4 B and SI  Appendix ,  Fig.  S11 A ).  Missense  mutations  also showed notably negative zero -shot scores. In contrast, intergenic regions and introns displayed scores closer to zero, indicating lower evolutionary  constraint  and  a  reduced  likelihood  of  deleterious effects (Fig. 4 B and SI Appendix , Fig. S11 A ). However, we observed that a subset of mutations in repetitive regions still received very low zero -shot scores, suggesting that repetitive regions may be too easy  for  the  model  to  predict  the  masked  tokens.  This  is  likely because  the  repetitive  nature  of  these  sequences  makes  them highly predictable from their context, regardless of their functional importance. Overall, the zero -shot score of PlantCaduceus aligns with established concepts of deleteriousness (44, 45).

Besides  in  silico  mutagenesis,  we  also  evaluated  whether zero -shot score can be used to identify deleterious mutations in natural populations. Deleterious mutations tend to have lower frequencies within a population due to selective constraints (  38  ), we therefore used minor allele frequency (MAF) to quantify the deleteriousness  of  mutations  predicted  by  different  methods. Despite the potential for low MAF in neutral/beneficial alleles, we believe this approach provides useful signals for assessing deleterious mutations because truly deleterious variants are likely to be under strong negative selection and thus maintained at low frequencies in populations (  38  ). We benchmarked PlantCaduceus against two evolutionary -informed methods, phyloP and phastCons (  46  ), GPN (  21  ), as well as AgroNT (  25  ). Both phyloP and phastCons assess evolutionary constraint using multiple sequence alignments and phylogenetic models ( Methods ). While both methods assign higher scores to conserved regions, phyloP measures conservation or acceleration at individual sites, and phastCons identifies conserved elements by analyzing patterns across adjacent sites. We analyzed 4.6 million SNPs in the sorghum TERRA population (  42  )  and  9.4  million  SNPs  from maize Hapmap 3.2.1 population (  47  ) and observed that most of the SNPs had neutral zero -shot score, while there was still a heavy tail with negative zero -shot scores (  Fig. 4 C and SI Appendix , Fig. S11 B ). By defining the top 0.1% as the most deleterious mutations, we observed a significant enrichment in coding regions, as reflected by the high odds  ratios  in  both  sorghum  (40.70)  and  maize  (42.42)  with P -values  less  than  2.2e -16  ( SI Appendix ,  Fig. S12  ).  We  next categorized  SNPs  into  three  additional  percentiles  based  on zero -shot scores: the top 50%, 10%, and 1% most deleterious mutations. We observed that all models had a decreasing average MAF of SNPs in higher percentiles for missense, nonsynonymous, and noncoding SNPs in both sorghum ( SI Appendix ,  Fig. S13  ) and maize (  Fig. 4 D ). Notably, the putative deleterious mutations identified by PlantCaduceus exhibited the lowest average MAF across all percentiles, outperforming GPN and significantly surpassing AgroNT, phyloP , and phastCons (  Fig. 4 D and SI Appendix , Fig. S13  ). When we further analyzed noncoding regions by separating them into UTR, promoter, intron, and intergenic regions, we observed similar trends, although GPN showed slightly better performance in promoter regions ( SI Appendix , Fig. S14  ). Given the  success  of  protein  LMs  in  predicting  deleterious  missense mutations (  13  ,   16  ), we also incorporated ESM (Evolutionary Scale Modeling)  (  12  )  as  a  benchmark.  For  missense  mutations,  we found  that  PlantCaduceus  matches  the  performance  of  the state -of -the -art protein LM ESM (  12  ). At the top 50%, 10%, and 1% percentiles, the putative deleterious mutations identified by PlantCaduceus showed even lower average MAF than those identified by ESM in sorghum ( SI Appendix , Fig. S13  ).

However, since GPN is only pretrained with genomes from eight Brassicales species and specifically designed for mutation effect prediction in Arabidopsis , we further validated PlantCaduceus by analyzing  over  10  million  mutations  from  the Arabidopsis 1001 Genomes Project (  48  ). Being pretrained with a broader range of evolutionarily distant genomes, PlantCaduceus effectively captured deleterious mutations in Arabidopsis and showed slightly lower average  MAF  than  GPN  ( SI Appendix , Fig. S15  ). For missense mutations, PlantCaduceus consistently matched the performance of the state -of -the -art protein language model ESM and was nearly competitive with GPN for noncoding mutations.

We further verified whether PlantCaduceus could pinpoint known causal deleterious mutations. We collected 19 candidate phenotype -impacting and potentially deleterious mutations identified in homozygous EMS mutants in Arabidopsis (  49  ). Among these, 15  mutations  were  ranked  in  the  top  1%  or  top  10%  by  the PlantCaduceus zero -shot score (  T able 2  ), highlighting the zero -shot score of PlantCaduceus can be used for pinpointing causal deleterious mutations. In contrast, while 15 mutations were ranked in the top 10% by GPN score, none were ranked in the top 10% by AgroNT. Additionally, PlantCaduceus successfully identified a well -studied causal sweet corn mutation, which derives its characteristic sweetness from the W578R mutation at the sugary1 ( Su1 ) locus (  50  ).  This mutation disrupts starch metabolism, leading to the accumulation of phytoglycogen, which lowers seedling vigor and reduces germination, ultimately decreasing fitness (  51  ). Although GWAS revealed numerous significantly sweet -trait -associated variants on chromosome 4, identifying the exact causal mutations is challenging due to high LD in this low recombination region (  Fig. 5 A ). By integrating zero -shot scores from PlantCaduceus with GWAS data (  Fig. 5 B and C ), we successfully identified the W578R mutation as the sole causal variant in this QTL region (  Fig. 5 D ).  Notably, neither GPN nor AgroNT was able to identify this causal variant (Dataset S7  ). T aken together, these results demonstrate that the PlantCaduceus model effectively pinpoints known causal deleterious mutations, highlighting its potential as a powerful tool for identifying causal variants underlying important agronomic traits.

### Discussion

Functional  annotation  of  plant  genomes  is  crucial  for  plant genomics and crop breeding but remains limited by the lack of functional genomic data and accurate predictive models. Here, we introduced PlantCaduceus, a multispecies plant DNA LM pretrained on a curated set of 16 evolutionarily distant angiosperm genomes, enabling cross -species prediction of functional annotations with limited data. PlantCaduceus leverages Mamba (  24  ) and Caduceus (  23  ) architectures to support bidirectional, RC equivariant sequence modeling. We demonstrated the superior cross -species  performance  of  PlantCaduceus  on  five  tasks  involving transcription, translation, and evolutionary constraint modeling. These results highlight the potential of PlantCaduceus to serve as a foundational model for comprehensively understanding plant genomes.

PlantCaduceus has the potential  to  accurately  annotate  any newly sequenced angiosperm genomes. Unlike supervised deep learning  models  that  easily  overfit  on  limited  labeled  data, PlantCaduceus demonstrates robust cross -species performance in modeling transcription, translation, and evolutionary constraints, even for species not included in pretraining (  Fig. 2 and SI Appendix , Fig. S2  ). This indicates that through self -supervised pretraining on  large -scale  genomic  datasets,  PlantCaduceus  has  captured broad evolutionary conservation and DNA sequence grammar.

The cross -species prediction ability of PlantCaduceus can significantly accelerate plant genomics research, aiding initiatives such as  the  1000  Plant  Genomes  Project  (  1  )  by  providing  accurate annotations and insights across diverse plant species.

T raining with phylogenetically diverse species is crucial for DNA LMs  with  robust  cross -species  generalization  ability.  T o  further demonstrate this, we trained PlantCaduceus\_l20 on two phylogenetically  close  species  sets  (8  species  from  Poaceae  family  and  8 species from Brassicales order). We observed decreased performance compared to the full 16 -species set, with average AUPRC dropping from 0.727 (full 16 species) to 0.642 (Poaceae -trained) and 0.605 (Brassicales -trained) ( SI Appendix , Fig. S16  ). This result suggests that the phylogenetic diversity of training species substantially contributes to the model's ability to capture fundamental sequence patterns shared across plants. Although AgroNT (  25  ) was trained on a more diverse set of plant species, its performance was limited by two factors: the use of nonoverlapping k -mer tokenization and pretraining on unfiltered full  genomes that include nonconstrained regions. These limitations may have reduced its ability to capture sequence patterns and evolutionary conservation, resulting in lower performance in single -site prediction tasks. In contrast, PlantCaduceus achieves superior cross -species performance while maintaining computational efficiency.

PlantCaduceus offers a more effective approach to estimate deleterious  mutations  without  relying  on  multiple  sequence alignments (MSAs). Deleterious mutations are considered as the genetic basis of heterosis, where hybrids yield more due to the  suppression  of  deleterious  recessives  from  one  parent  by dominant alleles from the other (  52  ). For missense mutation, multiple computational approaches have been developed and well -benchmarked to evaluate deleteriousness (  53  ). Historically, deleterious mutations have been estimated by generating MSAs (  38  ,    54  ,    55  )  and using evolutionary methods such as phyloP and phastCons (  46  ). However, the prevalence of transposable elements  and  polyploidy  in  plant  genomes  complicates  the genome -wide MSA generation (  56  ,   57  ). PlantCaduceus overcomes these challenges by using a masked language modeling strategy to learn conservation from large -scale genomic datasets of diverse species. Promisingly, the deleterious mutations prioritized  by  PlantCaduceus  with  the  zero -shot  strategy  showed threefold rare allele enrichment compared to phyloP and phastCons,  and  our  approach  is  competitive  with  state -of -the -art protein LM for missense mutations. Furthermore, PlantCaduceus enables pinpointing causal variants from significant GWAS signals, which are usually confounded by LD. These results suggest that PlantCaduceus can be utilized as a powerful tool in crop breeding, enhancing genome -wide deleterious mutation identification,  optimizing  parental  line  selection,  and  promoting hybrid vigor (  52  ).

While SSM -based architectures (  23  ,   24  ,   27  ,   28  ) like PlantCaduceus are capable of handling longer sequence contexts efficiently, we chose a 512 bp context window to balance model capability with practical computational constraints. Although longer context windows might benefit certain tasks such as gene expression prediction or long -range regulatory element identification, our focus on fundamental gene annotation tasks and variant effect prediction primarily relies on local sequence context. During the exploratory phase, we pretrained a compact version of PlantCaduceus (4M parameters) and evaluated its reference allele prediction accuracy using simulated variants through in silico mutagenesis with variant masking (  Fig. 4 B and Methods ).  We found that model capacity, rather than context window size, was the primary factor affecting performance in variant effect prediction tasks (Dataset S8  ). Moreover, the 512 bp context length enables PlantCaduceus to run efficiently on widely available GPUs like the NVIDIA 3090, making it more accessible to the broader research community.

In future work, we plan to incorporate additional plant genomes from diverse lineages, such as gymnosperms, to capture broader evolutionary  conservation.  Additionally,  we  plan  to  pretrain PlantCaduceus with longer context windows, enabling it to capture long -range DNA interactions and better handle tasks benefiting from long -range cis -effects, such as allele -specific expression, chromatin state prediction, and chromatin interaction mapping. Furthermore, it would also be interesting to explore how to better tokenize repetitive sequences in plant genomes. We envision that these approaches will allow us to push the boundaries of what PlantCaduceus can achieve, establishing it as an even more powerful  and  versatile  foundation  model  for  advancing  genomic research and facilitating crop improvement.

### Methods

Pretraining  Dataset. The  pretraining  dataset  comprises  16  genomes  from two distinct clades: eight genomes from the family Poaceae and eight genomes from the order Brassicales (Dataset S1). To visualize their relatedness, we subset these taxa from a large phylogeny of seed plants (58). The Poaceae species displayed substantial variation in genome size and repetitive sequence content, with the hexaploid wheat genome exhibiting a size of 15 Gbp. For each Poaceae genome, except for Tripsacum,  we  obtained  the  genome  and  corresponding genome  annotation  and  repeat -masked  annotation  from  the  Joint  Genome Institute.  For  the Tripsacum genome, the genome FASTA and annotation files were  downloaded  from  MaizeGDB  (https://maizegdb.org/genome/assembly/ Td -FL\_9056069\_6 -DRAFT -PanAnd -1.0),  and  the  EDTA  tool  (59)  was  used  to identify repetitive sequences within the genome. Based on the repeat -masked annotation, each genome was softmasked with bedtools (60) and subsequently divided into genomic windows of 512 bp with a step size of 256 bp. Each window was assigned to a unique class based on the genome annotation, and all coding sequence regions were selected for pretraining. The remaining genomic regions were then down -sampled to ensure an equal number of CDS regions and noncoding regions. It is important to note that for the hexaploid wheat genome, only subgenome A was utilized to avoid species bias. The Brassicales genomes datasets were acquired from the GPN (21) Hugging Face repository (https://huggingface. co/datasets/songlab/genomes -brassicales -balanced -v1). The validation and testing datasets were randomly selected and constituted 5% of the total dataset.

Caduceus Model Architecture and Pretraining. We use the recently proposed Caduceus architecture (23), which is tailored to DNA sequence modeling. Caduceus is based on the Mamba architecture (24), a model which scales to long sequences more  efficiently  than  attention -based  methods  while  maintaining  accuracy. Mamba stems from the class of structured state space models (SSMs) (61). SSMs are sequence operators that are instances of linear recurrent networks (RNN). While previous iterations of SSMs (62) relied on weights that were input independent to define the sequence operator for gains in computational efficiency during training, Mamba utilizes data dependency, which they term as 'selection mechanisms' to set the weights of the SSM operator, which increases the expressivity of these models. These selection mechanisms allow Mamba to overcome the limitations of previous SSMs and rival Transformers (29) on sequence modeling tasks across domains. For efficient computation, Mamba uses optimized algorithms that enable fast processing on modern computing hardware. Mamba blocks are composed of projection matrices that increase the dimensionality of the inputs, followed by element -wise nonlinearities before being processed by the selective SSM module. Finally, sequences are passed through a gated multilayer perceptron, where gating mechanisms regulate the flow of information and selectively learn important sequence features before being projected back down to the original dimensionality.

Of note, most of the parameters in a Mamba block come from the projection matrices at the beginning and end of each block, a fact which can be leveraged for efficient bidirectional extensions. Specifically, to account for upstream and downstream gene interactions, Caduceus employs a bidirectional Mamba implementation where a sequence is processed once from left -to -right and again from right -to -left, with the outputs of both forward passes summed together, a common technique for rendering RNNs bidirectional. To avoid doubling the parameter count of the model, the linear projections discussed above are shared between the forward and reverse passes of the model, greatly reducing the memory footprint of the bidirectional model.

Finally, Caduceus is designed to consider the RC symmetry of DNA sequences. This  is  accomplished  by  encoding  RC  equivariance  as  an  inductive  bias: The Caduceus language model commutes with the RC operation. Combining these three design decisions, Caduceus has shown promising results when applied to human genome modeling (23). The implementation of RC equivariance in Caduceus entails doubling the number of channels for intermediate representations. At a high level, half the channels are used to encode information about a sequence and the other half are used to encode information about its RC. For downstream tasks in which we trained a classifier on top of learned embeddings, the labels were invariant to the RC operation, since both DNA strands carry the same label. To account for this, we therefore split embeddings of the Caduceus model along the channel dimension and averaged. This ensures that both a sequence and its RC will have the same final embedding, i.e., we render the embeddings invariant to the RC operation as well.

For the pretraining of PlantCaduceus, each model was trained for 240,000 steps using a Decoupled AdamW optimizer (63) with the global batch size of 2,048. The model scaling strategy, including hidden size, hyperparameter scaling practices from the original Mamba paper (24). Training time scaled with model size: PlantCaduceus\_l32 (225M parameters) required approximately 25 d on 8 H100 GPUs, while PlantCaduceus\_l20 (20M parameters) completed training in about 2 d using the same hardware configuration. The learning rate is 2E -4 with a cosine decay scheduler, and 6% of the training duration was dedicated to warm up. The learning rate decayed to 4E -6 by the end of training. The default BERT (64) masking recipe was used with a masking probability of 0.15. For each masked token: i) there is an 80% probability it will be replaced by a special token ([MASK]), ii) a 10% probability it will be replaced by a random token, and iii) a 10% probability it will remain unchanged.

Unless otherwise specified, all models were trained using a sequence length of 512 base pairs. A weight decay of 1E -5 was applied throughout the training process.

TIS,  TTS,  Splice  Donor  and  Acceptor  Training,  Validation,  and  Testing Dataset Generation. To generate high -quality training datasets for TIS, TTS, splice donor sites, and splice acceptor sites, we used the well -annotated model plant genome of Arabidopsis with Araport 11 annotation (65). To accurately reflect the inherent imbalance in junction sites prediction, all annotated junction sites were considered as positive observations, while a randomly selected subset of sites (5%) that matched specific appropriate motifs (e.g., ATG for TIS, UAA, UAG, and UGA for TTS, GT for donor splice sites, and AG for acceptor splice sites) were used as negative observations. For each task, the pretrained model weights were frozen, and XGBoost models (n\_estimators = 1,000, max\_depth = 6, learning\_rate = 0.1) were trained using embeddings extracted from the last hidden state of the pretrained model. To ensure robust model training and validation, chromosome 5 was used for hold -out testing, and the rest of the Arabidopsis genome was used for training.

Given the relatively poor annotation in other species compared to Arabidopsis , we used the BUSCO tool (66) to identify 3,236 orthologous genes specific to monocotyledons in O. sativa , S. bicolor, and Z. mays and 2,326 orthologous genes specific to eudicotyledons in G. hirsutum and G. max to generate reliable testing datasets in other species. BUSCO -identified genes are evolutionarily conserved, providing a robust benchmark set for cross -species performance evaluation. Specifically, BUSCO was utilized to scan the annotated protein isoforms, and only complete BUSCO genes were considered as true positives. For those BUSCO genes with multiple transcripts, we selected the longest transcript to avoid sequence redundancy in the testing dataset. Subsequently, BUSCO gene/transcript -supported junction sites were used as positive examples for their respective tasks. To generate negative sites, all sites within the BUSCO genes that matched appropriate motifs (e.g., ATG for TIS, TAA, TAG, and TGA for TTS, GT for donor splice sites, and AG for acceptor splice sites) but were not part of any annotated gene models were used as true sites. Sites belonging to alternate transcripts were excluded to avoid ambiguity. Furthermore, to expand the negative observations and capture a broader range of nonjunction sites, we included sites in the intergenic regions flanking the BUSCO genes that matched the appropriate junction motifs. By incorporating both genic and intergenic sites from the BUSCO gene set as negatives, we created an extremely imbalanced testing dataset to reflect the real -world scenario of junction site prediction (Dataset S3).

Evolutionary  Constraint  Estimation. The  evolutionary  constraint  was  estimated primarily within the Andropogoneae tribe, a large clade of grasses comprising approximately 1,200 species that descended from a common ancestor approximately 18 Mya (39). In this analysis, 34 genomes from Andropogoneae and the rice genome were used to estimate the evolutionary constraint (67). Due to the substantial transposable element (TE) content in these genomes, AnchorWave, a sensitive genome -to -genome alignment tool (56), was used to align the 35 genomes to the sorghum reference genome using the parameters ' -R 1 -Q 1.' Following the alignments to the sorghum reference genome, we counted the number of identities, SNPs, and coverages ( SI Appendix , Fig. S6). Then the fine -tuned labels were generated based on per -site identity and coverage (Fig. 3 A ).  Conserved sites were defined as having an identity greater than 34, while neutral sites were defined as having an identity of 15 or less and coverage of at least 34. Sites with low coverage were excluded due to their potential ambiguity. Given the large size of the training dataset, only 5% of conserved sites were randomly selected for training, and an equivalent proportion of neutral sites was also randomly selected. Sites from chromosomes 1 to 9 were used for training, while those from chromosome 10 were used for validation. To generate the testing dataset in maize, the maize reference genome B73 was used. Then, using the same approach, genome -wide evolutionary constraints were generated by aligning 35 genomes to the maize reference genome with AnchorWave, using the parameters ' -R 1 -Q 2,' except for Tripsacum clades. For Tripsacum and maize, which share the most recent whole genome duplication, we used ' -R 1 -Q 1.'

In Silico Mutagenesis. All potential mutations in the genic regions and 1 kb flanking  regions  of  maize  and  sorghum  chromosome 8 were generated and annotated using the Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) local API (44), with the upstream/downstream parameter set to 1,000 to classify variants as either upstream or downstream. For intergenic variants, we randomly sampled 100,000 SNPs from the intergenic regions across chromosome 8 to ensure more even coverage of the entire chromosome. For each variant type, we randomly sampled 100,000 mutations and calculated zero -shot scores.

Benchmarking  Variant  Effect  Prediction  in  Natural  Populations. We benchmark PlantCaduceus against two evolutionary -informed methods, phyloP and phastCons (46), GPN (21), as well as AgroNT (25). For DNA LMs, we calculated a zero -shot score as the log -likelihood difference between reference and alternate alleles at the masked position for each SNP . For evolutionary methods, we calculated conservation scores using multiple sequence alignments of 34 Andropogoneae genomes constructed by Cactus (68), which is a multiple genome alignment tool that uses a progressive alignment strategy. Using PHAST (46), we computed phyloP scores with the SPH scoring method ( -  -method SPH) in CONACC mode ( -  -mode CONACC), and phastCons (46) scores based on a neutral model derived from fourfold degenerate coding sites.

We applied these methods to variants from the Sorghum TERRA population (42), maize Hapmap 3.2.1 population (47), and Arabidopsis 1001 accessions (48). For comparison across methods, we categorized variants into percentile: the top 50%, 10%, 1%, and 0.1%. Since more negative zero -shot scores from PlantCaduceus, GPN and AgroNT indicate higher predicted deleteriousness, we ranked variants from the most negative to most positive scores, while for phyloP and phastCons, we ranked from highest to lowest scores as higher values indicate greater conservation. At each percentile, we calculated the average minor allele frequency to evaluate models' performance.

Genome -Wide Association Study for Sweet Phenotype. T o perform a GWAS for the sweet phenotype, we used a subset of genotypes from the Hapmap 3.2.1 population (47), where sweet phenotype data are available (69). This subset consists of 272 diverse inbred lines with recorded sweet phenotype data. We coded starchy corn as 0 and sweet corn as 1, with 266 entries in the first category and 6 in the second. To map the sweet phenotype, we utilized a model specifically designed to account for population structure: y = X β 1 + 5 global PCs + e. The methods for GWAS followed those outlined in ref. 70. Briefly, the five global principal components (PCs) were derived from 66,527 SNPs across 3,545 diverse inbred lines, and the SNPs from 272 inbred lines were then rotated to such PCs. The selected SNPs had no missing data across three maize populations, ensuring effective control for population structure and kinship. This approach also reduced computational time compared to mixed linear models while maintaining consistent trait mapping across populations.

GPN, Custom GPN, AgroNT, and NT -v2 Baselines. To comprehensively evaluate our foundation model's performance, four foundation models including GPN (21) (https://huggingface.co/songlab/gpn -brassicales), custom GPN, AgroNT (25) (https://huggingface.co/InstaDeepAI/agro -nucleotide -transformer -1b),  and  NT -v2  (26)  (https://huggingface.co/InstaDeepAI/nucleotide -transformer -v2 -500m -multi -species) were used as baselines for various tasks. GPN is a convolutional DNA LM pretrained on eight genomes of Arabidopsis and seven other species from the Brassicales order. However, since GPN was pretrained with only eight evolutionarily close species and has only 65M parameters and most of the tasks in this paper focus on evaluation in crops, we retrained a custom GPN with 130M parameters using 50 convolutional layers and the same dataset as PlantCaduceus for a fair comparison. We increased the number of training steps for the custom GPN model to match PlantCaduceus's training duration (240 K steps), ensuring comparable training exposure. The other hyperparameters were kept identical to the original GPN (Dataset S6). In contrast, AgroNT (25) is a transformer -based (29) language model with 1 billion parameters, pretrained on 48 plant genomes. NT -v2 (26), is a nonplant multispecies transformer model pretrained on 850 genomes excluding plant species. These models employ different tokenization strategies: GPN uses single -nucleotide tokenization, while AgroNT and NT -v2 use 6 -mer tokenization.

Supervised CNN+LSTM Baseline. To  establish  a  fair  comparison  between our DNA LM and existing supervised models, which are primarily trained on human data, we used the DanQ model architecture (34) as the supervised baseline. DanQ is a hybrid convolutional and recurrent neural network specifically designed for predicting the function of DNA sequences. It has demonstrated impressive performance in predicting chromatin states in plant species, making it a suitable choice for our comparative analysis (71). For each task, the CNN+LSTM model was trained from scratch using one -hot encoded DNA sequences  as  input.  The  Adam  optimizer  with  a  learning  rate  of  0.01  was employed for model optimization. The batch size was set to 2,048. Training was limited to 200 epochs, with early stopping if validation performance did not improve for 20 consecutive epochs.
