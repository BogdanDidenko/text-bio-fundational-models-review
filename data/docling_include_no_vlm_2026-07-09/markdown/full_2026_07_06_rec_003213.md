## ChIP-GPT: a managed large language model for robust data extraction from biomedical database records

Olivier Cinquin

<!-- image -->

Corresponding author. Olivier Cinquin, Tel.: 949-824-0000; Fax: (949) 824-4709; E-mail: olivier.cinquin@uci.edu

### Abstract

Increasing volumes of biomedical data are amassing in databases. Large-scale analyses of these data have wide-ranging applications in biology and medicine. Such analyses require tools to characterize and process entries at scale. However, existing tools, mainly centered on extracting predefined fields, often fail to comprehensively process database entries or correct evident errors-a task humans can easily perform. These tools also lack the ability to reason like domain experts, hindering their robustness and analytical depth. Recent advances with large language models (LLMs) provide a fundamentally new way to query databases. But while a tool such as ChatGPT is adept at answering questions about manually input records, challenges arise when scaling up this process. First, interactions with the LLM need to be automated. Second, limitations on input length may require a record pruning or summarization pre-processing step. Third, to behave reliably as desired, the LLM needs either well-designed, short, 'few-shot' examples, or fine-tuning based on a larger set of well-curated examples. Here, we report ChIP-GPT, based on fine-tuning of the generative pre-trained transformer (GPT) model Llama and on a program prompting the model iteratively and handling its generation of answer text. This model is designed to extract metadata from the Sequence Read Archive, emphasizing the identification of chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) targets and cell lines. When trained with 100 examples, ChIP-GPT demonstrates 90-94% accuracy. Notably, it can seamlessly extract data from records with typos or absent field labels. Our proposed method is easily adaptable to customized questions and different databases.

Keywords : large language model (LLM); generative pre-trained transformer (GPT); natural language processing; biomedical databases; error-tolerant data mining

### INTRODUCTION

Biomedical databases provide an investigative tool whose power increases as more and more data are accumulating. But to fully tap this potential on a large scale, there is a need for data cleaning andextraction tools that robustly handle the disparity of database structures, that can correct typographical errors or higher-level errors or inconsistencies and that are capable of nuanced understanding and logical reasoning.

Recent advances with large language models (LLMs) provide a fundamentally new way to interact with databases. ChatGPT, for example, can readily reason about a record manually pasted into its interface and answer questions about it and can handle variations in the record that would throw off traditional, stringbased database extraction tools. Yet significant challenges remain in applying an LLM at scale on a database-e.g. identifying suitable pre-trained LLMs to serve as a starting point, optimizing LLM and fine-tuning for record analysis, and developing pipelines to interface LLMs with databases. It is also not clear what performance and failure modes one should expect, especially given fast developments in the LLMarea.

Although generative pre-trained transformer (GPT) models [1] were proposed to be able to perform a broad range of tasks in zero [2] or few [3] shot settings, there have been differing views as to whether GPT models are best applied to the biomedical field with specific pre-training on a biomedical corpus, fine-tuning or just few-shot examples [4-7]. We devised a prompt intended to work both in a zero- or few-shot setting or in a fine-tuning setting. We tested that prompt with ChatGPT (whose versions evolved as the present work was underway; the tests reported here were performed with version 4, May 24), which overall proved to perform well even in the absence of fine-tuning, although variability in the structure of the responses would pose challenges in automated parsing of the output. For the fine-tuning approach, we chose a generalist GPT model with high reported performance on a number of tasks: Meta's LLaMA model [8]; we assayed three different model sizes: 7B parameters, 11B and 33B. Although science was not the main focus of LLaMA training, a fraction of the large training corpus did include scientific literature [9]. Focusing on a popular pre-trained model with an open-source implementation and available weights makes it easier to leverage development efforts in areas other than biology and facilitates investigation of model properties.

Thesystem we introduce here,ChIP-GPT,is tailored to the automated analysis of records from the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) [10]. ChIP-GPT automatically identifies experimental treatments that were applied to SRA samples prior to DNA sequencing and, where applicable, extracts the official human gene name corresponding to the experimental chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) targets. This kind of task poses a problem that is both of high practical use and well suited to investigate the unique capabilities of LLM-based approaches.

to be of broad utility. SRA metadata are well structured, and a number of powerful tools already exist to retrieve and manipulate it (e.g. [10, 11]). Yet there is considerable variability across entries in the way that metadata is structured. For example, the metadata of some entries contains a field specifying a 'chip target', others an 'antibody target' or yet others an 'ip-target'. Many records do not explicitly specify a target at all, which must be inferred from other fields, and sometimes even just from an abbreviation found in the sample title. A variety of typographical errors are, in addition, apparent (e.g. 'chipandtibody', 'antiboy'), as well as errors in antibody catalog references. Accordingly, substantial efforts have already been undertaken to create an independent database that provides a mapping of SRA sample IDs to ChIP target [12, 13]. These efforts rely, in part, on manual curation. This manual curation would gain from supplementation with LLM automation and makes for a useful reference point to assess LLM performance.

One interesting challenge in identifying ChIP targets is that many experiments rely not only on an antibody raised against the native protein of interest but also on expression of a chimera fused with a tag that serves as the antibody target (e.g. HA, Myc, V5 tags). For many records that do not specify an explicit target but somehow state, explicitly or implicitly, the antibody used (e.g. anti-HA), identifying the ChIP target actually entails identifying the tag targeted by the antibody used for a particular sample, as well as the protein expressed as a tag fusion. It is crucial to avoid confusion with genetic manipulations, such as gene knockdown, which are independent of the ChIP target. This adds another level of sophistication in required LLM inferences.

The system we report here, ChIP-GPT, relies on an iterative LLM prompting approach that accommodates prompt length limitations that remain a major shortcoming of common LLMs and that helps elicit chain-of-thinking to improve response accuracy. ChIPGPT, whose structure is outlined in Figure 1, consists of (1) an LLM fine-tuned for two tasks: summarizing formatted SRA metadata records (by extracting relevant sentences) and answering a prespecified list of questions on the summarized records and (2) a Python script that constructs LLM prompts, directs LLM answer text generation and collates answers to the list of questions. ChIP-GPT achieves an accuracy of 90-94% for ChIP target and cell line identification, two tasks we chose because they are of broad interest for large-scale analysis of SRA records. This level of performance is likely comparable to the accuracy achieved through human review, considering the arduous nature of the task caused by its repetitiveness. This not only offers a practical tool for ChIP-seq data analysis but also demonstrates a versatile approach that can be customized for various other applications without modifying ChIP-GPT internals as such, but adapting the contents of the records or the questions that are posed to the model on the records.

### METHODS

#### Choice of pre-trained language model

We narrowed our choice of language models to GPTs because of the relative ease with which their output format can be specified and because of the remarkable performance they have displayed over very different tasks [3]. Among those, we chose Meta's LLaMA model [8], which was recently trained on a large corpus including scientific literature [9], was shown to be readily and efficiently fine-tunable [14, 15] and has open source implementations. We picked the 33 billion parameter version (trained on 1.4 trillion tokens), the largest version that was convenient for the 80 GB VRAM of the A100 GPUs at our disposal, as well as the two smaller

13 billion parameter and 7 billion parameter versions, and used the Hugging Face Transformers library v4.28 [16, 17] along with its parameter-efficient fine-tuning library [18] implementing lowrank adaptation [19], following the setup in Wang [15] and reusing some of its code.

#### Data preparation for LLM processing

The information for each database entry of interest (corresponding to an SRA 'experiment', i.e. a record with an identifier of the form [S|E|D]RX[0-9] + ) was formatted into plain text with a fixed format grouping close together fields expected to contribute the same kind of information (e.g. antibody specifics; Table S1). Study-level information was also retrieved, in order to integrate the study abstract, design, protocol, etc., to the output text. For records that had a matching entry in the GEO database [20], which received more curation and whose structure is more uniform across samples, GEO entries were merged with SRA entries. XML, MiniML or text format metadata were downloaded from SRA and GEO using the Entrez direct utilities [11]. Relevant fields were identified based on the XML structure of the data.

#### Iterative LLM prompting

In preliminary trials, we found chain-of-thought prompting [21] to improve LLM outputs. We thus designed a series of questions for the LLM to answer, ordered such that the LLM would explain its reasoning and cite supporting facts in the record data before providing the concisely worded output to be automatically extracted for downstream uses (Table 1). The LLM prompt includes a prefix introducing a domain-specific expert named 'Barb' who answers questions accurately (Table 2). We found it best to ask the questions in sequence, rather than giving a predefined list of questions in one go. For each question, we defined a list of relevant previous questions and included the model's own answers to those questions in the prompt. In addition to minimizing the chance of the LLM skipping over questions, this provided an opportunity to give detailed instructions for key questions that did not need to be repeated when the model was 'reminded' of its previous answers-thus saving on content length.

#### Prompt summarization

For some records, the prompt length exceeded the model's limit of 2048 tokens. In addition, even for ChatGPT, which allows for longer prompts,especially with its more recent model updates, we informally observed that increasing prompt length could lead to sudden degradation of question-answering performance. We thus implemented a summarization step that we applied to prompts whose length was above 2048 tokens or that did not leave enough tokens for generation of an answer of typical length (we chose the value of 250 tokens, applicable to the questions asking for detailed rationales). The summarization introduces an expert named 'Bob' and defines the kinds of information he wants to keep or discard (Table 2).

We initially attempted to summarize entries by giving them to ChatGPT, either in full or as individual, short paragraphs, along with a prompt instructing which kind of information to keep (e.g. antibody or ChIP target specifics) and which to discard (focusing largely on ChIP protocol details, such as cell fixation and DNA library preparation). However, we found it difficult to get ChatGPT to produce a summary that consistently adhered to these nuanced instructions. We next prompted ChatGPT to process the sentences one by one and to (1) repeat the sentence; (2) give rationales for answering six questions whose answers classify the sort of information included in the sentence; and (3) give its final Yes/No formatted answers to each of the questions. We left the selection of relevant sentences to a downstream, stringbased analysis that parsed the Yes/No answers for each question and labeled those labeled as potentially carrying information of interest. While this approach appeared to improve performance, we did not converge on a prompt that consistently led to correct sentence selection across records and thus decided to follow a fine-tuning approach. For simplicity, we fine-tuned a single model with the 'Barb' and 'Bob' prompts.

#### Fine-tuning

The LLaMA model weights were loaded as 16-bit floats, an end-ofstream (EOS) token was added and rotary positional embeddings updated following Wang [15],and training was performed in 16-bit mixed precision on an A100 GPU with 80 GB VRAM.We trained the model sequentially for summarization and question answering, on sets further described below of 28 and 100 examples, respectively, using LoRa parameters rank=8, alpha=1.0, dropout=0.5 and learning rate 1E-4 or 4E-4 and weight decay 0.05 for the Transformers library's AdamW optimizer.

#### Generation

We found it useful to generate multiple prompt completions for each question and select the completion with lowest perplexity as the best answer. Memory limitations made it difficult to use standard generation techniques such as, e.g. diverse beam search [22]. We thus used a custom algorithm generating tokens as follows. Whenever a token's probability was below a threshold p\_thresh (with temperature 0.5), an alternative completion branch was initiated using the token of second-highest probability. The number of concurrent completion branches was restricted to 10. If that number was reached, each branch was completed using the 'generate' method of the Llama model, with sampling and temperature 0.5, top\_p=0.92, top\_k=12. The initial value of p\_thresh was set to 0.7 and was incremented by 0.1 up to a ceiling of 0.9, but only so long as fewer than five branches had been generated. Generation terminated early if any completed branch (meeting the stopping condition defined hereafter) possessed a perplexity less than or equal to 0.0021 or when all its tokens had probabilities of at least 0.9. The stopping condition was generation of an EOS token or an end-of-answer sequence used in training examples, or reaching the maximum desired answer length.

#### Training and withheld datasets

We chose SRA entries based on their existence in ChIP-atlas, the sample source being human and availability of genome peak files; a few extra entries were added based on relevance to an independent project that will be reported elsewhere. We do not expect this particular choice of dataset to strongly bias performance data reported here.

#### Circumventing weaknesses

We observed ChIP-GPT to be affected by low-frequency 'hallucinations' [23] at the summarization stage, whereby sentences would appear in the output that had a topic similar to bona fide input sentences, but that did not appear as such in the input. Sentences appearing in the summary output but not in the input were thus discarded.

We also observed a detrimental impact of extraneous punctuation in the input record and of the presence of multiple fields with the same name but with different contents (this occurred in particular for 'Sample name' because a record can have multiple potential sources for that name). We thus merged the contents of fields with the same name and stripped extraneous punctuation in the record included in the prompt.

Finally, we observed a low-frequency tendency of the model to generate numbers of unclear origin as the answer to some questions-in particular the ChIP target question. We thus suppressed the generation of leading digits in the answers to that question, by setting the probability of the corresponding tokens to 0 if they appeared as the highest probability token at the beginning of generation.

#### Performance evaluation

Evaluation of ChIP-GPT answer accuracy was based on agreement with a human expert examining the same record. Specifically, we reviewed the identified cell line, ChIP target and associated gene name for each of 50 withheld entries. ChIP-GPT sometimes outputs explanations or comments between brackets, which are useful for human review, may conceivably contribute to answer accuracy and can easily be discarded for automated, downstream processing. Contents between brackets, or following an open bracket that did not close due to truncation, were therefore ignored when assessing answer correctness. Similarly, when the answer started with 'N/A', the answer was considered to be 'N/A' irrespective of the following explanations. These rules match the expected, simple post-processing of the answers before they are used in downstream tasks. Given that many genes are known under multiple aliases and given that the set of official gene names is constantly evolving, gene names were marked as correct even if they did not match the most current official human gene name nomenclature (additionally, some variants of gene names are provided that indicate a mutation carried by the gene, a piece of information that may prove useful and that it is best not to penalize the model for preserving). Finally, if the ChIPGPT summarization step failed to make the size of the record sufficiently small for the subsequent question-answering step, for example, because the summarization step marked too many record sentences as containing relevant information,the resulting missing answers were scored as incorrect.

### RESULTS

#### Formulation of the LLM prompts

We found SRA entries to vary considerably in the ease with which ChIP targets and treatments of interest can be identified and in the ways they are indicated. This is particularly striking for ChIP target identification. Some entries include a very explicit field such as 'chip target' or variation thereof. Others have a field such as 'antibody' whose contents can differ greatly across records (e.g. catalog number, name of the antibody, name of the target, etc.) and, in the case of tagged proteins, may require substantial inference work to identify the target of interest (e.g. in the case of an anti-GFP antibody, one must identify the protein to which GFP is fused). Yet others contain no explicit field identifying the target. For these, one may instead infer the ChIP target from the study abstract or protocol in combination with the sample name,whichsometimes additionally requires a comparison of the sample name to the names of other samples in the same studywith the aim of identifying and leveraging a pattern in the sample names. A notable challenge in the analysis of sample names is that abbreviations are commonly used and may not explicitly distinguish between a cell line identifier, drugs or treatments applied, the name of the ChIP target, and names of genes manipulated as part of the experiment. Compounding these difficulties of a structural nature, some samples contain typos in the names of the fields (e.g. 'chipandtibody', 'antiboy') or in the field contents (e.g. in antibody catalog numbers).

To maximize chances of retrieving the information of interest, weconstructed an LLM prompt that included all information from the SRA entry that was not explicitly identified as being irrelevant based on field name (such as submitting organization, lab name and date of submission; Table S2). We grouped fields whose name indicated direct relevance to ChIP target, cell line, tissue type or treatment (Table S1) and included a study abstract and protocol when they were available, as well as the names of other samples in the same study to help LLM ad hoc parsing of sample name. We included 20 questions to be answered by ChIP-GPT, which can be classified into three broad categories (Table 1). In category I, questions are often answerable with a single word, or a few words, in unambiguous fashion. These include, e.g. the name of the cell line used for the experiment and the canonical human gene name corresponding to the ChIP target (when applicable). In category II, more variability is expected in answers that can be deemed correct. For example, the 'organ of origin' of the cells may have different, reasonable designations (e.g. 'breast' versus 'mammary gland') and might be ambiguous in some cases (e.g. immune cells collected from a particular organ, metastatic cells). In category III, questions are designed to improve the quality of answers to category I questions, by eliciting 'chain-of-thought' reasoning [24, 25].

Prompts varied considerably in length across records, depending for the most part on whether they were associated with a detailed study abstract or protocol. To stay within the 2048 token limit of the model, we implemented a summarization pre-processing step that was triggered as necessary. We found it best to ask the model to mark individual sentences to be retained, rather than generating new,summary text, and to follow a fine-tuning approach rather than a zero- or few-shot approach (Methods).

We note that the simple way in which we framed the prompt and the training is suitable for most samples of interest, but does not explicitly address more subtle cases where the technique used is a variation of the 'classical' ChIP approach (e.g. ChIPLoop), where multiple antigens are simultaneously targeted or an antigen does not map to a unique gene (e.g. ubiquitin tag), or where the DNA-binding properties of a chimeric protein are ascertained (e.g. some samples involve AML fusion proteins generated by chromosomal rearrangements). ChIP-GPT was instructed to output 'Unsure' any time the experiment could not be clearly interpreted and 'N/A' when a specific gene could not be named as corresponding to the ChIP target.

#### Accuracy

We scored answer correctness with a view to a downstream use of the answers for automated extraction of ChIP targets and cell lines. If an answer was not given with the expected formatting, it was scored as incorrect. If the answer could reasonably be argued as correct but did not convey the expected information, it was also scored as incorrect. Finally, not all ChIP targets have a single gene naturally associated with them. This is particularly so for histone modifications, whose answer to the 'gene' question was scored as correct as long as the ChIP target was correctly identified as a histone modification.

On a sample of 50 heldout SRA records, accuracy was 94% for identification of the ChIP target and the gene corresponding to the target and 90% for cell line identification (Table 3). To ask how accuracy depends on model size, we performed the same model training and performance assessment with the substantially smaller Llama models with 7 billion parameters (7B) or 13 billion parameters (13B). Accuracy differences between the 7B and 13B did not appear to be consistent across tasks, but overall accuracy was lower than for the 33B model (Figure 2).

### DISCUSSION

Our results illustrate the applicability of ChIP-GPT, which relies on a generalist LLM and relatively few training examples to specialized bioinformatics tasks requiring stereotypical outputs, with relatively few training examples. Relying on such models makes it possible to leverage the substantial effort that went into their development (in particular their pre-training). It also adds to our understanding of the capabilities of these models.

The ChIP-GPT answers we observed as part of this work make apparent areas in which it would be useful to further investigate model behavior and to adjust the fine-tuning and the questionanswering algorithm. First, for the ChIP-GPT outputs to be readily usable in downstream, automated tasks, it is important that generated answers follow the requested output format. While this is mostly the case, there are exceptions whereby, e.g. ChIP-GPT outputs information or considerations that were not requested or uses alternative formatting. This may be addressed by more training, a change in training parameters or the LoRA setup or other more general approaches such as defining a consensus between the outputs of independent models or using a second model to review the output of the first. Second, ChIP-GPT appears biased toward regular ChIP-seq samples, of which it saw more during training, and sometimes erroneously identifies sample types (e.g. DNase-seq samples) as ChIP-seq-ignoring explicit information about sample type and leading to incorrect inferences-or stumbles on controls. This could perhaps be addressed by changing the balance in training samples, in addition to more general approaches.

In terms of general correctness of ChIP-GPT inferences, another aspect potentially worth exploring is the structure of the interdependent questions asked of the model and the role of the 'chain of thought' they attempt to elicit. We have not yet quantified the extent to which these questions contribute to performance, distinguished their impacts at the fine-tuning and generation steps and identified potential drawbacks-for example, whether an error in one of the first answers gets locked in by repeating the answer as a preamble to subsequent questions.

Another avenue to investigate is the impact of pre-trained LLM choice. We investigated the effects of Llama model size on accuracy and found higher accuracy for the 65B model than for the 7B or 11B models. It is possible that the 65B model may have yet higher accuracy. In addition, other models may give equally good or superior results. We have not investigated the effects of Llama model size on accuracy. In addition, other models may give equally good or superior results. For example, Llama version 2 was released while the present work was underway and would be an obvious upgrade to test (if only because of its increased context size). It would also be possible to leverage closed models such as ChatGPT that are accessible online through an application programming interface and that can be fine-tuned (although the fine-tuning process may not be as easily controlled as it is with open-source code and weights). The significantly larger context window length advertised, e.g. by ChatGPT, may, in theory, obviate the need for the summarization stage we implemented. We note, however, that we observed multiple instances where prompts that were long, but under the advertised maximal length, failed to be processed properly by ChatGPT (as tested through OpenAI's interactive web interface). The scheme proposed here may thus retain its usefulness even with commercial models.

We note that ChIP-GPT is not intended as a solution to answer general questions in the fields of medicine or biomedical sciences [26]. Rather, it is designed to answer a defined set of questions on a large set of records, with a stereotypical answer format that lends itself to automation of downstream tasks. But while we focused on the identification of ChIP-seq targets and cell lines, ChIP-GPT is readily extensible to other questions about SRA records, with a preliminary parameter-efficient fine-tuning step on training samples with those other questions and without code modifications. It could also conceivably be used to automatically identify potential errors in the records -especially since it appears robust against typos (which we did not formally quantify)or omissions in the records: for example, in the course of this work, we identified records with typos in English words, as noted above, but also less-readily-apparent typos in antibody catalog numbers.

ChIP-GPT is also more generally applicable to processing of different kinds of biomedical records altogether. For example, electronic health records contain a wealth of information that is critical both to patient care and to research uses. Automated analysis is, however, limited by lack of structure [27], inconsistencies in medical condition coding [28] or other problems that can severely affect both research and patient care [29, 30]. The kind of natural language processing proposed here may be particularly suitable to such records.

### Key points

- ChIP-GPT leverages fine-tuned large language model to extract information from a biomedical database.
- ChIP-GPT is robust against missing or suboptimally labeled information, and against variability in record structure and contents.
- ChIP-GPT distinguishes roles for different genes in an experiment (e.g. knockdown target versus immunoprecipitation target).
- Approach is generalizable to other questions and other kinds of records in biology and medicine.
