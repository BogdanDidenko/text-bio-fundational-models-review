##### Abstract

Background : The COVID-19 pandemic has caused over 776 million infections and 7 million deaths globally between December 2019 and November 2024. Since the emergence of the original Wuhan strain, SARS-CoV-2 has evolved into multiple variants-including Alpha, Delta, and Omicron-primarily through mutations in the Spike glycoprotein. The S1 subunit, which binds the human angiotensinconverting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptor, mutates frequently and plays a key role in infectivity and immune escape, while the more conserved S2 subunit mediates membrane fusion. Anticipating future mutations is essential for guiding vaccine design and therapeutic strategies. Generative Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown promise in protein sequence modeling due to their capacity to produce realistic and functional synthetic sequences. Here, we introduce SARITA, a GPT-3-based LLM with up to 1.2 billion parameters, finetuned via continual learning on the protein model RITA trained on 107 017 high-quality SARS-CoV-2 Spike sequences (up to March 1st 2021) to generate high-quality synthetic SARS-CoV-2 Spike S1 subunits. Results: SARITA is able to generate realistic, full-length synthetic S1 subunits starting from a 14-amino-acid prompt. When evaluated on unseen sequences collected between March 2021 and November 2023-including major Variants of Concern (VOCs) such as Delta and Omicron, and Variants of Interest such as Iota-SARITA outperforms baseline and state-of-the-art LLMs in terms of sequence quality, biological plausibility, and similarity to real-world viral evolution. SARITA generates high-quality sequences in over 97% of cases, with markedly lower False Mutation Rate and higher similarity scores (PAM30, Levenshtein distance) compared to alternative approaches. It also accurately reproduces key mutations characteristic of future variants-such as L212I, R158L, T95P, and E406K-which were not present in the training data but emerged later in VOCs like Omicron and Delta. Structure-based analysis confirms the functional plausibility of these substitutions, with /Delta1/Delta1 G values within experimentally supported thresholds for ACE2 and antibody binding. Furthermore, SARITA anticipates immune-evasive mutations and accurately captures the positional and statistical distribution of mutations found in post- March 1st 2021 variants, highlighting its potential as a predictive tool for viral evolution. Conclusion: These results indicate the potential of SARITA to predict future SARS-CoV2 S1 evolution, potentially aiding in the development of adaptable vaccines and treatments.

Keywords: foundation model; COVID-19; mutations; vaccine; public health; SARS-CoV -2; LLM; protein design

### Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic, caused by the SARS-CoV-2, has significantly affected global health, economies, and daily human life, resulting in over 776 million estimated cases and 7 million deaths between December 2019 and November 2024 [1]. Since the emergence of the original Wuhan strain, SARS-CoV-2 has evolved into variants such as Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Delta, and Omicron, all distinguished by mutations in the Spike glycoprotein (Fig. 1a). The Spike protein is vital for the virus entry into human cells via its S1 and S2 subunits (Fig. 1b) [3]. In the SARS-CoV-2 ever-evolving genomic landscape, accurately predicting viral evolution is essential for implementing effective public health measures and developing new vaccines and therapies. Various methods have been devised to forecast whether a current SARS-CoV-2 sequence might become a variant of concern [4, 5] or a dominant lineage in the future [6], or to predict the likelihood of a given viral protein va riant to induce immune escape from antibodies [7]. While these bioinformatics tools are critical to quickly respond to viral evolution, especially if used in the context of genomic surveillance, they can detect new, dangerous variants only post-sequencing, i.e. after the virus has already evolved them. In other words, these methods allow us only to react to viral evolution rather than proactively countering it. If we want to shift to a more proactive stance and anticipate the virus evolution, we must develop tools capable of predicting dangerous variants before they emerge, and preemptively design appropriate treatments and vaccines.

Methods based on Large Language Models (LLMs), may accurately capture the biological context of a protein sequence [8, 9], unlike simpler approaches like k -mer representations,which inherently overlook the positional information of (sub)sequences and amino acids, or substitution matrices such as PAM and BLOSUM [10], which rely on statistical properties only. Recent research has investigated the use of LLMs to predict and generate novel SARS-CoV-2 sequences. Dhodapkar et al. introduced SpikeGPT2, a model fine-tuned on Spike protein sequences, which successfully fore casted future RBD ( ∼ 100 amino acids) mutations and identified potentially high-risk variants in this subsection of the Spike protein [11]. The main limit of SpikeGPT2 is that, while trained on the whole Spike protein, it is tested only on the RBD specific subsection. Ramachandran et al. proposed PandoGen, a method combining synthetic data generation, conditional sequence generation, and reward-based learning to forecast future SARS-CoV-2 sequences [12]. However, while the PandoGen code is accessible, the model is not readily available, as the user should reproduce the whole fine process, requiring both V100 and A100 GPUs (4 GPUs in total) in order to obtain the model. Additionally, these models have a large number of parameters (738 million for SpikeGPT2; 192 million for PandoGen), which makes them complex and difficult to use in settings where computing power is limited [11, 12].

To fulfill this research gap we develop SARITA (or SARS-CoV-2 RITA), an LLM designed to generate new, synthetic, whole, highquality, and highly realistic SARS-CoV-2 S1 subunits. SARIT A builds upon the continual learning framework of RITA, a state-ofthe-art generative language model [13]. RITA is an autoregressive model for general protein sequence generation with up to 1.2 billion parameters. To capture the unique biological features of the Spike protein and obtain a specialized a pproach, we apply continual learning to pre-train RITA via 107 017 highquality SARS-CoV-2 S1 sequences from GISAID [14]. To match different needs in terms of computational capacities, SARITA comes in four different sizes: The smallest model has 85 million parameters, while the largest has 1.2 billion. SARITA generates new S1 sequences using as an input the 14 amino acid sequence (signal peptide) preceding it. We show how SARITA provides significantly better performances than concurrent models, including other LLMs, not only in generating whole, high-quality and realistic S1 sequences, but also precisely predicting the emergence of 2022-2023 Spike mutations when trained on data from 2019 to 2021. SARITA, unlike existing models, introduces several key innovations: (i) it is trained through continual learning on a protein-specific architecture, enabling it to adapt effectively to the evolutionary patterns of SARS-CoV-2; (ii) it is tested on the generation of entire 686-amino-acid S1 subunits from compact 14-mer prompts, rather than being limited to partial domains such as the RBD, allowing for context-aware and comprehensive sequence synthesis; (iii) it is released in four scalable configurations (85 M-1.2B parameters), ensuring broad accessibility for research groups operating under diverse computational constraints.

### Methods

#### Architecture

SARITA is built upon a decoder-only transformer architecture (Fig. 2) inspired by GPT -3 [15], a design choice driven by the sequential nature of protein data and the need for efficient autoregressive generation. Unlike encoder-decoder models, decoder-only transformers predict each token based solely on the preceding ones, making them particularly suitable for generating full-length sequences from short prompts, such as N-terminal motifs. This autoregressive setup mirrors the natural process of amino acid chain elongation and allows the model to capture biologically plausible dependencies along the sequence. Similar architectures have proven effective in related works-ProGen2 [16], and RIT A [13]-which demonstrated strong performance in generating structurally and functionally coherent protein sequences using decoder-only transformers. SARITA employs Rotary Positional Embeddings (RoPE) to enhance the model's ability to capture positional relationships within the input data

[17]. SARITA is available in four configurations: SARITA-S with 85 million parameters, featuring an embedding size of 768 and 12 transformer layers; SARITA-M with 300 million parameters, featuring an embedding dimension of 1024 and 24 layers; SARITAL with 680 million parameters featuring an embedding size of 1536 and 24 layers; and SARITA-XL, with 1.2 billion parameters, featuring an embedding size of 2048, and 24 layers. All SARITA models can generate sequences up to 1024 tokens long. SARITA uses the Unigram model for tokenization, where each amino acid is represented as a single token, r eflecting its unique role in protein structure and function. This approach was chosen, also in the RITA model, over methods like Byte Pair Encoding (BPE) because protein sequences lack clear word boundaries, and segmentation strategies based on frequency, such as BPE, can generate tokens that disrupt biologically relevant patterns [13]. The tokenizer also includes special tokens like &lt; PAD &gt; for padding shorter sequences and &lt; EOS &gt; for marking sequence ends, ensuring consistency across datasets. This pr ocess reduces variability and enhances the model's ability to learn meaningful patterns from protein sequences [13]. At the end each token is transformed into a numerical r epresentation using a lookup table (Table S1). After tokenization, each amino acid and special token is mapped to a high-dimensional embedding vector via a learned embedding matrix. After tokenization, each amino acid and special token (e.g. &lt; PAD &gt; , &lt; EOS &gt; ) is associated with a unique index in the vocabulary. These indices are then transformed into high-dimensional vectors using a learned embeddingmatrix,whereeachrowcorrespondstothecontinuous representation of a specific token. This embedding matrix is initialized with the values of the RITA model and updated during training through Backpropagation, allowing the model to learn meaningful representations of amino acids in context. These token embeddings are passed as input to the Transformer decoder layers, where self-attention mechanisms learn dependencies across the full sequence-both local patterns (e.g. motifs) and long-range relationships important for protein structure and function. Thanks to continual training on over 100 000 curated Spike protein sequences from GISAID, S ARITA's embeddings evolve to capture biologically relevant signals. In particular, they encode contextual and evolutionary information-such as which amino acids are likely to appear together, in which positions, and under what evolutionary constraints-enabling the model to reproduce known mutational patterns and to generate plausible future variants. This is evidenced by SARITA's ability to match real-world mutational trends, including those that emerged after its training cutoff.

#### Data set

We downloaded 16 187 950 Spike protein sequences from the GISAID database. The sequences come fr om samples collected from December 19th, 2019 to November 30th, 2023.

#### Training set

We select proteins that (i) are derived from genomes marked as high coverage in GISAID i.e. genome with &lt; 1% Ns and &lt; 0.05% unique amino acid mutations (not seen in other sequences in database) and no insertion/deletion unless verified by submitter; (ii) have a length o f 1273 ± 30 amino acids, thus representing a complete Spike sequence; (iii) are collected from a human host; (iv) have a lineage assigned, determined by the Pango algorithm [18]; (v) do not contain letters representing non-unique or nonstandard amino acids (i.e. 'X', 'B', 'Z','J', 'U', 'O'); and (vi) have

After filtering, 4 952 389 high-quality Spike sequences remain. We select sequences collected between December 19th, 2019, and February 28st, 2021 as the training set, resulting in a total of 793 732 available sequences. The time window for building the training set was chosen with two goals in mind: (i) to secure a sufficiently large and well-balanced collection of sequences for optimal model training [19]; and (ii) to include sequences belonging to Variants of Concern-such as Delta and Omicronand Variants of Interest. In this way, we can rigorously assess the model's ability to anticipate future sequences that carry new mutations. As the distribution of the unique sequences is far from uniform, with some unique sequences having a very high number of copies, we implement a subsampling strategy to mitigate this bias. Our rationale is that overrepresented sequences can lead SARITA to skew the generated synthetic sequences toward the overrepresented ones, limiting its ability to produce novel and previously unseen sequences [13]. To balance the dataset we use the mean number of copies of unique sequences (n = 25) as a an upper threshold for the number of copies of the very same sequence, i.e. any unique sequence present with a number of copies (instances) above the initial mean value is subsampled to 25 copies. The Gini index for the unique sequence copy number decreases from 0.93 to 0.07 after resampling [20]. A total of 107 017 sequences (32 376 unique) are eventually available for training (of these, 10% for training validation).

#### Test set

The unique GISAID sequences with a collection date spanning from March 2021 to November 2023 (145 059 in total) are used as a test set. Importantly, by design only the Alpha, Beta and Gamma variants are included in the training set, while other known variants, such as, Delta, Omicron and Kappa, are only present in the test set. We can ther efore validate the ability of SARITA in predicting Variants of Concern (VOCs) like Delta and Omicron, as well as their sub-lineages or Variants of Interest (VOIs), including Kappa, Iota, and Zeta that emerged after March 1st, 2021 (Fig. S1). This test set is used to measure the performance of SARITA (see Evaluation str ategy, below).

#### Continual learning

We apply continual learning [11] to expand the general-domain pretrained model (RITA) using external, domain-relevant SARSCoV-2 spike sequences, enabling the model to incrementally acquire new knowledge. Training is conducted over 10 epochs with a training and evaluation batch size of 48. Checkpoints are save d at the end of each epoch, and validation is performed after every epoch. The optimization is performed using the Adam optimizer with β 1 =0.9, β 2 =0.999, and ε =1e -8. We use a linear learning rate scheduler with an initial learning rate of 5e -5. All experiments are run with a fixed random seed (42) to ensure reproducibility and use mixed-precision training (fp16) via PyTorch's native Automatic Mixed Precision (AMP) on CUDA-enabled GPUs to improve efficiency. Through domainadaptive continual learning, SARITA progressively acquires the specialized lexicon of SARS-CoV-2 spike evolution, shifting its latent representations from generic proteins to viral spike proteins. In this work supervised fine-tuning has not been used because it is applied only once explicit labels are availablee.g. to discriminate fit versus non-fit mutations-and is therefore unsuited to the open-ended sequence-generation task addressed

#### How SARITA can be used to predict the future evolution of SARS-CoV-2

SARITA is a generative autoregressive language model based on the GPT-3 architecture, designed to model the amino acid distribution of the SARS-CoV-2 S1 subunits. Tokenization is performed at the single-amino-acid level using a Unigram tokenizer, in which each residue is treated as a token. This strategy avoids the potential ambiguity introduced b y learned subword tokenization schemes such as BPE, which may be suboptimal for sequences lacking natural segmentation boundaries, such as proteins [13]. During training, SARITA learns the conditional probability distribution over the next amino acid giv en its context.

The model incorporates RoPE [17], enabling it to represent relative positional information more effectively than f ixed encodings. Through self-attention [22], SARITA is able to identify regions of high sequence variability-typically associated with adaptive pressure in domains such as the RBD or NTD-and assigns higher entropy distributions to these positions. In contrast, conserved regions result in sharper, lower-entropy predictions. At inference time, the model generates new sequences by sampling from the learned distribution, thus naturally introducing mutations in high-variability sites while maintaining conserved motifs. This generative ca pacity allows SARITA to produce realistic, structurally consistent S1 sequences with realistic mutations, and in several cases, mutations that appeared in nature only after the model's training cutoff-suggesting it has internalized latent evolutionary constraints [16, 23].

#### Competing approac hes

We compare SARITA with several other approaches (Table 1). As baseline, we implement three simple random sequence generation methods: The first samples each amino acid position entirely at random ('Rand'); the second samples each position based on amino acid frequency in the training set ('Rand Fr'); and the third uses PAM30 (Point Accepted Mutation 30 matrix) weights for sampling ('Rand PAM30'). We also evaluate the performance of the following LLMs: SpikeGPT2, RITA-S, RITA-M, RITA-L, RITA-XL, and ProGen2 [13]. SpikeGPT2 is an autoregressive model with 738 million parameters derived from ProtGPT2 [24] initially trained on the UniRef50 dataset to learn general protein structures, finetuned on 20 000 SARS-CoV-2 Spike protein sequences, specifically focusing on sequences gathered prior to May 2021. The finetuning enables SpikeGPT2 to adapt to SARS-CoV-2 mutation patterns, with modifications applied to select layers only, preserving core protein knowledge while specializing in SARS-CoV-2 mutations. RITA is a group of four autoregressive generative models for protein sequences, scaling up to 1.2 billion parameters, trained on more than 280 million protein sequences from UniRef100 [25], MGnify [26], and Metaclust [27]. ProGen2 is a family of five autoregressive generative models for protein sequences, scaling up to 6.4 billion parameters, tr ained on over 1 billion sequences drawn from UniRef90 [25], BFD30 [28] [cit], and immune repertoire databases such as OAS [16]. For our comparative analysis, we selected ProGen2-Base, a representati ve model with 764 million parameters.

#### Evaluation stra tegy

The evaluation strategy involves assessing the performance of various models in generating S1 Spike pr oteins. To generate the sequences to be tested (Figs. 2 and 3), we used all the 113 unique prompts of signal peptide (14 amino acids long) found in the training set preceding the S1 Subunit. For each prompt, we use the SARITA, SpikeGPT2, RITA, and random models to generate 100 sequences, each with a length of 686 amino acids, including the prompt, thus matching the S1 length, spanning from position 15 to 686 [1, 3].

To evaluate the performance of SARITA and its competing approaches, we assess three main aspects: (i) sequence quality, i.e. are the generated sequences composed of valid amino acids? (ii) similarity, i.e. are the generated sequences similar to the ones in the test set? And (iii) single-point mutation prediction, i.e. are the generated mutations biologically plausible?

#### Quality of generated proteins

Thequality of the generated sequences is checked by the presence of invalid amino acids, represented by characters not corresponding to the twenty canonical amino acids (i.e. 'X', 'B', 'Z','J', 'U', 'O'). We further examine the quality of the synthetic sequences by measuring their similarity to the original Wuhan strain. Our rationale is that higher dissimilarity implies less realism. To measure similarity, we use alignment (Needleman-Wunsch, Biopython package version 1.80, default parameters) via the PAM30 and BLOSUM62 scoring matrix. PAM30 and BLOSUM62 assesses evolutionary similarity by assigning scores to amino acid substitutions based on their likelihood over short evolutionary distances and is optimized for closely related protein sequences. Higher PAM30 or BLOSUM62 scores indicate stronger similarity to the Wuhan strain, suggesting conservation of structural or functional properties, while lower scores imply greater divergence, potentially highlighting adaptive mutations or functional shifts.

#### Sequence similarity with the test set

To evaluate whether the proposed approaches are capable of modeling viral evolution-specifically, their ability to generate plausible future variants-we assess the similarity between the generated sequences and those in the test set. The test set includes sequences submitted to GISAID between March 1st, 2021 and November 8th, 2023, corresponding to Variants of Concern (VOCs) such as Delta and Omicron, as well as their sub-lineages and several Variants of Interest (VOIs), including Kappa, Iota, and Zeta.

Sequence similarity is quantified using the Le venshtein distance (LD) [29], a string metric that computes the minimal number of single-character edits (insertions, deletions, or substitutions) required to transform one sequence into another. For each generated sequence, LD is computed against all sequences in the test set. Both the minimum and median LD values are recorded to ca pture best-match similarity and overall distribution. In the case of SpikeGPT2, LD is computed using the full length of the generated spike protein sequence.

We further assess viral evolution prediction via PAM30 and BLOSUM62 scoring as described above: We compare each generated sequence with the test set, and retain the best score. Similarity if further investigated by calculating the False Mutation Rate (FMR), i.e. the presence of mutations in the synthetic sequences that are not matched by the best alignment in the test set. #### Single-point mutation prediction

To evaluate single-point mutation prediction, we examine whether the mutations of the synthetic sequences show a positional correspondence with the ones of the training (pre March 1st 2021) and the test sets (post March 1st 2021). To assess whether the mutations that appear in generated S1 subunit sequences by SARITA could plausibly appear in nature, we performed a two-fold evaluation combining both statistical and structural analyses. First, we estimated the potential biological relevance of these mutations by evaluating their effect on the binding free energy ( /Delta1/Delta1 G) between the RBD spike protein and two key molecular targets [11]. For this analysis we concentrate on the RBD of the SARS-CoV-2 spike because this ∼ 200-residue module is the portion that physically engages the human ACE2 receptor and, at the same time, the main target of neutralising antibodies and small-molecule/peptide entry inhibitors [30]. We used mmCSMPPI [31], a computational tool that estimates changes in binding free energy upon amino acid substitution, based on predefined protein structures. To assess the interaction with the human ACE2 receptor, we used the cryo-EM structure of the SARS-CoV-2 spikeACE2 complex (PDB: 7DF4) [32]. To evaluate immune evasion potential, we employed the structure of the spike RBD in complex with the monoclonal antibody cocktail tixagevimab/cilgavimab (PDB: 8D8Q) [33]. Mutations predicted to increase binding affinity with ACE2 and simultaneously decrease binding to the antibody complex were fla gged as high-risk substitutions. These variants were visualized using PyMOL [34].

Second, we computed the difference in mutation density between synthetic and real sequences. For each amino acid position in the S1 subunit, we quantified how frequently SARITA introduced mutations and estimated positional mutation densities using Gaussian kernel density estimation (KDE), a nonparametric method that generates a continuous probability distribution from discrete mutation data [7]. A smoothing adjustment parameter of 0.1 was applied to balance resolution and noise, allowing local mutational trends to emerge without overfitting. To correct for variation in mutation frequency, a base-10 logarithmic transformation was applied, reducing the influence of highfrequency outliers while enhancing interpretability. In addition we computed Pearson's correlation coefficients to quantify the degree of similarity between mutation fr equency distributions in SARITA-generated sequences and those in the training and test sets. Together, these analyses provide a comprehensive understanding of SARITA ability to generate realistic, biologically relevant mutations.

### Results

SARITA has been trained on 107 017 SARS-Cov-2 complete, highquality Spike protein sequences uploaded to the GISAID database [14]. SARITA takes as an input the first 14 amino acids of the Spike S1 subunit, and generates a synthetic S1 subunit sequence (Fig. 3). To estimate its performances, we split GISAID data into a training set (December 19th 2019 to February 28th 2021) and test set (March 1st 2021 to November 8th 2023). Information about training times and batches are reported in the supplementary in Table S2.

All the 113 unique 14-amino-acid prompts present in the training set are used to generate 11 300 new sequences (100 sequences for each 113 signal peptide found in the training set), which were then compared to the real-world test set. We evaluate the generated synthetic sequences across three main objectives: (i) Quality; (ii) sequence similarity with the test set; and (iii) single-point mutation prediction. Quality is assessed by checking for the presence of undefined or non-standard amino acids and biological alignment against the original Wuhan S1. Sequence similarity with the test is evaluated with LD, PAM30 scores, and FMR. Single-point mutation prediction is evaluated by examining the ability to replicate position-dependent mutational patterns.

#### Quality: SARITA generates high-quality, synthetic SARS-Co v-2 spike S1 sequences

For each of the 113 possible prompts of the training set we generate 100 synthetic sequences for a total of 11 300 sequences for each SARITA model (S, M, L, XL). We compared the results against an equivalent number of S1 sequences generated by competing approaches: two other LLMs (SpikeGPT2 and RITA); and three baseline approaches ('Rand', 'Rand Fr', 'Rand PAM30').

If a generated sequence shows one or more uncharacterized amino acids, it is not considered high-quality [1, 4]. Another measure of quality is related to the sequence length: We consider a sequence with a length of 686 ± 10 to be high-quality. SARITA generates high-quality sequences at least 97% of the times (Table 1, Fig. S3). RITA models provide a better performance, with 100% high-quality generated sequences. Instead, only 3% of SpikeGPT2generated sequences (339 out of 11 300) are high-quality. Additionally, none of the sequences generated by SpikeGPT2 actually match the expected S1 length, despite the fact that it was instructed to match the length of the S1 subunit. Specifically, the median length of SpikeGPT2-generated sequences is 2003 amino acids, ∼ 192% longer than the expected S1 subunit length (Fig. S3).

SARITA outperforms competing methods in generating sequences closely related to the original Wuhan strain, therefore indicating higher biological plausibility and realism, as shown by the median PAM30 score (Fig. 4). Specifically, SARITA produces synthetic sequences with median PAM30 scores ranging from 5191 (SARITA S) to 5278 (SARITA M), indicating high similarity to the original sequence. In contrast, all competing methods generate sequences with significantly lower PAM30 scores, indicating a lower-quality alignment with biological S1 sequences. Their median scores range from 661 (Rand PAM30) to 2062 (SpikeGPT2).

#### Sequence similarity with the test set: SARITA outperforms competing approac hes in matching viral evolution

We evaluated the ability of the models to generate plausible future S1 subunit sequences by measuring their similarity to sequences in the test set, which includes major Variants of Concern (VOCs) such as Delta and Omicron, as well as Variants of Interest (VOIs) including Kappa, Iota, and Zeta. Low Levenshtein distances (LDs) between generated and test sequences indicate a strong resemblance, suggesting that the models are able to anticipate ev olutionary trends. Notably, SARITA was able to generate synthetic S1 subunits differing by only 1-2 amino acids from sequences associated with Delta and Omicron sub lineages present in the test set, effectively anticipating the emergence of these variants. As reported in Table 2, SARITA consistently outperforms all baselines. RITA variants and random models never achieve a minimum LD below 500. Only 1.8% of SpikeGPT2generated sequences reach a best LD under 500. In stark contrast, SARITA achieves a best LD below 10 for the vast majority of its outputs: 93% (SARITA S), 98% (SARITA M), 98% (SARITA X), and 99% (SARITA XL). Additional LD distributions are provided in Fig. S4. Consistent with these results, SARITA models also obtain the highest median PAM30 scores, ranging from 5210 (SARITA S) to 5302 (SARITA M), further supporting their close similarity to the test set sequences. Competing models perform markedly worse, with median scores ranging from 670 (Rand PAM30) to 2102 (SpikeGPT2). An equivalent anal ysis via BLOSUM62 confirmed the results that SARITA obtain the highest median BLOSUM62 scores, ranging from 3641 (SARITA S) to 3690 (SARITA L) outperforming the competitive approaches (Fig. S5).

We also compute the FMR, or the frequency at which SARITA introduces mutations in synthetic sequences that are not cor -responding with the best alignment with test set sequences. As shown in Fig. 5, SARITA significantly outperforms all baseline methods in generating realistic synthetic sequences. The lowest median FMR is achieved by SARITA L (0.5), whereas the best-performing baseline reaches a considerably higher value of 0.98. In addition to lower FMR, SARITA models introduce far fewer mutations per sequence. While baseline models, such as SpikeGPT2, produce sequences with an extremely high number of mutations-reaching a median of 438-SARITA maintains a controlled and biologically plausible mutation load. Median mutation counts range from 38 in SARITA S to only 14 in SARITA L. Notably, SARIT A S does not generate sequences with very low FMR ( &lt; 10%). However, SARITA M, L, and XL are capable of producing synthetic sequences with extremely low FMRs (as low as 1-0.5%), closely matching the reference VOC and VOI sequences present in the test set, including Omicron, Delta, Kappa, Iota, and Zeta (Fig. 5).

### Discussion and limita tions

The results confirm SARITA as a highly specialized LLM for generating biologically meaningful synthetic SARS-CoV-2 S1 sequences. SARS-CoV-2 has continued to mutate and evolve since the pandemic onset, challenging public health responses and emphasizing the importance of predictive models that can anticipate viral changes. By generating synthetic sequences that align closely with future real-world mutational patterns, SARITA addresses a critical need for proactive measures in viral genomics, providing

Unlike other generative models, SARITA maintains a low FMR and does not introduce an excessive number of mutations within a single synthetic S1. This reasonable mutational load, similar to the one of real-world data, is crucial for creating synthetic sequences that are biologically plausible. By maintaining this balance, SARITA supports studies aimed at understanding the evolutionary trajectory of SARS-CoV-2 without sacrificing biological integrity, making it a more reliable model for predicting variant emergence.

In terms of model comparison, SARITA outperforms (Fig. 10) alternative generative models, including SpikeGPT2 and RITA, by generating sequences with lower LD and higher PAM30 scores relative to real-world viral sequences. One of the key innovations is its generation of novel mutations that are not present in either the training or test sets, yet are highly realistic, reflecting the ability to explore mutational landscapes beyond known variants. The fact that larger SARITA models, such as SARITA-L and SARITAXL, generate a higher proportion of novel mutations compared to smaller models supports the idea that scaling model parameters enhances generative diversity. This feature is promising for applications where anticipating novel mutations is necessary, such as in the early identification of potentially dangerous variants that could arise due to selective pressures or recombination events in viral genomes.

Figure 10 shows that the worst method for generating synthetic S1 subunits is Rand\_PAM30. Rand\_PAM30 might underperform the other random models due to overfitting specific amino acid substitutions favored by PAM30, which are too general and do not very well fit the SARS-CoV-2 genome. PAM30 introduces biases toward particular substitutions , potentially limiting its general applicability. In contrast, the basic Rand model, being fully random, avoids these biases and achieves relatively better results.

Despite its strengths, SARITA presents certain limitations. Its predictive power is inherently dependent on the quality, diversity, and completeness of the training data-entirely sourced from GISAID [46]. Any sampling biases or sequencing errors in this database could propagate through the model outputs, generating low-quality sequences. Additionally, SARITA is specifically finetuned for the SARS-CoV-2 S1 subunit and it is not designed to generalize on other viral proteins or pathogens without substantial re-training and architectural adaptation. The mutation patterns and selection pressures governing other viral systems (e.g. influenza, RSV, or non-Spike SARS-CoV-2 proteins) may differ significantly, limiting SARITA applicability without further customization. Moreover, SARITA currently does not incorporate immuneselection pressures, such as B and T cell epitope mapping or antibody escape profiles. This represents an important limitation, as immune-driven dynamics are central to the emergence and dominance of real-world variants. Integrating immunological data-such as escape maps from deep mutational scanning or known epitope datasets-would substantially enhance the biological relevance and predictive utility of SARITA, particularly for applications in vaccine design and immunotherapy development. We consider this a key direction for future research.

Future research could expand on SARITA by exploring its adaptability to other coronaviruses or respiratory pathogens. Additionally, integrating SARITA outputs into a broader predictive framework that incorporates epidemiological data, host-pathogen interaction studies, and environmental factors could offer a more holistic understanding of viral evolution, ultimately supporting more comprehensive and adaptive public health strategies. Furthermore, extending SARITA with structural information-when available-or coupling it with tools like Alphafold3 that, unlike mmCSM-PPI which is focused on specific mutations, are highly descriptive of the peptidic tertiary structure as a whole, will allow to better assess their potential functional and immunological impact. Such advancements would strengthen SARITA utility in applications like vaccine design, variant surveillance, and pandemic preparedness.

We present SARITA, a generative LLM tailored on SARS-CoV2 Spike S1. SARITA outperforms other models in generating high-quality, realistic sequences, and shows the capability of correctly anticipating viral evolution. ### Key P oints

- SARITA Model for SARS-CoV-2 Prediction: SARITA is a large language model designed to generate synthetic SARS-CoV-2 Spike S1 subunit sequences. It builds on the GPT -3 architecture and employs continual learning to predict future viral mutations.
- Superior Performance in Predicting Mutations: SARITA outperforms existing models like SpikeGPT2 and RITA in generating high-quality, biologically realistic sequences. It accurately predicts key m utations of Variants of Concern and Variants of Interest that emerged after its training period.
- High-Quality and Realistic Synthetic Sequences: SARITA produces sequences with a low false mutation rate and maintains a mutation load comparable to real-world sequences, ensuring biological plausibility and avoiding excessive, unrealistic variations.
- Model Scalability and Adaptability: SARITA comes in four sizes (85 million to 1.2 billion parameters), making it adaptable to different computational resources. Lar ger models exhibit better performance in generating novel, e volutionarily plausible mutations.
- Potential Applications for Vaccine and Therapeutic Development: By anticipating future viral mutations, SARITA can support genomic surveillance and proactive public health strategies, potentially aiding the design of adaptable vaccines and treatments for emerging SARSCoV-2 variants.
